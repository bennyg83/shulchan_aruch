**** ENGLISH ****
<b>That its impression remains.</b> See Ba'er Heitev. See also Ginat Veradim, who writes that it appears to him that the Tur also agrees to this, namely that we require the writing to stand and endure. When the Tur writes that “its impression is recognizable,” he means that both requirements are necessary: it must endure and it must be recognizable. However, if it endures but is not recognizable, such as gallnut water on gallnut-treated parchment, as written below in se'if 3, or if it is recognizable but does not endure, such as fruit juice and the like, it is not valid. This is also written by the Pri Chadash, cited in Yeshuot Yaakov, se'if katan 1.

He further writes that there is a type of thick, shiny ink called “papil bruneido,” known in our language as “glanz tint.” Its nature is that after the writing dries nicely, after a day or two, because of the thickness of the ink, the letters fall off and spring away from the paper. It appears that this is considered something that does not endure, and one must be careful not to write a get with this ink. See there.

See also Get Mekushar, in Kuntrus Acharon, letter 6, who disagrees with him. He writes that it seems to him that this ink also does not spring off unless caused by something external, such as moving the paper and the like. Since it does not pass away on its own, it is properly called enduring. This is especially so because even after it springs off, an impression remains, and for a get an impression is sufficient. See there. The Torat Gittin here also disagrees with the Ginat Veradim on this matter. See there.

<b>So as not to come to engraving.</b> See the Levush below, se'if 22, who writes: I am astonished by this reason, for we explicitly conclude in the second chapter of Gittin, as I wrote above in se'if 4, that engraving can be considered writing. Therefore, it does not appear to me that the reason for those who are careful not to write with these quills is the reason they gave. Rather, it appears that the reason is because the verse says “and he shall write,” which implies one act of writing, and not writing on top of writing. Here, if he writes with one of these quills, the quill engraves and digs the writing into the parchment, which by itself is valid, as we wrote above in se'if 4. Afterwards, the ink in the quill comes and fills the engraved writing made by the quill, and this becomes writing on top of parchment in a way that we hold is invalid, as above in se'if 10 regarding the care not to rule lines with lead. End quote.

See Ginat Veradim, end of se'if katan 15, who explains the words of the Rama, “so as not to come to engraving,” to mean that perhaps the paper will be thin and the point of the quill will engrave the paper and puncture the letter with a hole going through it. He then cites the above words of the Levush and writes about them: I do not know from where he derived that writing on top of writing is invalid. According to his words, if one letter was written and there was not enough ink in it, and he passed the quill over it a second time, it would become invalid because of writing on top of writing. We have neither heard nor seen such a thing. On the contrary, we see what Maran writes below in siman 135, se'if 5. Even according to the one who says there that it is not a get, that is because it was originally written not for her sake, and the second writing does not help at all. However, if both writings were for her sake, everyone agrees that it is valid, and there is no invalidation because “the Torah said one writing and not writing on top of writing.” We have not heard such an exposition. Regarding the proof that the Levush brought from se'if 10, see Ginat Veradim below, se'if katan 45. Moreover, Terumat HaDeshen, siman 48, cited in Orach Chaim, siman 32, se'if 27, writes regarding letters and words that were partly erased, and the Levush himself copied this in Orach Chaim there. These are his words. See there.

See Yeshuot Yaakov, se'if katan 2, who was asked about this matter, namely the practice of scribes to pass the quill over the get a second time before the signatures. The rabbi who asked wrote that this is improper, since the lower writing is not visible, and the upper writing is considered writing on top of writing according to the Levush. He answered him that there is no concern in this at all. He also mentioned the words of Mahari in Terumat HaDeshen, cited in Orach Chaim, siman 32, se'if 27, which oppose the words of the Levush. He writes that it appears proper to rule that the essential law follows Mahari, for we say that writing on top of writing does not help only where the first writing was written invalidly. He discusses this at length. See there.

See Torat Gittin, se'if katan 3, who explains the words of the Rama differently. His explanation is somewhat similar to the reason of the Levush, but in another form: there is concern that he may engrave with the iron because there is no ink in the quill, and this too is considered engraved writing. Afterwards, he passes ink over the engraved place, and it is then like ink over red pigment, regarding which we say in Gittin 19 that one is liable twice, once for erasing and once for writing. Since he erased the first writing and wrote a second writing, we say that the witnesses have already fulfilled their agency. Certainly, if the scribe first wrote with red pigment and afterwards passed a quill with ink over it, the get is void, since the witnesses already fulfilled their agency with the first writing. So too here. These are his words.

In my humble opinion, although he has said a wise idea, there remains a difficulty. The Rama should not have needed to warn about this here, for it is explained above, siman 122, se'if 1, that the husband gives permission to write from one get up to one hundred, until one is valid, with no concern. Even though the custom is to be stringent in every matter, nevertheless, since this is not the strict law, it is not appropriate to decree one safeguard upon another. Furthermore, it appears that once the custom is that the husband gives the parchment and ink to the scribe and says to him, “Here is the parchment and ink, write a get,” as explained in Seder HaGet, se'if 15, we see that the husband is particular that it be written specifically with ink. It is therefore not relevant to say that through engraving he fulfilled his agency. Although engraving is also considered writing, that is only where the husband did not instruct him to write with ink. But according to the custom, where he gives him the ink, he certainly has no permission to change. Even if he wrote with red pigment, it would also be nothing, and if so it is not relevant to say that he already fulfilled his agency. This requires further study.

See Taz, se'if katan 3, who writes that it appears that if one needs to write with an iron quill, there is no need to be stringent at all.

<b>When the entire letter was made in this manner.</b> See Ba'er Heitev, who writes that since he is not performing an action, but where he does perform an action, and so on. See Taz, se'if katan 4, who writes that according to this, in the case below in se'if 9 of an open mem that became closed, if he closed the mem before completing it, he may scrape away the closure, since he still needs to add the slanted strip shaped like a yud. Therefore, he should first scrape away the attachment and then complete the letter. End quote.

What Ba'er Heitev writes, “and if a little ink fell,” is the language of Beit Shmuel, se'if katan 5. It is obvious that this applies specifically in such a case, where the ink fell first and he then completed the letter through writing, in which case it is valid because the letter was completed validly. But if the drop itself completed the letter, it is certainly invalid, for everything follows the completion. This is also explicitly clear from the words of the Beit Yosef. See there, and see what I wrote on this in my commentary to Seder Gittin, in Seder Get Rishon, letter 87.

What Ba'er Heitev writes, “The Beit Shmuel wrote that if an invalid person wrote the letter,” and the Pri Chadash writes that it should simply be invalidated, from this wording it sounds as though the words of the Pri Chadash refer only to the ruling of the Beit Shmuel, namely where an invalid person wrote the letter and a valid person completed it. In truth, however, this is not so, as I saw in Beit Meir, who cites him. The Pri Chadash also disagrees with the main ruling of the Beit Yosef in the case where a little ink fell, and with the ruling of Terumat HaDeshen and the Rama here, where part of the letter was made through chok tochot and still needs correction. See there.

See Magen Avraham, Orach Chaim, siman 32, se'if katan 23, where it is also clear that this law is not agreed upon. There he cites the Radach, that even if one completely erased the drop and completed the letter by writing, it is invalid, because the entire letter must be made through writing. He then cites Rabbi Moshe Alashkar, siman 5, that if a drop of ink fell and formed something like half a letter, we do not find anyone who permits completing the letter through writing. However, from the wording of the Magen Avraham there it appears that he agrees with the opinion of the Beit Yosef and Rama here. See there.

The Ginat Veradim, end of se'if katan 30, also writes: As to the halacha, since the Kol Bo, Rivash, and Maran agree that one may add to the letter, and similarly may add to a drop of ink to make it into a yud or a vav, this is how we rule. End quote. However, this requires analysis, because from the language of the Ginat Veradim here it sounds that one may do so even ab initio, whereas from his words in se'if katan 39 it does not sound so. There he greatly elaborates on the above words of the Radach, and he mentions there that the root of the Radach’s words concerns the case of an open mem that became closed, whether one must erase all of it or whether it is enough to erase part of it until its form is ruined and then complete it. See Beit Yosef, Orach Chaim, siman 32, on this. At the end of his words he writes: As to the halacha, we have seen one side against the other, and Maran the Beit Yosef’s view is decisive, that where a mem became closed, scraping away the protrusion is certainly enough, and this is the proper ruling after the fact. However, ab initio one should be concerned for the view of the Radach and Rabbi Moshe Alashkar, who hold that one must scrape away the whole thing. End quote.

According to this, the same, and all the more so, applies in the case of a drop of ink that fell and one wishes to complete the letter by writing: this is not proper ab initio. If so, his words contradict each other. One must say that what he wrote at the end of se'if katan 38, that ab initio it is proper to be concerned, was only as a matter of extra care. But as to the essential law, throughout that se'if katan his view appears to agree with Mahari ben Chaviv, his son the Ralbach, and Maran the Beit Yosef, that even ab initio it is sufficient to scrape away only the protrusion, as all the later authorities ruled in Orach Chaim, siman 32, se'if 18.

I later saw that Taz, se'if katan 10, disagrees with the Beit Yosef regarding the case of a drop of ink falling. He writes that it does not appear proper to rely on this leniently in practice, because the entire body of the letter must certainly be formed through writing. What does the small addition made by writing help? This is only where the drop that fell is still moist, and one moves it and pulls it here and there with a dry quill, or even with a knife, and from it makes an entire letter, such as a vav or a yud. In such a case there is no concern, and it is valid, for it is as if he wrote the whole thing anew with ink. This is the explanation of the words of the Kol Bo cited by the Beit Yosef. See there.

From these words it appears at first glance that he also disagrees with the ruling of Terumat HaDeshen and the Rama here, but from his words in se'if katan 4 it is clear that he does not disagree about that. The Beit Meir below, se'if 8, also distinguishes between these cases. In his view, where a drop of ink fell, which is considered actual chok tochot, the main ruling follows the Pri Chadash, who disagrees with the Beit Yosef and holds that the small added part is considered as if it does not exist. But in the case of Terumat HaDeshen, and the Rama here, where one needed to write a bent kaf and wrote a bent nun, and a strip extended from end to end, and after scraping one still needs to draw and make it into a kaf, in that case it appears to him that since everything was made through writing, albeit invalidly, and at least not automatically, it is only rabbinic chok tochot. Certainly one can say that wherever the scraping alone does not complete the form of the letter, and one must add until the form is made properly, everyone agrees that it no longer appears to be chok tochot, even rabbinically. See there.

See further Ginat Veradim, se'if katan 34, who writes: However, I am uncertain according to the above-mentioned opinion of Rabbi Moshe Alashkar, where a drop of ink fell and one extended it and made it into a yud or a nun, and in the part that he extended there is enough measure for the letter. What is the law? Perhaps half a letter made through chok tochot is invalid only where, if the legs were removed, the necessary measure would be lacking, and the drop that fell already completes the letter. But where he extended the letter beyond that, the drop that fell is considered as if it does not exist, and it is permitted according to all. This seems reasonable. End quote. See also what I wrote about this in my commentary to Seder Maharam, in Seder Get Rishon, letter 87.

<b>Surrounded by parchment.</b> See Ba'er Heitev. See also the responsum of Maharam Lublin, siman 101, concerning a get that had already been given and was found to contain attached letters, namely a dalet attached to a heh in the word דהוית. See Ginat Veradim, se'if katan 21, who questions this, for the word דהוית is part of the standard formula. Even if this word were omitted entirely, it would not change the meaning of the divorce and we would not be concerned after the fact once the get was given, as written in siman 126, se'if 49, and there in se'if 35 in the gloss. See the responsum of Noda BiYehuda, siman 85, concerning a get that was brought from a distant place by an agent. Because it had fallen into water, several letters became blurred, and the impression of the ink spread outside the letters, between the letters and in the spaces between lines, so that even outside the letters the parchment was black. Nevertheless, the letters themselves were recognizable, because the body of each letter was very black, while what was outside the letter was much lighter. The question was whether such a get should be invalidated because the letters were damaged and because they were not surrounded by parchment.

He writes that certainly writing on top of writing does not help, even if the second writing is blacker. However, he was uncertain in a case where ink fell on blank parchment and the parchment remained black, and afterwards one wrote in that place with ink that blackens more strongly, so that the letters are clearly recognizable, whether this is called writing or not. Rashi explains, under the words “where it was treated with gallnuts,” that the appearance of the parchments is like the appearance of the writing, and the writing is not recognizable. The Shulchan Aruch above, se'if 3, also uses this language. This implies that if it were recognizable, it would properly be considered writing. But Rabbenu Tam there in Tosafot, under the words “ink,” writes that ink is not made from gallnuts, for how could we write a Torah scroll on parchment that was treated with gallnuts? See there. Now certainly ink made from gallnut water with vitriol is recognizable on gallnut-treated parchment, and nevertheless according to Rabbenu Tam it does not help.

Since in this law we do not rule like Rabbenu Tam, as is explained in Tur, Yoreh Deah, siman 271, and in the Beit Yosef there, it is proven that if it blackens more strongly and is recognizable on the parchment, it is considered writing. Furthermore, it appears that this is clear from Shulchan Aruch, siman 125, se'if 8, in the gloss, where the Rama writes, “There are those who are stringent even if the letter is recognizable,” as I wrote there. Since, according to the questioner, there is great concern of igun in this case, because the husband is in a distant country, caravans are not common, and there is the upheaval of war, the rabbi who asked ruled properly to validate the get. <b>In pressing circumstances, to correct it.</b> See Chelkat Mechokek and Beit Shmuel, who question the words of the Rav in the gloss. Part of this is cited in Ba'er Heitev, se'if katan 7. He further writes that where there is a complete invalidation, such as an extra letter in the essential text of the get, it does not help for another person to scrape away the letter, even in pressing circumstances. It is valid through witnesses of delivery in a case of igun, such as where the husband has abandoned the religion or the like, where there is no time to write another. Even with tefillin, scraping away a letter can help to validate it. One should not say in this case that it is chok tochot, since the word is corrected through scraping, because that concern applies specifically to a letter and not to words. See there. Ginat Veradim, se'if katan 36, also writes this. See there.

<b>And there are those who are stringent.</b> See Beit Shmuel, se'if katan 12, who writes that Seder Maharam, letter 87, is stringent in this matter if it is in the essential text of the get, even in a case of igun. But this does not seem to be the view of Maharik.

See Ginat Veradim, se'if katan 37, who also writes that in a case of igun and pressing circumstances, even according to Maharik, one may erase the drop of ink. Even if the drop touches the sides of the letter, if a child reads it properly, he may scrape away the drop and it is not chok tochot.

See there further, where he writes that if a drop of ink fell and the form of the letter did not change, and before the ink was absorbed into the parchment or paper the scribe quickly sucked the ink with his tongue and wiped it from the get, and it is not recognizable that there had been an erasure, it is logical to be lenient in this. Since even if the drop had dried there, it would not have changed the form of the letter, and according to the strict law it would be permitted to erase it even with a knife, except that as a stringency we do not erase with a knife because the erasure is noticeable, in this case one should not be stringent because the erasure is not noticeable. All the more so, if gallnut water or coffee water fell on the get and made stains, the get should not be invalidated, since the letter is recognizable.

However, if actual ink fell on one word, or even on one letter, whether in the essential text or in the standard formula, and it changed the meaning of the divorce, and if the ink had dried there it would have ruined the form of the letter, but the scribe quickly wiped it with his tongue, it appears to me that it is invalid and is considered chok tochot. It looked like two yuds inside the hollow of the heh.

He wrote: In siman 125, se'if 8, it is explained that if a drop of ink fell inside the letter, and there are those who are stringent even if the letter is recognizable. However, it appears that there the case is where the form of the letter did not change, but nevertheless the drop is attached to the upper roof or to the leg on the side. This is unlike our case, where it is not attached, but merely an extra dot inside the heh. It is possible that everyone agrees that he may erase the extra part and that this is not chok tochot.

It must be that the Chelkat Mechokek, se'if katan 12, also understood this way, for he asks that if the intent is to be stringent ab initio, there would have been no need to write it, as cited in Ba'er Heitev, se'if katan 10. If the view of these stringent authorities were that even if a drop of ink fell in the middle of the hollow of a letter, such as in a bet or heh, and it does not touch the sides, it is still prohibited, then what is his question from letters touching each other? There, they touch and meet; here, the drop does not touch any side. If so, in our case it is obvious that one may rely on this in a case of igun. These are his words. See there.

<b>שבא מארץ רחוקה. </b> עיין בתשו' פרח שושן כלל א' ס"ס ב' שהביא דכ"כ בתשו' מהרש"ך ח"ב סי' ה' דכל היכא דהשיירות מצויות לא מקרי שעת הדחק וכ"כ בת' הרד"ך סוף בית ג' מיהו בתשו' הריב"ז סי' ש"מ כתב דהאומר דהיכא שהמקום קרוב והשיירות מצויות אין כאן חשש עיגון כלל קרוב בעיני שזה טעות בדבר משנה דתנן המביא גט ממדינה למדינה במ"ה צ"ל בפ"נ ובפנ"ח כו' ואמרינן עלה ובדין הוא דלובעי תרי אלא משום עיגונא אקילו בה רבנן כו' אלמא דאפי' ממקום קרוב איכא למיחש לעיגונא דלא נתני דבריהם לשיעורין כו' וטעמא דמסתבר הוא דמאן לימא לן שבעוד שחוזר הגט לא ילך הבעל למ"ה או יבטל השליח או ימות ואין שליט ברוח ואין שלטון כו' ע"ש. שוב ראיתי בס' ג"פ ס"ק ע"ט הביא ג"כ דברי הרדב"ז הנ"ל וחולק עליו ומסיק דאין לסמוך ע"ז להקל ולומר דהשתא בכל אתר יש עיגון דבכל דוכתא דשיירות מצויו' לאו מקום עיגון חשיב וכן העלה הרב מהרד"ך כו' ע"ש וגם בסי' קכ"ו ס"ק ס"ה הזכיר קצת מזה ע"ש. ועיין בס' בית מאיר ס"ו כתב דמה שהשיב הג"פ על ראיית הרדב"ז הנ"ל אינו מובן כו' (גם אני בנ"צ כתבתי לתמיה על הג"פ בזה מצד אחר) אך לדינא יפה כיון הג"פ דכן פי' רש"י להדיא דף י"ט בשעת הדחק שהלך זה לדרכו או נישת ומתקנת נאמנות השליח אין ללמוד כלל דמשום עיגון קל האמינוהו ותדע שהרי אף בהבאת גט שחרור נאמן כו' אבל לענינים אחרים צריך שעת הדחק ממש עכ"ד ועמ"ש לעיל סי' מ"ב סק"ו:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>That came from a distant land.</b> See the responsum Perach Shoshan, kelal 1, end of siman 2, who cites that the responsa of Maharshach, part 2, siman 5, also writes that wherever caravans are common, it is not called a pressing circumstance. The responsa of the Radach, end of bayit 3, also writes this.

However, the responsa of Rivaz, siman 340, writes that one who says that where the place is close and caravans are common there is no concern of igun at all, in his eyes this is close to an error in an explicit Mishnah. For we learned: One who brings a get from one country to another overseas must say, “It was written before me and signed before me.” The Gemara says about this that by right we should require two witnesses, but because of igun the Sages were lenient. This shows that even from a nearby place there is concern of igun, for the Sages did not make their words depend on measures. The reason is logical: who can tell us that while the get is being returned, the husband will not go overseas, or nullify the agency, or die? No one has control over the spirit, and there is no dominion. See there.

I later saw in Ginat Veradim, se'if katan 79, that he also cites the above words of the Radbaz, disagrees with him, and concludes that one may not rely on this to be lenient and say that now in every place there is igun. Wherever caravans are common, it is not considered a place of igun. So too concluded Rabbi Maharadach. See there. He also mentions some of this in siman 126, se'if katan 65. See there.

See Beit Meir, se'if 6, who writes that what the Ginat Veradim answered against the above proof of the Radbaz is not understandable. I also wrote in Nachalat Tzvi to question the Ginat Veradim on this from another angle. However, as to the law, the Ginat Veradim was correct, for Rashi explicitly explains on page 19 that “pressing circumstances” means that this person went on his way or she remarried. One cannot learn anything from the enactment of believing the agent, because they believed him due to a light concern of igun. Know this, for even when bringing a bill of manumission, he is believed. But for other matters, actual pressing circumstances are required. These are his words. See also what I wrote above, siman 42, se'if katan 6.
