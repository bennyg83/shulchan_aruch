**** ENGLISH ****
(ב) <b>And if he did not reach it, this is a safek.</b> This is the version of Maggid Mishneh and Beit Yosef. Maggid Mishneh explained that what it says, "it is a safek," means that if he did not say "give," it is uncertain whether his law is like a deathly ill person, who need not say "give." The Tur's version in Rambam is: if the matter was uncertain whether he reached Lot's drunkenness, this is a safek. Beit Yosef explained that even according to his version, his words accord with the Tur, and "if he did not reach it" means that it is not clear to us that he reached it; this is a safek. So it also implies in Darkei Moshe. From this law there is support that one should prevent them from drinking intoxicating things on the day of the get. So wrote Shiltei Gibborim in the name of Maharam. If when he commanded to write he was not drunk, and afterward became drunk, it appears at first glance that it is permitted to write the get while he is drunk.

<b>חולה דסמי' בידן</b>. הנה לדעת הטור אם נשתטה בשעת הכתיב' ונתינה אפי' אם היה בדעתו בעת שצו' לכתוב מ"מ הגט בטל מדאוריתא ואין חילוק בין סמי' בידן לבין אין סמיה בידן וכ"פ בש"ג וכתב אפי' לכהונה אינו פוסל אלא המחבר מחלק לדעת הרמב"ם בין אם סמי' בידן לבין אם אין סמי' בידן ואין נראה, ויותר מסתבר דהרמב"ם ס"ל כשהיה בדעתו בעת שצוה לכתוב אף על גב שנשתטה בעת הכתיבה ונתינה מדאורייתא מגורשת אלא מדרבנן פסול לכן אפי' לר' יוחנן אינו אלא פסול מדרבנן ואפי' אין סמי' בידו וכן כתב ב"ח וח"מ ובזה ניחא הא דאין צריך בדיקה כשנשחט בו רוב סימנים כמ"ש ס"ס ולא חיישינן שמא נשתטה אף על גב בעת שרמז לכתוב אין חשש כל כך שנשתטה כמ"ש בתו' מ"מ למה לא חיישינן שמא בעת הכתיבה ונתינה נשתט' אלא משום דאינו אלא מדרבנן לא חיישינן לזה:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>A sick person whose cure is in our hands.</b> According to the Tur, if he became deranged at the time of writing and delivery, even if he was mentally sound when he commanded them to write, the get is void by biblical law, and there is no distinction between whether the cure is in our hands or not. So ruled Shiltei Gibborim, and he wrote that it does not even disqualify her for kehunah. But the Mechaber distinguishes, according to the Rambam, between a case where the cure is in our hands and where it is not, and this does not appear correct. It is more reasonable that the Rambam holds that if he was mentally sound when he commanded them to write, then although he became deranged at the time of writing and delivery, she is divorced by biblical law and the get is invalid only rabbinically. Therefore even according to Rabbi Yochanan it is only rabbinically invalid, even where the cure is not in our hands. So wrote the Bach and Chelkat Mechokek. This explains why no examination is needed where most of the signs were cut, as written at the end of the siman, and we are not concerned that he perhaps became deranged. Although at the time he hinted to write there is not such great concern that he became deranged, as Tosafot write, why are we not concerned that he became deranged at the time of writing and delivery? Rather, because this is only rabbinic, we are not concerned for it.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>At the time of writing and at the time of delivery.</b> All the more so at the time he commanded them to write. But if he became deranged between writing and delivery and then recovered, or after part of the get was written he became deranged and they did not write until he recovered, there is no concern. So ruled Beit Yosef. However, Tosafot imply that even in such a case there is concern, and Darkei Moshe ruled that it is proper to be concerned for them; bedieved, however, one may be lenient. If they did not examine a deathly ill person who was speaking at the time of writing and delivery, Beit Yosef wrote that perhaps it is valid. What Darkei Moshe wrote that the Rosh in Seder HaGet invalidates is not so; there he speaks where it was known he was not mentally sound. It implies that even if they did not examine him when he commanded them to write, the law is the same. If he was not sound at the time of writing, it depends on the dispute above: according to the Rambam it is only rabbinic, and according to the Tur and Shiltei Gibborim it is biblical. If he was not sound at the time of delivery, perhaps even the Tur agrees.

If he became mute in his illness, the matter depends on a dispute. The Yerushalmi explains that the Mishnah at the beginning of Perek Mi SheAchazo, which says that one who became mute requires three examinations, speaks specifically where he became mute while healthy. But if he became mute because of illness, he does not require three examinations, since we attribute his muteness to weakness. Several decisors hold this way, including the Itur. According to him, even one who became mute while healthy is not suspected of insanity, because if there were such a concern, examination would not help, since perhaps he is sometimes sound and sometimes insane. Rather, the examination is to determine his form of communication. Thus if he had already commanded them to write and then became mute, even while healthy, no examination is needed. This is unlike Beit Yosef’s presentation of the Itur. According to other decisors, however, one who became mute raises concern that he became insane and requires examination, specifically if he became mute while healthy. If he became mute from illness, there is no such concern. But Rashi and the Ran explain the Mishnah as speaking where he became mute from illness, and even then we are concerned that he became insane; all the more if he became mute while healthy. According to this approach, if he commanded while speaking and then became mute, and they wrote without examination, one should not be lenient. Beit Yosef suggests that in a case of illness, where examination is impossible, all may agree bedieved that it is valid, but this is not correct according to that approach. Perhaps according to the Rambam, where the concern is only rabbinic, there is room to be lenient, but this is not certain. According to the Tur, if he was not sound at the time of writing the get is biblically void, and so ruled Shiltei Gibborim; therefore one should not be lenient.

<b>חד הן ותרי לאו</b>. מבואר בש"ס אם שאלוהו ג"פ הן וג"פ לאו ברצופים לא מהני דאמרינן שמא שיחא הן הן נקטי ליה ואפשר אם השיב על הן לאו לא מחזיקים אותו לשוטה ודאי אלא אמרינן שמא שיחא לאו לאו נקטי ליה ובודקין אותו אבל אם שואלים אותו בסירוגין והשיב על הן לאו מחזיקי' אותו לשוטה ודאי ולא בדקינן אותו עוד, ובתוס' ובטור מוכח אפי' כששואלי' אותו בסירוגין שואלים לו חד הן ותרי לאו חד הן ותרי לאו כי היכא דלא להוי ג' לאוין רצופים ולא כהמחבר שכתב חד הן ותרי לאוין חד לאו וכו', ורש"י פי' בתרצן הראשון בסרוגי' היינו ששוהה בין שאלה לשאלה וכ"כ רי"ו והגמיי' ויש לחוש לדבריהם:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>One yes and two no.</b> It is explained in the Talmud that if they ask him three times yes and three times no consecutively, it is ineffective, because perhaps he has adopted the pattern of yes, yes. If he answers no to a question requiring yes, we may not immediately presume him definitely insane; perhaps he has adopted the pattern of no, no, and we examine him further. But if they ask him in alternating fashion and he answers no where yes is expected, we presume him definitely insane and do not examine further. Tosafot and the Tur prove that even when asking alternately, they ask one yes and two no, one yes and two no, so that there not be three consecutive no answers. This is unlike the Mechaber’s wording of one yes, two no, one no, etc. Rashi’s first explanation of alternating means that he pauses between question and question; so wrote Ri"v, and Hagahot Maimoniyot, and one should be concerned for their words.

<b>He divorces by hint.</b> This implies no examination is needed, as Beit Yosef wrote, because just as a mute or deaf-mute married by hint, so he divorces by hint. However, the Rif and the Rosh at the beginning of Perek Mi SheAchazo write that a congenital deaf-mute is examined. The Maggid Mishneh also writes this. According to Beit Yosef, this examination is not an actual sanity examination but only checking whether he wants to divorce his wife. Hagahot Maimoniyot, however, writes in the name of the Rif that they examine him, so one should not be lenient in this.

This hint means with his head or hands, but mouth movements do not help. Handwriting depends on the dispute: according to the Rambam’s approach, handwriting is like hinting and may help; but according to the other decisors I brought in siman 120, handwriting does not help, and bodily hinting is better. What the Bach wrote in the name of Tosafot, that handwriting helps, is not precise; Tosafot wrote that only at the beginning of the sugya, when the assumption was that writing is better than hinting, but according to the conclusion that hinting is better, handwriting does not help. If, however, he married through handwriting, one can say that just as he married through handwriting he can divorce through handwriting; likewise if he married through mouth movements, he divorces through mouth movements. A deaf-mute who divorces has a get written like the standard formula, and a document is given to the woman stating that he divorced her through beit din by hint. See Ri"v and Responsa Tzemach Tzedek for the formula. Even if he himself betrothed her, the scribe’s writing by his hint is effective. Although I brought above the dispute whether full agency is needed to write a get, one cannot prove from here that agency is unnecessary, because since his kiddushin are not full kiddushin, full agency is not required here and they were lenient with a deaf-mute. But generally full agency may be required. An insane person’s kiddushin are not kiddushin. If he was insane at kiddushin, recovered, and became insane again, perhaps when sound he had relations for the sake of kiddushin; this applies when he was secluded with her before witnesses. If he was not secluded before witnesses but lived with her as husband and wife, I have written several times that the decisors dispute this. The signs of insanity are explained at the beginning of Chagigah, and the Rosh rules that even one sign, if done in a deranged manner, makes him a shoteh. Beit Yosef derives similarly from the Rambam. See Choshen Mishpat, siman 35, and Yoreh Deah, siman 1, where the listed signs are examples, not exclusive.

<b>גוסס</b>. הב"י כתב דליכא פלוגתא בין הפוסקים אלא כשיש שהות לבדוק אותו יכול לגרש לכ"ע אפי' אינו מדבר לפ"ז מ"ש כאן הרי הוא כחי איירי כשיש שהות לבדוק אותו לפ"ז לדעת רמ"א אפי' בדיקה לא מהני וצ"ל דלאו הטעם הוא משום דחיישינן שמא תטרוף דעתו דהא בנפל מן הגג יש לחוש שמא תטרוף דעתו ומהני הבדיקה אלא ס"ל מדהיה חולי ועכשיו הוא גוסס ואינו מדבר חשוב הוא כמת לכן א"י לגרש ונשחט חשוב יותר חי דהוא היה בריא עד שנשחט:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>A goses.</b> Beit Yosef wrote that there is no dispute among the decisors: when there is time to examine him, he can divorce according to everyone, even if he cannot speak. According to this, what is written here that he is like a living person refers to where there is time to examine him. According to the Rama, even examination does not help. One must say the reason is not concern that his mind may become confused, for with one who fell from a roof there is also concern that his mind may become confused and examination helps. Rather, the Rama holds that since he had an illness and is now a goses and cannot speak, he is considered like dead and cannot divorce. One whose windpipe and esophagus were cut is considered more alive, because he was healthy until the cutting.

<b>ורמז ואמר</b>. היינו שא"ל נכתוב גט לאשתך והרכין בראשו כ"פ תוס' והם ס"ל דא"צ בדיקה וכן משמע מהרמב"ם והטור ולרמב"ם שפיר משום דס"ל אם נשתטה אחר שצוה לכתוב מדאוריית' כותבין ונותנים הגט וכאן בעת שרמז לכתוב לא חיישינן שתטרוף דעתו דהא היה בריא ונשחט ורמז בודאי עדיין לא תטרוף דעתו כמ"ש בתוספות ובעת הנתינה לא חיישינן שמא תטרוף דעתו כיון דאינו אלא חשש דרבנן אבל לשיטות הטור דס"ל דהוא חשש דאורייתא קשה למה לא חיישינן שמא תטרוף דעתו ובדיעבד צ"ע אם לא בדקו אותו כי לא מצינו בדיקה זו אלא הר"ן כתב שהיה חוכך להצריך בדיקה וי"ל בדיעבד אפילו לא בדקו אותו נותנים גט, כתב ב"ח אנן אין אנו בקיאים בבדיקות אלו וח"מ כתב ע"ז דלא משמע כן מדברי רמ"א וצריך להתיישב בדבר, מיהו נראה חולה המדבר מודה ב"ח דהוא א"צ בדיקה ממש, וכן בנשחט דהא לרוב הפוסקים א"צ בדיקה כלל:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And he signaled and said.</b> Meaning that they said to him, "Shall we write a get for your wife?" and he nodded his head. So explained Tosafot, and they hold that examination is unnecessary; this is also implied by Rambam and the Tur. According to Rambam this works well, because he holds that if one became deranged after commanding them to write, by Torah law they write and give the get. Here, at the time he signaled to write, we are not concerned that his mind became confused, for he was healthy and then was slaughtered, and certainly when he signaled his mind had not yet become confused, as Tosafot wrote. At the time of delivery, we are not concerned lest his mind become confused, because that is only a rabbinic concern. But according to the Tur's approach, that this is a Torah concern, it is difficult why we are not concerned that his mind became confused. After the fact, if they did not examine him, this requires study, for we do not find such an examination except that the Ran wrote he was inclined to require examination. One can say that after the fact, even if they did not examine him, they give the get. Bach wrote that we are not expert in these examinations. Chelkat Mechokek wrote against this that it does not seem so from Rama's words, and the matter requires consideration. Nevertheless, it appears that Bach admits that an ill person who can speak does not require an actual examination; likewise one whose throat was cut, for according to most poskim no examination is needed at all.
