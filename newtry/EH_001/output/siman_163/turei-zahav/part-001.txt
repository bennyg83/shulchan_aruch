We do not heed his claim, for we rule like Rabbi Abba, who says that once judgment has been rendered the judgment stands. After a ruling has been made concerning the deceased's property, he cannot challenge it; regarding what he asks from the yavam's property, we tell him: bring proof. We do not follow Rabbi Yirmeyah, who says that the judgment is reopened and all the property is divided equally. So ruled the authorities cited by the Beit Yosef. Since there is also another view that rules like Rabbi Yirmeyah, it seems that the "there is one who says" mentioned in the Shulchan Aruch belongs here, meaning that the judgment is reopened.<br />
The glossator cites Beit Shmuel, who explains that this is Rambam's view. He distinguishes this case from the doubtful son and the sons of the yavam with respect to the grandfather's estate. Here, if the doubtful son had litigated with his father, each would have taken half; therefore even now, when he litigates with the yavam's sons, their rights are not changed, and they take their father's share. In the grandfather's estate, by contrast, had the doubtful son litigated with the yavam he would have been entirely rejected; only now is a new claim created, so he first takes what they concede and divides only what remains in dispute.

These words are very difficult. If, when litigating with the yavam, the yavam would have taken half because both sides were doubtful, why should that decide the case when he litigates with the yavam's sons? Against the yavam himself each side denied the other's status, so the property was genuinely in doubt. Against the sons, however, they concede one portion to him and dispute only the remainder; only the remainder should be divided. Nor is there proof from the Rosh's question, for the Rosh answered that in the grandfather's estate the yavam's position is stronger than his sons' position: the yavam can say the doubtful son is not the grandfather's heir at all, whereas the sons concede that he is an heir, only they claim through the yavam and he claims through the deceased brother. That answer applies here as well.

If one were to say that Rambam rejects the Rosh's answer and treats the sons as standing in their father's place, then in the grandfather's estate the sons should take everything except what they concede. Beit Shmuel's suggestion that because the doubtful son's right was newly created all his rights are newly created has no reason behind it. It is especially untenable where logic favors the sons of the yavam: had the yavam survived, the doubtful son would have been rejected; had the yavam died only after the grandfather, the doubtful son would have taken nothing. Only because the yavam died during the grandfather's lifetime does the doubtful son have an unavoidable claim to one portion, and as to the rest his claim is doubtful while theirs is certain.

Rambam's own language shows that in the deceased brother's estate both the doubtful son and the yavam are themselves doubtful claimants, and the same is true of the yavam's sons. The Rosh's question about the grandfather's estate does not apply in the same way, for there the yavam was certainly an heir of the grandfather and the doubtful son was doubtful. Here, by contrast, even the yavam was only a doubtful heir with respect to the deceased brother's estate. If so, why does Rambam say that all the property is divided equally?

The Maggid Mishneh indeed gives the reason that the sons stand in their father's place, but he also cites the view of R. Shimshon that the doubtful son first takes what they concede and only then divides the rest, and he says that this appears correct. The Taz therefore suggested that the gloss is misplaced and belongs in section 5, as an opinion following Rabbi Yirmeyah that the judgment is reopened. But this cannot be right: the gloss says that all the property is divided half to him and half to them, whereas according to Rabbi Yirmeyah he would take only as one brother, not half. Nor do we find an authority who allows the doubtful son to remove property from the yavam's sons in that manner. And even if this explains the Shulchan Aruch, it does not explain Rambam.

It seems rather that the gloss is indeed in section 4 and reflects Rambam. The reason is that in the estate of the deceased brother, when the yavam performed yibbum the estate initially belonged to the yavam. Only afterward, when the doubtful child was born, was a claim raised against property already in the yavam's possession. The ruling gives the doubtful heir half, while the remaining half stays with the yavam; that half is considered to have belonged to him retroactively from the time of yibbum. Therefore, when the yavam later dies, the doubtful son has no claim against the yavam's own estate as against the yavam's sons. The Maggid Mishneh's phrase that the sons stand in their father's place means that whether the father is alive or dead, that half is treated as his property.

This does not contradict Tosafot's distinction that "the judgment stands" applies only after the yavam and doubtful son have already divided. Before division, the doubtful son can say: even according to you, the second half is in your father's possession only because I might be your father's son; if so, give me a share with you. Since no judgment has yet been completed, "the judgment stands" cannot be said. But in the original dispute between the doubtful son and the yavam over the deceased brother's estate, the doubtful son has no claim to the half that remains in the yavam's possession. In the grandfather's estate, by contrast, the grandfather must still have been alive when the yavam died; otherwise the grandfather's property would already have passed to the yavam and the doubtful son could not claim against the sons. Since the grandfather's property was never in the yavam's possession, when the doubtful son and the yavam's sons divide it, neither side is more established than the other regarding the disputed portion, and they divide it.
