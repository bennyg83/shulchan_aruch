(א) <b>And some say that even with food and drink.</b> This is the approach of most poskim, who disagree with Rambam and hold that Ravin does not dispute the Mishnah. The general rule is: if he did not eat, everything is returned, even if he is the one who retracts; she must pay for items normally consumed through use, and likewise for food and drink that he sent and she consumed, less one third. At first glance, if the item is still present and became worn, then if it remains fit for its original use she need not pay for the wear, as written at the beginning of siman 96 regarding dowry property. If they were not items normally consumed through use and she sold them, it appears her law is like that of a robber, and she pays according to their value at the time of the taking, without deducting one third; see Nimukei Yosef. If he ate or drank to the value of a dinar, then if he retracts we say he waived items normally consumed through use that were consumed. If they are items normally consumed through use and she used them but they were not consumed, this is an unresolved question in the Gemara and we do not extract from her; if he seized, seizure helps according to those poskim who hold that seizure helps in an unresolved question, as Chelkat Mechokek wrote in several places. Regarding beikha and savanta, he certainly waives. Rambam explained these as types of scarves and women's ornaments, while Ri"v explained them as shoes and scarves; even if they are still present she need not return them, because he certainly waives them, and I do not know why the Tur wrote nothing about this. Ri"v ruled that even if she is the one who retracts, she need not return them, and they are treated more leniently than food and drink. Items not normally consumed through use must be returned even if she sold them; I wrote nearby that she must pay their value at the time of sale, and even if she profited with the money she need not give him anything, as Nimukei Yosef wrote. If she retracted, she must pay for items normally consumed through use that were consumed, less one third of their value. Money is treated like items normally consumed through use, and clothing depends on the circumstances. Likewise, when one of them died, we also say that if he ate, he waived. The Gemara's question regarding what she must pay is where he ate one third of a dinar, whether we say he waived one third of what he sent; it also asks what the law is regarding appreciation; see Nimukei Yosef. The same applies if he sent it to him to eat, or if his agent ate it, and in all these cases we do not extract from her. In the responsum of the Rosh cited by the Tur, he wrote that if each sent to the other, but one party's item became worn, the other deducts the entire depreciation. Bach in Kuntrus Acharon explained that this speaks of shiddukhin before kiddushin, and therefore all the depreciation is deducted. This does not appear to me. Nimukei Yosef distinguishes between shiddukhin and erusin only regarding a case where he ate there, for one may say he waived for an arusah but not for a meshuddekhet. But regarding payment less one third, we hold in several places that one deducts a third; likewise, if a father leaves his orphans a borrowed cow and they ate it, they pay less one third. Therefore even with a meshuddekhet she need not pay more. The main explanation is like the Perishah's second answer: when the party who retracts is in possession, he pays less one third; but when each sent to the other, each is in possession, so all depreciation is deducted from the original value. According to this, even if he sent food and drink, they would deduct. However, one can say that in the case there, the retraction was from the bride's side, and therefore he ruled that the groom deducts from what he holds the entire depreciation from the original value, because there were two favorable factors: he was in possession and he was not the one who retracted. But if he is in possession and he is the one who retracts, one can say he does not deduct the entire depreciation; although he is in possession, since he retracts, he does not deduct all the loss.

Even if you say that he can appease her with money, even if he stipulated that if she refuses he must appease her, must he give heaps of dinars? And if you say that he should have stipulated that if she refuses he is exempt, this is uncommon coercion, for all daughters agree to whomever the father wishes. Even if it were somewhat common, he need not stipulate. See Shach, Yoreh Deah siman 232, Terumat HaDeshen siman 55, and this responsum, where his words are explicit. See Mordechai, chapter 1 of Bava Metzia, which seems not like Rashba, and Mordechai, chapter Mi Shemet. See siman 144: we hold in gittin that even common-uncommon coercion does not help, but in monetary law even common-uncommon coercion need not be stipulated, and he is exempt. Chelkat Mechokek wrote that at first glance here, where the obligation was to give the groom the dowry and bride, and if he does not fulfill this he must pay a penalty, he is exempt from the penalty because he is coerced. But if he obligated himself in a certain sum without condition, and the groom gave a writing that if he gives him the bride and dowry the debt is waived, the claim of coercion is not relevant. Nevertheless, one may say that there is a clear assessment that he obligated himself only if his daughter would not refuse. I wrote in siman 77 in the name of Hagahot Maimoniyot that when his daughter claims "he is repulsive to me," he need not pay the debt document he gave his son-in-law for the dowry; this is compared to where his daughter died, where we do not extract the dowry money from him even if he gave a debt document, as written in siman 55, for presumably his intent was specifically that his daughter benefit from the dowry money. Therefore here too, even if he gave a debt document and his daughter refuses, he is exempt from the penalty. There I wrote that even if his daughter did not say "he is repulsive to me," but said other claims, he is exempt from giving the dowry, for there is a clear assessment that his intent was to give the dowry in a way that his daughter would benefit. Although there the marriage had already taken place, nevertheless he is exempt from giving the dowry when his daughter will not benefit; all the more so here, before the marriage, where she refuses, he is exempt. However, Taz wrote there that where he gives a plain debt document like ours, he is obligated to give, because our debt document is like already having paid, since he can give it into another's hand; siman 55 does not speak of our kind of debt document. Where the coercion is that he died, one can distinguish as Chelkat Mechokek did. If the son made a match for his sister without authorization from his father, and the father does not wish to make the match, its law is like where his daughter refuses. See Responsa She'erit Yosef, simanim 2, 4, 5, and 6. There are several practical laws, and I will write them briefly: if the father sent his son to find a match for his daughter and to inform him before making the match, but the son changed and said that he had full power to act, and made the match, and the father refuses and does not want the match, both are exempt from the penalty. This is similar to what is written in Choshen Mishpat siman 182, that when an agent changes and the sender wishes to void it, the sale is void. At first glance it is difficult why the son is exempt, since he caused the embarrassment. One must say that he holds one is exempt for indirect causation of embarrassment. One may also say that this is only grama and not garmi, for although it was not in his power to act, he thought his father would agree. From this we learn that if the father says he has a written authorization from his daughter and this was false, and she refuses, both are exempt, for it is obvious that the father relied on his daughter not refusing. Wherever the father is exempt from the penalty, the guarantors are also exempt. If he was coerced by money, he is exempt from the penalty; but if he obligated himself by oath, he is liable because of the oath, for one cannot be exempted from an oath because of monetary coercion. The guarantors, however, are exempt, since he is exempt monetarily and the guarantor guaranteed only the money. See Responsa She'erit Yosef siman 78. Rashba, in responsum siman 775, exempts even from an oath because of coercion where his daughter refuses, as written nearby, even if he can appease her with much money, because there is a clear assessment that he did not swear with this in mind.
