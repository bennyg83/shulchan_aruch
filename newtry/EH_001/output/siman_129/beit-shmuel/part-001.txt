**** ENGLISH ****
(א) <b>The name of the place of writing, "who is called."</b> Although nearby he wrote that one writes "and every name," the two places are different. Taz wrote that where it is known to everyone that he has another name in another place, everyone agrees that the names are specified; the poskim disagree only where it is not known to everyone that he has another name. So too the Tur wrote in the name of Ramah, that one specifies both names. His reason is that it is doubtful whether the place of writing is primary, as stated in the Talmud, or whether the place of giving is primary, as stated in the Tosefta; therefore one specifies both. The Mechaber abbreviated this law, so I will briefly state the approaches. According to the Rosh, Ran, and Tur: if he has one name in the place of writing and another in the place of giving, and they know in the place of writing that he has another name in the place of giving, she must write both. If she wrote only one, even if she wrote the name of the place of giving, the get is invalid. This is proven in the Talmud, chapter HaSholeach: if he divorced his wife in Judea with his Judean name, meaning the place of giving as Tosafot wrote, she is not divorced until he divorces his wife in Judea with his Judean name and the Galilean name with it. But if they did not know that he had another name, the get is valid, even if afterward it becomes known that he had another name. According to Semag and Maggid Mishneh, however, the sugya has another explanation: if he has two names, one in the place of writing and one in the place of giving, even if they do not know that he has another name elsewhere, the get is invalid, lest people in that place say that this is not the same man who divorced. What Rav Ashi said, "but where he was not established with two names," means that he does not have a special name in each place; rather, the name he has in the place of giving is also known in the place of writing, and in both places they know both names. Even though he has another name in a third place that is neither the place of writing nor the place of giving, it is valid because he wrote the name of the place of writing and giving. This is the approach of Ba'al HaTerumah.

**** ENGLISH ****
(ב) <b>We write "who is called."</b> At first glance this contradicts what was written nearby, that when one of the names is a foreign-language name, one writes "who is known as." Bach distinguished and wrote that where his father gave him two names when entering him into the covenant, Rama distinguishes between foreign-language names and sacred names; but if a new name was added afterward, one always writes "who is called," even for a foreign-language name. This distinction is not correct, as is clear from Responsa Maharam Mintz and Responsa Rama. It is more reasonable that the Rav Rama's intent here is not to teach whether one writes "who is known as" or "who is called"; rather, his intent here is that the Hebrew name is primary. So too wrote Taz and Nachalat Shivah. As a matter of law, it appears primary that where there is doubt one writes "who is called," as written in Responsa Rama, siman 84; see there. The term "who is called" applies even to a nickname, for that is what people call him. But with a Hebrew name the term "nickname" does not apply. So too wrote Sefer Shemot, folio 31.

(ה) <b>Two times, etc.</b> With this kind of name change one writes demitkarei, all without a vav, because the later name always uproots the first name, and therefore it is not appropriate to say umitkarei. But with a nickname one writes vehamekhuneh, as written above: hamekhuneh Zalman vehamekhuneh Zamil. If his name was changed because of some other matter, it appears that one writes udemitkarei, for then we do not say that the first name was uprooted, unless it was changed because of illness. However, perhaps one could say that the Sages did not distinguish. If his name was changed and the first name fell out of use, Beit Yosef wrote that two Gittin should be given; but in Mordekhai, Perek HaSholeach, and in the Ashkenazic responsum cited by Beit Yosef, they wrote that one should divorce only with the second name, and so he wrote in siman 128. This is also found in Rashi at the end of Perek Bameh Madlikin, and so Levush ruled, and so Maharam's Seder wrote; this is the main ruling. Levush wrote that if the obsolete name was also written, the Get is invalid, even though it was his first name. In Ger HaMegarresh he wrote that if a convert's gentile name fell out of use and he wrote the gentile name, it is valid after the fact. At first glance this is surprising: why is it different from an Israelite name that fell out of use? But in truth this is not difficult at all, for with an Israelite name there is concern that people will say this is not the divorcing man but another person with that name; but no Jew has a gentile name. I wrote similarly above in the name of Taz.
