**** ENGLISH ****
Some say that one should be stringent, and the groom does not say, etc. See Responsa Kehillat Yaakov of Karlin, siman 18, about a man who became engaged to a woman through the customary binding agreement, after which it became known that he was wicked and already had two wives. She withdrew from the match, and he spread a report that immediately after the engagement he had given her four gold coins and claimed that he said he was giving them as kiddushin. She denied hearing any language of kiddushin, and one witness said he had intentionally listened lest the man try to mention kiddushin and knew clearly that he said nothing. The other witnesses likewise said they heard no language of kiddushin. Meanwhile the man fled and his location was unknown, leaving the young woman an agunah.

Kehillat Yaakov analyzes the laws of concern for gifts sent after betrothal and concludes that although we cannot decide among the great earlier authorities and therefore generally adopt all stringencies, in our time there is not even a minority who betroth in that manner. No one ordinarily suspects gifts as kiddushin, except that Rema requires initial caution when the man says he intended kiddushin, unless there is another ground for leniency. Here his statement is only a stringency, for words do not help when she denies them, as in the Mishnah where a man says "I betrothed you" and she says "you did not." In addition, the money was given immediately at the engagement, when it was known there had been no prior kiddushin; he claims to have said explicit kiddushin language, but a witness who listened carefully denies it, the second witness heard nothing, and the facts show him to be lying. Even according to his own account, if the witnesses did not hear it, the kiddushin lacked witnesses.

Kehillat Yaakov adds that in our time, when everyone writes formal engagement conditions, sets a wedding date, records gifts to the bride, and includes penalties, there is no basis to suspect gifts as kiddushin. The classic concern is that a man would not risk sending money for nothing and therefore likely betrothed first; today engagements are secured by written obligations and penalties, and people commonly send even large gifts without fearing withdrawal. Pitchei Teshuvah notes that this consideration may not fit the case of a wicked fiance who knows that once his conduct is discovered she will withdraw without penalty, but it is useful in other cases. Kehillat Yaakov further compares the matter to Hillel's reliance on the wording of ordinary documents: the engagement document states that the groom will marry the bride by chuppah and kiddushin according to the law of Moses and Israel and fixes a time for the chuppah, so there is no reason to suspect a hidden act contradicting those explicit terms. He concludes that the woman may plainly marry whomever she wishes, especially in an agunah situation where the fiance has disappeared.
