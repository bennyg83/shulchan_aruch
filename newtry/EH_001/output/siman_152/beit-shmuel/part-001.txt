**** ENGLISH ****
<b>It should be validated through its signatures.</b> Then he has no migo and is not believed. But claims that it was lost or fell from him are not believed even where there is a migo. If two witnesses challenge it and say it is forged, the Tur holds that validation is effective, and Beit Yosef also wrote this simply. See siman 141 seif 53, Derishah at the end of siman 67, Responsa Rosh, klal 45, and the Tur at the end of siman 67. If a witness says that the husband did not instruct him to sign, he is believed even if his handwriting is confirmed from elsewhere, because, due to the sins of the generation, it is common for many to occupy themselves with gittin though they know only a little, and they assume that when the husband says "write," his intention is that they sign. According to this it implies that even if two witnesses say so, and the scribe does not support them, they are believed, unlike Ba'er Heitev, who wrote that the reason is because the scribe supports them. Although witnesses are not believed to say that they erred in the king's year, as stated in Choshen Mishpat siman 43, that case is different, for here Shmuel was uncertain whether "write" means "sign," and therefore this is the kind of error people commonly make. From this we learn regarding what is written in Choshen Mishpat, in the gloss to Shulchan Aruch siman 8 in the name of the Rosh, that witnesses are not believed to say they erred: this does not apply to an error that people commonly make. The Shach there discusses this at length, but this responsum escaped him. One should not say that here the reason is that the scribe supports them, for certainly if the witnesses are not believed, the scribe's support would not help. Bach wrote that the witness is believed to say that the husband did not instruct him to sign because he becomes disqualified as wicked only when he is wicked through monetary wrongdoing. His words are astonishing, for there is a Torah prohibition here, and wickedness through monetary wrongdoing is not required. Perhaps, since there are witnesses to the delivery, the signatures involve only a rabbinic prohibition. Yet this remains difficult: even if he is not rendered wicked, once he has testified he cannot return and testify differently, for we hold that witnesses signed on a document are treated as though their testimony has been examined in beit din. Therefore witnesses are not believed to say that they were coerced by threat to life, as stated in the Talmud and the decisors. Certainly this is not comparable. The main reason is as I wrote.
