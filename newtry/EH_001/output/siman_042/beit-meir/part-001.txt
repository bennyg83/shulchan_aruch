Gloss: but if he contradicts. The Beit Shmuel wrote: it does not appear to me, for this is like one who says, I snatched it, etc. I do not know what one matter has to do with the other. There, the early authorities already addressed why he should not be believed with an oath that he snatched his own property by means of a migo. One reason is that it is a weak migo, since to contradict a witness by oath is an especially brazen claim. Another reason is that one witness with respect to imposing an oath is like two witnesses with respect to money by Torah law, and whenever he cannot swear according to law, the migo does not help. See the Shach there. But here there is no place for an oath, as is stated in Rashi, Kiddushin 65b, cited by Terumat HaDeshen in his rulings, siman 37, where he explained that even a heset oath does not apply. If so, according to all reasons, a migo properly applies here. See siman 7 seif 4 and Shach, Choshen Mishpat siman 58 s.k. 23. However, this is difficult: how can we believe him in the claim of jesting by means of a migo more than contradiction itself would have helped? For in contradiction, at least the one who admits is forbidden. If so, granted that with respect to himself he is believed by the migo that he could have contradicted, but from where will help come to her? If you say that since with respect to himself he has a migo and is not interested in the matter, then with respect to her he is no worse than one witness, that is not so, for this is a matter of ervah. See siman 35 seif 11. However, in the Bach and in his responsum siman 97 I did not find any hint of what is written in his name. Rather, in the responsum the case was that both of them claimed that they intended to jest, and in his novellae too he says only that a claim of jesting helps like contradiction. What he wrote, both of them or one of them, etc., means each according to his law: if both claim this, both are permitted to the other's relatives; if one claims it, that one is permitted and the other is forbidden. It seems to me that based on the Chelkat Mechokek, even the Bach was explained this way; but in my humble opinion this is not so. The Rama z"l also intended only that where one contradicts the other, there is no concern, as is proven from the sugya to one who studies it. These words of the Bach, however, are that with a claim of jesting against one witness, matters in the heart do not apply. Since the Chelkat Mechokek and Beit Shmuel understood that the matter depends on migo, the Chelkat Mechokek properly challenged: where they are matters in the heart, and one cannot say that we are witnesses or that there is a clear presumption, even a migo does not help. This is also proven in Kiddushin 65 regarding me'ilah, from which the principle that matters in the heart are not legally operative is derived. The Gemara concludes that he has a migo that he could say, I remembered; nevertheless he has committed me'ilah. This proves that a migo is not like we are witnesses or a clear presumption, which are like matters expressly stated at the time of the act; a migo is different. But in truth, in the Bach the word migo is not mentioned. Rather, this is his language: it is explained that the case is not only where he contradicts him by saying, this never happened, but also, etc.; he is called one who does not admit to the words of the witness, etc. As for what is written in Teshuvot Maimoniyot, etc., there the case involved two witnesses; since, if he contradicted them, we would not listen to him, the same applies where he says, my intent was to jest. But with one witness, whenever he makes a claim and they say that no kiddushin took place there, this is called that he does not admit, and there is no kiddushin. It appears in my humble opinion that this is his view: even the one who holds that we are concerned for one witness means as Rashi z"l explained, that one witness is important enough to obligate an oath in monetary law; therefore in ervah too he is important enough for kiddushin to be established before him, but only where both admit. Meaning: with any claim that they make at least to void the kiddushin, they are no longer called admitting, and the kiddushin is not considered established through him. Nevertheless it is not certain that one should be lenient, and the law follows the Chelkat Mechokek; the Rashba is well explained as the Beit Shmuel explained. But I later examined the Tashbetz to see the Rashba's own language. It is in part 1 siman 70. The question there was about one who betrothed a woman before women, and he admitted the matter, but she said, I was jesting with you because I knew that the testimony of women is nothing. He answered that even according to the one who is concerned for one witness, he is not concerned for female witnesses, since they do not stand for an oath or to combine like one witness. Moreover, even with one witness, the one who is concerned is concerned only where both admit; but if there is contradiction between them, we are not concerned for kiddushin. So wrote the Rashba z"l. Since the woman claims that she did not take them as kiddushin and was jesting, there is no one who is concerned for kiddushin even with one contradicting witness, and all the more so with female witnesses. End quote. Thus, in the name of the Rashba it says only that where there is contradiction, there is no view that is concerned. The Tashbetz added that if his case had occurred even before one witness, he would not have been concerned, and he judged and wrote: all the more so with female witnesses. Certainly in his case she only said that in her intent she was jesting, not explicitly, as the Beit Shmuel explained. This is almost as I explained the reasoning of the Bach, who ruled in his case, where the woman claimed that she did not understand how far the matter reached, etc., that it is like contradiction before one witness. One cannot distinguish that there she claimed, because I knew that female witnesses are nothing; that is not so, for according to them one may still say that these are matters in the heart. Rather, it implies as I understood from the Bach, and so it appears from the Beit Yosef's citation. Still, it is difficult to build a foundation on the words of the Tashbetz, which are only incidental; for in truth, with female witnesses there is no concern even without a claim of jesting. The reasoning is also difficult, for it is already proven from the Gemara's question, Rava challenged Rav Nachman, that according to the one who is concerned for one witness, even with contradiction only the one who contradicts is permitted while the other is forbidden. If we accepted the reasoning that contradiction prevents the kiddushin from being established through them, the second party should also be permitted. Rather, this is not so; only with respect to the contradicting party is he not believed. If so, where both admit but claim that they intended to jest, and matters in the heart are not legally operative, why should the kiddushin not take effect? Therefore, where this is not a place of iggun, it is proper to be stringent. In a case involving a mitzvah, it is in any event permitted as in the Shulchan Aruch.

And there is no distinction, etc. See Beit Shmuel, who wrote: if the wine had no presumption of permissibility, etc. Necessarily he means that something happened to the wine that creates room to doubt whether it was libated, similar to the case of throwing the kiddushin money, where one witness and another witness disagree whether the libation was completed. He then wrote: necessarily he holds that what is said in Shevuot, that one witness in contradiction is not believed, etc., is proven, etc. I do not know from where this is proven according to Tosafot's method, for where she has a presumption of being unmarried, they plainly hold that even where a witness is contradicted, he is not believed. He further wrote: according to this, what the Rav wrote in the name of the responsum is proven; see the Shach. That is, the Shach inferred from the precise language of responsum Rashba the opposite, while he proved from the Chelkat Mechokek in the Rashba's name that she may not marry even where she has a presumption of being unmarried, and one witness says her father betrothed her while the other says he did not betroth her; in that case the presumption was not weakened, yet she may not marry. In my humble opinion, this is not proof. One can say the Rashba speaks in a case where the presumption was weakened, such as where betrothal arrangements were gathered at her father's house and the witnesses disagree whether the kiddushin were completed. The Maggid Mishneh did not descend to explain this, for his main intent was to explain that the case speaks where she is silent and cannot contradict them. One can also say that here, because of the dignity of Jewish lineage, they were stringent. Although Tosafot hold that where the presumption was not weakened she is permitted ab initio, that follows their method, as explained in siman 6 in their name, that even where the presumption was weakened they are not rabbinically stringent in all prohibitions except in a matter of lineage dignity. They also hold that where the presumption was not weakened, even in a matter of lineage dignity, she is permitted ab initio. But the Rashba holds that in all prohibitions, where the presumption was weakened, we are rabbinically stringent; one may say that in a matter of lineage dignity he holds they were stringent even where it was not weakened. But regarding wine of gentile libation it may be as the Shach implies. So too, according to what I wrote there, that Tosafot follow the method of Maharam in Hagahot Maimoniyot at the beginning of Hilkhot Ishut, to be more stringent in a doubt between witness and witness than in a doubt of fact, one must also say that all this did not seem reasonable to them where the presumption was not weakened. As for the Beit Shmuel's support from responsum Rashba in the Beit Yosef, at the end of siman 67, that one cannot distinguish between the present law, ab initio, and the law of forbidden wine, since the Rashba compares ab initio prohibition to extracting money, meaning a document about which witnesses say he was seeking to forge it, where we rule that collection is not made with it: I am astonished. According to his words, it would be impossible in any way to prohibit marriage ab initio except where money could be extracted. One witness himself proves otherwise, for no one says a person can extract money through him, even if the litigant does not contradict him, such as where one witness says, your animal damaged, as explained in Shach, Choshen Mishpat siman 72 s.k. 49. Even according to the initial thought that for the view requiring payment where one is liable for an oath but cannot swear, even where he could not have known, he would indeed be liable, that is because of being liable for an oath but unable to swear. But if one witness supports him and contradicts the other witness, it is obvious that according to everyone he is exempt. If so, it would be difficult for the Rashba according to his own method: why here may she not marry? According to my words, however, it is clear. The Rashba's words are certainly correct: in this matter we should ordinarily compare the prohibition that she may not marry to extracting money, since we wish to remove her from her presumption, except where we find that the Gemara was stringent regarding witness against witness because of lineage dignity, that she may not marry. Nevertheless, one should not learn from this to other prohibitions to forbid them, for they too are ordinarily comparable to extracting money.

And likewise, if he betrothed before two witnesses and one was a relative, it is like betrothing before one witness. The Chelkat Mechokek wrote that this speaks where the invalid witness did not join at the time of seeing. The Beit Shmuel added and wrote that one may also say that he did not intend for testimony; nevertheless what they testify today helps, even though he did not intend for testimony. In Responsa Shevut Yaakov, question 21, s.v. but, he was pressed by the difficulty of the Beit Hillel and answered in a remote way. I do not know what astonishment he found in the above answer. Incidentally, see also what he asked within his answer: why should we say that Rabbi and Rabbi Yose disagree about this reasoning? In my humble opinion this is not difficult at all. Once Rabbi derived three from two regarding monetary law, that if one of the two is invalid the two valid ones are also disqualified, there is no longer reason to distinguish and say that where there are two and one is invalid, the valid one is believed for an oath. This is unlike Rabbi Yose, who does not make any comparison to monetary law for this reason. Examine carefully the Shach in Choshen Mishpat siman 36, according to his explanation in s.k. 2 and 3. According to him, the Shulchan Aruch and Rambam here necessarily speak only where the invalid witness's defect is that he did not intend, but the valid witness did intend for testimony. For otherwise, if both merely came to see, one cannot say that the testimony is established through the remaining witnesses on the basis of his reasoning, where he says that since a relative or invalid person who came to see does not disqualify the other witnesses, necessarily because they came only to see they are not considered witnesses. If so, the valid people who came to see should also not be considered witnesses, and judgment should not be decided by them. It is not reasonable that relatives should not be considered witnesses while valid people are considered witnesses. Rather, we say: anyone who came to see is not considered a witness, and anyone who came to testify is considered a witness. This is the essence of his language; see there. In my humble opinion this is not implied by the Rama's language here. In my humble opinion, with due respect, his explanation is not clear in the language of the Rambam, and certainly not in the language of the Shulchan Aruch. For the Shulchan Aruch wrote: but if all of them did not intend to testify, the testimony is established through the others. He is forced to explain s.k. 2 as referring to others who did intend to testify. If so, if ten witnesses came to testify and one of them was found invalid, and we examined them and eight valid witnesses said, we came to see, while the relative together with one valid witness, or two relatives, said they intended to testify, then not all of them intended to testify, and nevertheless the testimony cannot be established. Furthermore, the main reasoning is astonishing. He wrote that therefore, whenever a relative is found, all who came to see are not considered testimony, and the proof is that therefore the relative too is not considered testimony. This may be forced where the relative is among those who came to see. But if the relative is among those who came to testify, and we disqualify all who came to testify, who forces us to say from this that all who came to see are not considered testimony? The main point in my humble opinion is the exact opposite: if the relative is among those who came to testify, then all who came to testify are disqualified, and the testimony is established through those who came to see. If the relative is among those who came to see, then the testimony is established through all the valid witnesses. The reason is not as he wrote, that then we do not regard the invalid witness's testimony as testimony. This is not reasonable, for if coming to see counts as valid testimony in truth, how can it be that when an invalid witness is found among them it is not testimony? Rather, the reason is that all who came to testify at the time of the act are joined by their intent into one group and one testimony regarding the rule that if one of them is found invalid, etc. Therefore, if the relative came to testify, all his companions who came to testify are disqualified, and those who came to see remain valid. If the relative came only to see, he has no joined group at all, and all valid witnesses, whether they came to testify or to see, are valid. By this the above Shulchan Aruch is precise. So too at the end, where it wrote, if among those who intended to testify a relative was found, their testimony is void, it means the testimony of those who intended; but those who came to see are valid, because they have no joining with those who came to testify, since with everyone who came to see the question, what should they have done, applies. As for the support that the Shach z"l brought from the language of the Rambam, this is his language: if one of them is found to be a relative, the testimony is void, etc. When does this apply? When all of them intended to testify. But if he did not intend, what should two brothers among the crowd do, when the people saw him kill, etc.? How does one examine the matter? When a large group of witnesses comes before bet din as one group, the court says to them: when you saw this person kill or injure, did you come to testify or to see? Anyone who says, I did not come to testify but only to see, and I came among the crowd, they separate him. If among those who intended to testify a relative is found, the testimony of all of them is void. In my humble opinion this means the testimony of all those who intended. But if he is found among those who came to see, even those who intended remain valid. Therefore they ask all of them and separate them; they do not ask only the relative. For if they asked only him, granted, if he says, I came to see, we know all are valid. But if he says, I came to testify, then it must be clarified who came to see so that the testimony may be established through them. What he wrote afterward, when does this apply, etc., may be understood as: when are we forced to investigate whether they came to see or testify? When there was among them, etc.; but if all are valid, etc. Meaning: in the body of the testimony there is no distinction between coming to see and coming to testify, and therefore this investigation is needed only where one is found, etc. What the Shach concluded, that the testimony is established through the others who warned him for testimony, as taught in the Mishnah, perhaps he means that just as in the Mishnah, necessarily where they did not warn him, when it teaches that the testimony is established through the others, it means through the valid witnesses who did warn, for without warning what establishment of testimony is relevant in capital cases. So too ordinarily, when we say that if they came to see the testimony is established through the others, it is specifically through those who intended for testimony. In my humble opinion even this is not so, for establishment of testimony is relevant even in capital cases through those who came to see, even if they did not warn, namely where the warning was done in their presence by others, as explained in Rambam, Hilkhot Sanhedrin chapter 12 halakhah 2. The words of Tosafot in Makkot 6, s.v. heikhi amrinan, require study: why did they strain to explain that it refers to monetary law? In capital law too the question, how, applies, where the invalid witness did not warn, but one of those standing there did. So too Ritva there actually wrote according to Rashi. Necessarily this is the explanation: it refers to monetary law, for if it referred to capital law there would be no basis to ask, how, etc.; from where would we know that it depends on intent? Perhaps only warning creates the joining to consider them one testimony, whereas without warning, even if they intended, the question, what should they have done, still applies. Therefore they wrote that the main question is in monetary law, for even in monetary law, if one is found invalid, etc., and there, on 6b, it depends only on intent. Automatically, even in capital law, even without their having warned, the examination applies, as Rashi explained in the Mishnah regarding they did not warn and did not intend. I later found in Sanhedrin 9, at the end of the comment, that their intent is explained this way. In my humble opinion, my explanation of the Rambam is also indicated by his Commentary to the Mishnah, whose language is: the meaning of Rabbi's words is that when a relative comes and sees what he saw, the testimony is established by two valid witnesses from among those who saw, and we do not attend to the rest. Thus he wrote, two valid witnesses from among those who saw, meaning those who came to see. If so, here the case is simply where both came to see.

<b>If he betrothed before witnesses, etc., she is betrothed.</b> This derives from Kiddushin 43, where the school of Rabbi Sheila said: an agent cannot become a witness. What is the reason? If one says it is because he did not say to him, be a witness for me, then if he betrothed before two witnesses but did not say, you are my witnesses, would that too not be kiddushin? One could still say that although he need not say you are my witnesses, nevertheless the witnesses must at least intend on their own to testify; but if they came merely to see, they are not witnesses and she is not betrothed. Even so, the Gemara asks because the statement that an agent cannot become a witness was taught absolutely. Why not distinguish: where they intended on their own, in that case even an agent becomes a witness? Since the poskim do not require designating witnesses even ab initio, and did not worry that perhaps those who see will not intend to testify, so that he would be betrothing without witnesses, it follows that they hold that even a witness who merely sees becomes a witness. This is also explicit in Shulchan Aruch, Choshen Mishpat siman 36 seif 1, and it is from the Rambam. In my humble opinion, the Rambam's words are founded on his method in explaining the sugya in Makkot 6, where we ask all of them, as explained there in the Shach. It implies that the reason we need the inquiry whether they came merely to see is because one of them was found to be a relative; but with valid witnesses this is unnecessary. Even according to Rashi, whose implication is that we ask only the relative, the same is heard; otherwise Rava's question would make no sense, for this question would be necessary in every testimony. So too it appears from Responsa Rashba siman 1073, who wrote: witnesses need not be specially designated; anyone who saw or knew and testified, we accept from them, for testimony is established by seeing without knowing and by knowing without seeing, as with three who entered to visit a sick person, who may write if they wish, even though they were not designated to testify. This needs no proof, and even regarding kiddushin, which is ineffective without testimony, specially designated witnesses are not required. Since it was obvious to him that they need not be designated, he did not consider distinguishing between witnesses who independently intended to testify and those who came only to see. In my humble opinion this is also compelled from Shevuot 34b, where one said to his fellow: I counted a maneh for you at the side of this pillar, and Rav Nachman said he is established as a denier; Rava said to him, etc. When the witnesses saw that he had urinated, they certainly did not intend any testimony. How then did Rav Nachman think to accept their testimony and make him a denier through them? And why did Rava need to answer as he did? Necessarily there is no distinction. However, in Responsa Penei Moshe part 3 siman 25 he wrote: I am astonished how one can validate witnesses who did not intend to testify, for there is an explicit sugya in Keritot 12b: Abaye said, Rabbi Meir concedes to the Sages that if two said to him, do you know testimony for so-and-so, and he said I do not know, he is exempt, for if he wished he could say, I did not intend for testimony. It is explicit that if he said I did not intend, there is no substance to his testimony. In my humble opinion this is not explicit, for one can explain that he may excuse what he swore, that he did not know, because he did not focus on it and it was for him a matter not placed upon him, and he said not with awareness, as Shevuot rejects above. What Birkat HaZevach wrote against this interpretation does not even begin as a difficulty. Penei Moshe, however, brings support for his reading from Tosafot Yeshanim, s.v. I did not intend, which says: from here Rabbi said, and so ruled in practice, that witnesses who heard the matter from the principals but did not intend to testify and were not brought there as witnesses have no testimony if they later come to testify in bet din. Thus it is explicit that if they were not designated as witnesses and did not intend to testify, there is no substance to their testimony. How then did the Rambam rule that we rely on such witnesses, since they are not capable of giving testimony? Tosafot Yeshanim wrote there that we derive it from the verse and he is a witness, implying one who intends specifically. In truth, the Rosh and Rif did not bring this law. I am astonished that all the later authorities did not bring this, for it is an explicit undisputed sugya and requires study. End quote. According to what I wrote, the Rambam's ruling is compelled from the Talmud in two places. In my humble opinion, even according to Tosafot Yeshanim the halakhic ruling is correct, because in the two places it is compelled from Rava; and even if Abaye disagrees, the halakhah follows Rava against Abaye. Yet since they did not note Abaye's words at all, it seems to me that they did not explain them like Tosafot Yeshanim but as I explained, in order to minimize dispute. Still, so that the ruling of Tosafot Yeshanim not contradict all the poskim, I would press to explain it unlike Penei Moshe's understanding. Since the language says from here, etc., that witnesses who heard, etc., it does not sound like he speaks about an actual event; had that been so, he should have said, and so he ruled regarding witnesses, etc. Since he wrote witnesses who heard, it sounds as though he came to teach a law, and he should have taught it even regarding witnesses who saw an event. Rather, it seems that he ruled this specifically about witnesses by hearing. Perhaps it was in a case where a claim of joking or inducing an admission was relevant; alternatively, one may press to distinguish for some reason between witnesses by hearing, who require intent, and witnesses by seeing. He explains that the case of one who says do you know testimony means that they know it through hearing, so that the simple meaning is that if he did not intend for testimony he is not capable of giving testimony. What Tosafot Yeshanim wrote in the preceding comment, that we derive from and he is a witness that he must intend specifically, requires study in any case, for the verse also says or saw. According to the above one must press that he said it only as a support: and he is a witness refers to testimony by hearing, and or saw refers to an act. However, from the Responsa Rosh cited in Choshen Mishpat siman 51, where he wrote that the one witness who heard the will from outside joins with the other to testify even though they did not designate him, it implies that even with testimony by hearing, wherever joking or inducing an admission is not relevant, there is no difference whether he heard with intent to testify or heard incidentally, since he does not distinguish. The Beit Yosef already brought this in siman 36 as support for the Rambam's ruling. From all this, in my humble opinion, the words of Tosafot Yeshanim do not stand as halakhah unless they concern a case where joking is relevant. See Choshen Mishpat siman 81 seif 9; their words require study. Nevertheless, in my humble opinion it is proper ab initio to designate witnesses for kiddushin as for gittin. Aside from the above, the reason is that relatives usually stand present at kiddushin, and one must be concerned for what Ritva wrote in Kiddushin there in the name of his teacher, that regarding kiddushin there is reason to say that even if the relatives came merely to see, all the testimony is void; see there. His reasoning is exactly opposite the reasoning of Beit Shmuel s.k. 8. I later saw that according to the Rosh's approach there is no need for this, for although Ritva wrote that many disagree with his teacher, he concludes that one should be stringent for his view. Likewise in Makkot there he brings that it is an ancient custom to designate two witnesses for kiddushin, and writes that the reason is concern for Rashi's explanation, which is the first opinion in siman 36 cited above, that by intent alone, even without joining in testimony in bet din, all testimony is void when one of the relatives intended to testify. The Derishah there also concludes that it is proper to do so. The Shach there, s.k. 8, wrote simply that for us nowadays it is certainly valid, because we designate valid witnesses specifically for that purpose. Perhaps he refers to the custom brought by the Rama z"l in siman 31, that under the chuppah we ask the witnesses, etc.; this is like designating them as kiddushin witnesses. In my humble opinion, however, it is more proper to designate them explicitly to intend to testify about the essential kiddushin, and I believe this is the custom in Ashkenaz. What Beit Yosef, Sma, and Shach attribute there, that the Rambam's view follows Rashi and Rashbam and not the Rosh, at first glance requires study, for the Rambam's language, how do they examine the matter? When many witnesses come to bet din, one group says, etc., implies that he is particular also that they come to bet din as one group. The printed text places a verse break before one group and attaches one group says, but in my humble opinion this is a scribal error, for says refers to one of the judges, as explained in the Shulchan Aruch, and this requires study. Gloss: but, etc., until they see the giving. The Beit Shmuel wrote: see the Rama, etc., and in Choshen Mishpat siman 90, etc. With respect, he was not precise here. From there, in my humble opinion, there is proof to the opposite. The Rama did not write that in monetary law a matter is established by knowledge except in the case stated in Shevuot 34, by the admission of the litigant. This is not estimation, nor even near certainty, but actual certainty, because the admission of a litigant is like one hundred witnesses; in capital cases it is a Scriptural decree that a person is close to himself. In Choshen Mishpat siman 90, the Rama's law is learned from the case of one who was wounded, as stated in the Tur: just as we say regarding one who was wounded that if the matter is one by which he could have wounded himself, he takes without an oath, so too, etc.; this is testimony established by knowledge without sight. Thus he learned the law from the wounded person and based it on the verse or knew. On the contrary, the sugya in Shevuot makes clear that even according to Rav Acha, the case of the biting camel is called knowledge without sight, and we do not rule like him in that, as explained in Choshen Mishpat siman 408, but like the first tanna. In the wounded person and in the Rama's equivalent case, the first tanna agrees. It is therefore proven that even in monetary law we do not judge by knowledge without sight in the simple sense, meaning estimation based on the majority; that is Rav Acha's reasoning, as stated in Perek HaMokher Peirot. Rather, only an assessment like the wounded-person case, which is truly close to certainty, is accepted. In this, monetary law differs from capital law. This is exactly the view of Hagahot Maimoniyot and Rashba, who distinguish between near certainty, where she is doubtfully betrothed, such as where he threw money to her and it reached the airspace of the courtyard and we do not know whether it entered the partitions, where even though it is remotely possible that wind moved it, since it is close to certain she is doubtfully betrothed, and a case where he said he would betroth her and entered with the money in his hand with her into a room, which is not close to certain but only a strong inference like the camel; in such a case we are not concerned. The exception is kiddushin by intercourse, which is also close to certain because of the principle of fire near tow. Darkei Moshe further says that it is impossible to see like a brush in a tube; he means that the Torah's ways are ways of pleasantness, and presumably the Torah did not designate kiddushin by intercourse through literal sight, but through sight joined with knowledge close to certainty. Even Rashba in Responsa siman 1193 wrote only: furthermore, even if the etrog leaves her hand and she admits she received it from him for kiddushin, it does not help because there was no sight, but only something like knowledge. He means that without her admission, the sight of the etrog is only something like knowledge, not clear knowledge like the wounded person, but estimated knowledge: since he said see the etrog that I am giving, and then it emerges from her hand, ordinarily the estimate is that he gave it for kiddushin and she accepted it. Nevertheless, if she had claimed I accepted it as a gift, she would be believed; only through her admission does it become clear knowledge. Her admission does not matter, as he concludes, because with kiddushin it is effective only through witnesses whom she cannot contradict. See there and examine carefully.
