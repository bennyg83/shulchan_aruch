<b>Section 5, "among those disqualified for testimony."</b> One of the betrothal witnesses was the collector of the Sabbath candle payments collected nowadays in these countries. Elsewhere I wrote that one should not collect from those called "hidden," that is, people not listed in the government's population registers, and that it tends toward theft. Nevertheless, there is no concern about this witness, as in Choshen Mishpat 34:24-25, for he does not know of it; and even if informed, it is known that he will not comply. Besides, he thinks he is escaping danger to life from the army, and believes he acts for preservation of life. The holder of that collection originally leased it with the understanding that he would collect from the hidden, and no words would help him. Since this has spread throughout the entire country, no one will comply until it is corrected everywhere. The collector himself does not collect; a gentile goes with him. In any event, he is no worse than tax collectors.

Regarding a sexton about whom a rumor emerged that he misappropriated the candles he receives from the community to light in the synagogue and lights them in his house: as written in Responsa Mabit, section 51, there is no concern even by his own admission. So too with an uninterrupted rumor, even initially. Although the Rashba wrote that initially one should not join for testimony or extract money by a witness about whom rumor emerged, the Mabit wrote that this is not according to all. In such a case, even if there were witnesses to the theft, there would be grounds not to disqualify him, whether because it resembles those who bury the dead on the first festival day, or the tax collectors mentioned above, or because he thinks "the ox eats from its threshing," and that they gave with him in mind, and that it is money with no claimant or no owner. Still, this would require great analysis if there were an uninterrupted rumor. Here, however, it was not in the category of rumor; only one woman said so from a slight and inconclusive estimate. Even if she said so before several women, it is not a rumor, for we do not presume that the other women also speak so when we have not heard it. It is no rumor at all, and all agree that we do not establish aspersion against all testimony until now. There is no opening for objection here.

<b>(I also found)</b> Similar to what I wrote elsewhere regarding a custom that functions as an enactment, concerning a fixed town scribe for documents and ketubot where one witness does not know how to read: all the more a communal enactment helps here, that designated people serve as witnesses for all betrothals. It is as if they designated those witnesses alone for each betrothal. All the more the principle applies that whoever betroths does so according to rabbinic intent, and rabbinic intent is that the witnesses be valid and uniquely designated as witnesses. In such an enactment it is also possible that the cantor and sexton are designated as the sole witnesses. If one of them is a relative in a particular betrothal and another signs the ketubah, then the two witnesses signed on the ketubah are designated as the betrothal witnesses; that is the automatic form of the enactment. Nevertheless, one who states explicitly during the betrothal ceremony that the ketubah witnesses alone shall be the betrothal witnesses acts in the best manner explicitly. Even without that, however, it is so as stated above. The Nachalat Shiv'ah cites a custom to have two ketubah witnesses besides the two betrothal witnesses, and apparently there too specific people are designated for this and are always known as designated for that purpose.
