<b>בע"כ אינה מקודשת. </b> עבה"ט מ"ש ב' אומרים נתקדשה באונס כו' ועיין בס' שעה"מ פ"ז מה"א דין ב' שהביא תשו' מהרד"ב סי' מ"ו על אשה אחת שנתקדשה ושנים אומרים שנתקדשה מרצונה ושנים אומרים שנתקדשה באונס פחד וכתב דהיא מקודשת ודאי ולא תפסי קידושין מאחר משום דאיכא ס"ס להחמיר ספק אם האמת כדברי האומרים שנתקדשה לרצונה וא"ת נתקדשה באונס ספק אי חשיב אונס או לא (ר"ל כדאיתא בח"מ סי' ר"ה ס"ז בהגה) וכל דאיכא ס"ס להחמיר הו"ל מקודשת ודאית יעו"ש והוא ז"ל גמגם בזה ע"ש:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Against her will she is not betrothed.</b> See Ba'er Heitev, who discusses the case where two say she was betrothed under coercion, etc. See Sha'ar HaMelech, chapter 7 of Hilchot Ishut, law 2, who brings Responsa Maharadb, siman 46, regarding a woman who was betrothed and two witnesses say she was betrothed willingly, while two say she was betrothed under coercive fear. He wrote that she is certainly betrothed, and kiddushin from another man do not take effect, because there is a double doubt to be stringent: perhaps the truth is like those who say she was betrothed willingly; and even if she was betrothed under coercion, perhaps this is considered ones or perhaps not, meaning as in Choshen Mishpat, siman 205, seif 7 in the gloss. Wherever there is a double doubt to be stringent, she is considered certainly betrothed. See there. He, of blessed memory, questioned this. See there.

<b>And some say she is not betrothed.</b> See what I wrote below, siman 50, seif 6, s.k. 8, regarding the penalties and cherem made in shidduchim, and why they are not released before the kiddushin so that it not resemble coercion. See there.

See Responsa Chatam Sofer, siman 112, regarding one who wants to betray his betrothed, and the community leaders have the ability to force him through the city governor to enter the chuppah, whether there is concern here of kiddushin against his will. The Rav who asked inclined to permit, because the Chelkat Mechokek and Beit Shmuel challenged the Rama’s words in Choshen Mishpat, end of siman 205, that if one was coerced to buy he does not acquire. Thus the only remaining doubt is because they act improperly, whereas here, on the contrary, he is the one acting improperly, and it returns to the law of other coerced acquisitions, which are effective, unlike the Rama. He answered that in practice he also agrees to coerce him, and many such acts were already done. Although in his novellae to Bava Batra 48b he supported the Rama’s words in the name of the Ba'al HaItur, that one who is forced to take does not acquire, nevertheless this case is different. It is not comparable to one forced to take, because regarding a korban it is clear in Bava Batra that we compel him until he says, "I want," since he wants atonement. Tosafot there explain that whenever one is obligated to do the act, it is like a sale. Here too, since he obligated himself through cherem and penalty, and even if he pays the penalty he is not released from the cherem, and certainly in the case where he cannot pay, he plainly needs atonement, and the kiddushin are his atonement. Thus it is like a korban and everyone agrees that he may be coerced.

Still, according to this it should be no better than a korban itself, where he must at least say, "I want." In my humble opinion this is not a concern, as the Maggid Mishneh writes at the beginning of chapter 7 of Hilchot Ishut: whenever he performs the act, it stands, meaning that he need not specifically say "I want"; rather, as long as he performs the act we say he did it willingly. Chazal excluded only that one does not seize collateral for an olah as one does for a chatat or asham, but he must give or say "I want to give." So too, when one is pressured to betroth a woman, there is no greater act than saying, "You are hereby betrothed to me," and we attribute this to his will. This also removes the Lechem Mishneh’s astonishment at the Maggid Mishneh there, and so the Migdal Oz writes explicitly. Therefore even the Ra'avad may disagree only where the Rambam’s language is general, even where he did not say "you are hereby betrothed to me," but they were merely engaged in that matter. But when he performs an act showing "I want," it is clearly effective. Therefore he agrees that one may coerce someone to kiddushin in this case, and there is no concern. If the concern is that no peace will result between them, the truth is certainly with him, and he told the bearer that in his opinion it is better to separate those attached. End quote. See there.

<b>Which prove that she did not intend.</b> See Responsa Noda BiYehudah, beginning of siman 55, who writes that at first glance it is surprising why we should not believe her that she did not intend kiddushin. After all, one witness is believed regarding prohibitions even regarding his own matter, and we require it to be in his power only where a prohibition has already been established. Here no prohibition had been established, for we are discussing the beginning of the kiddushin, and on the contrary she stands in the presumption of being unmarried. In truth, Maharam’s reason is not that she is not believed; rather, she is believed, but we do not consider the intent, because the matter follows the spoken words, and even if the thought was otherwise, unspoken thoughts are not legally effective.

He discusses this at length and concludes that regarding credibility in a matter that is not against an established presumption, both the man who betroths and the woman betrothed are believed, even according to Beit Shmuel and Riva, who say above, siman 27, Beit Shmuel s.k. 5, that the woman does not have the power to say she did not understand. Perhaps there that is because the language is clear, so her claim that she did not understand is weak. But where there is no weakness, she is believed, because one cannot contradict the sugya that one witness is believed regarding prohibitions where no prohibition was established. From his words it appears that he assumes simply that where no prohibition was established, one witness is believed even in a matter of ervah, and this also appears to be his view in simanim 38 and 61, as I wrote below in siman 43, seif 1. However, in siman 54, brought below in siman 157, seif 4, s.k. 9, he is uncertain about this.

See Binat Adam, sha'ar Beit HaNashim, end of siman 29, who writes that Tosafot in Gittin 2 implies that even in a matter of ervah one witness is effective, and the Rashba’s novellae there imply this even more, and so the Ramban writes explicitly, as brought by the Ran in Perek HaMitkabel regarding one who tells his agent, "Go betroth a woman for me." But from the Ran’s novellae to Gittin it implies that in ervah it is not effective, and he writes so explicitly in Perek HaMitkabel, though his proof requires study. The Ran writes in two places that one witness is effective; see Beit Shmuel, siman 37, s.k. 45, and there seif 24 in the Rama, with Beit Shmuel s.k. 50, and so writes Maharit. The Nimukei Yosef in the name of the Ritva does not prove otherwise, for he speaks where a prohibition had already been established. Maharik, shoresh 72, writes explicitly that one witness in ervah, where no prohibition was established, is believed, and one may rely on him. See also Responsa Bigdei Kehunah, siman 18, and the later responsa cited there, as well as Responsa Brit Avraham, siman 82.

<b>He took the woman’s hand.</b> See Chelkat Mechokek, s.k. 2, who wondered why the Rama above, siman 28, seif 4, copied the words of the Ramah, while here he copied the words of Hagahot Mordechai. The Beit Shmuel, s.k. 3, answered that in the case of Hagahot Mordechai she initially wanted to become betrothed to him, and only afterward he took her hand by force, so we say she should have thrown the ring away, etc. See the Taz above, siman 30, s.k. 2, in the words of the annotator there.

See Responsa Chatam Sofer, siman 84, who writes that one who examines the source will see that the truth is with the Beit Shmuel and not with the annotator in the Taz. In his humble opinion there is another distinction: here, where he inserted the ring on her finger as jewelry, even if we say she did not throw it away because she thought she would be responsible for it, she should have removed it from her finger, guarded it in her hand, and covered it, rather than adorning herself with what was against her will. Therefore even if she had not consented initially, there is still concern. See also Biur HaGra and what he wrote further in siman 101, part of which was brought above, siman 28, s.k. 16.

He further wrote about the case there that the witnesses contradicted each other in the essence of the matter: one said he saw him insert it on her finger as jewelry, while the other said he saw them both holding it, then the man released it and left it in her hand, and it was never inserted on her finger. Although in that case there was no practical difference, nevertheless these are parts of the body of the act and sometimes affect the law. They are considered contradiction in the examinations and their testimony is removed completely. This matter was already stated in Maharit, part 2, Even HaEzer, siman 43, and Responsa Maharam Alshich, siman 104. In that case, although there were two witnesses disqualifying the first witness, one of them testified only based on his estimate that the witness had stolen his purse, and that is not testimony fit to remove a man from his presumption of validity. Therefore the witness remains valid, the witnesses contradict each other, and the woman is permitted. Since he found no other basis for leniency, he did not want others to act on his ruling unless the matter came before the leading Gaon of the generation. See Responsa Noda BiYehudah, siman 59, for several distinctions in this matter.

<b>If she contradicts the witness, there is no concern.</b> See Responsa Noda BiYehudah, siman 60, who concludes that this is the main law: one witness who is contradicted is certainly nothing. Although Responsa Maharam Padua, siman 32, was inclined to be stringent even in this, Chelkat Mechokek, s.k. 4, wrote that Maharam Padua himself retracted in siman 37. Although Chacham Tzvi, siman 115, wanted to say he had not retracted, one who sees Maharam Padua’s words in siman 37 will see that it was simple to him that one contradicted witness is nothing. The Gemara also proves this explicitly, that one need not be concerned for one witness who is contradicted, even according to the view that one is concerned for kiddushin in the presence of one witness. See there at length, and see Noda BiYehudah Tinyana, siman 76, Shivat Tzion, siman 77, and Sha'ar HaMelech, chapter 9 of Hilchot Ishut, law 51.

What Ba'er Heitev brings, that one distinguishes, etc., until the Chelkat Mechokek and Beit Shmuel disagree with him, see Responsa Bach, siman 37, Beit Meir, and Tumim, siman 61, s.k. 2. See Responsa Chatam Sofer, siman 104, who writes that the Beit Shmuel’s explanation of the Rashba, that if she says "I was joking" it is considered contradiction only where she says she explicitly said so at the time of kiddushin, is difficult in the Rashba’s language. From the Tashbetz it is explicit that the Rashba speaks even where she claims joking in intent, not explicitly; see Chatam Sofer, siman 99, who cites Tashbetz, part 1, siman 70. So too writes Beit Meir.

Regarding the Chelkat Mechokek’s main question on the Bach, that what difference is there between one witness and two witnesses regarding a claim of joking, since unspoken thoughts are not legally effective, it appears in my humble opinion that when the man who betroths claims he intended joking, this is indeed unspoken intent and is not effective. But when the woman claims joking, it is effective. The reason is that unspoken intent is ineffective only where the act itself does not point to the inner intent. Here, where a woman is betrothed before one witness, the logic leans that she intended joking, for what foolish woman would betroth herself to a man and forbid herself to everyone when the husband could deny the witness and be exempt, while she would be chained forever? Therefore the circumstances themselves indicate joking, and it is no longer merely unspoken thought. But for the man there is no such indication, because if she denies it he can marry another or divorce her. Thus his claim of joking is not effective. He also discusses a case of young people on Shabbat where one gave a ring to a girl and said kiddushin words. Since she denied hearing the kiddushin and said she thought it was play, and because there was only one witness who was contradicted, along with the fact that it occurred on Shabbat and in a playful setting, he permitted the man to her relatives. In siman 99 he discusses another one-witness case where the word "li" was not heard and concludes there was no concern of kiddushin. See what I wrote above, siman 27, s.k. 14.
