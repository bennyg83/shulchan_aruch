(ב) <b>However, regarding something that requires burning.</b> So wrote the Ran according to the view that anything standing to be burned is considered already burned; then it is as though the letters are flying in the air. R. Shimon and the Sages dispute this principle in several places. Rashi wrote in Menachot 102 that we hold like R. Shimon, who says that anything standing to be burned is considered burned. Chelkat Mechokek brought proof that Rambam holds the halakhah does not follow R. Shimon, for Rambam wrote in chapter 4 of Hilkhot Pesulei HaMukdashin that if it became known after the blood of a conditional guilt-offering was sprinkled that he had not sinned, the meat may be eaten. This implies that if it became known before sprinkling, it may not be eaten, which follows the Sages, who hold that what stands to be sprinkled is not considered sprinkled. One should wonder at him: how did he overlook the Gemara in Keritot 24, which establishes that case even according to R. Shimon? R. Shimon said his rule only regarding something standing for sprinkling, but this offering, if he did not sin, is not standing for sprinkling, and R. Shimon agrees. See Kesef Mishneh, chapter 3 of Pesulei HaMukdashin, law 19, where Rambam rules that a service vessel sanctifies disqualified blood to be offered, and he writes that Rambam rules like R. Yose there in Keritot, who holds that if it became known that he did not sin while the blood was in the cup, he should sprinkle it, because the service vessel sanctifies the disqualified item to be offered. This is difficult: if so, why did Rambam rule in chapter 4 that only if it became known after sprinkling may it be eaten, but not when the blood is in the cup? Rather, it appears that regarding a conditional guilt-offering Rambam does not hold like R. Yose for the above reason, because once it is known that he did not sin, it is chullin retroactively. But generally a service vessel sanctifies disqualified items where a disqualification occurred in the body of the offering, as Tosafot wrote explicitly in Keritot. According to this, Rambam holds that what stands to be sprinkled is considered sprinkled, and what stands to be burned is considered burned; a conditional guilt-offering is different, and a service vessel does not sanctify it. Regarding other disqualifications where the vessel sanctifies, one may say he learned this from the sugya in Zevachim 87, where R. Yochanan resolved that a service vessel sanctifies. Perhaps Rambam's distinction in chapter 3, that a service vessel sanctifies blood but not other items, is also learned from that sugya: in Zevachim R. Yochanan resolved that it sanctifies, while in Menachot 6 he resolved that it does not; therefore Rambam distinguishes between blood and other items. This resolves the difficulty of R. Peter in Tosafot there. Nor can one bring proof from Rambam at the beginning of chapter 3 of Me'ilah, where he writes that permission to the kohanim is called so only once the blood is sprinkled, while the sugya in Menachot implies that according to R. Shimon even before sprinkling it is called permitted to the kohanim because it stands for sprinkling. Tosafot in Me'ilah 5 wrote that the sugya holds that even according to R. Shimon, sanctity with respect to me'ilah is removed only after sprinkling. See Rambam, chapter 13 of Terumot, where it implies he rules like R. Shimon, and see the Rosh at the end of Perek Gid HaNasheh and Responsa Sha'ar Ephraim, folio 19.

(א) <b>With tattoo writing.</b> At first glance it is difficult: how can the witnesses sign with tattoo writing? They thereby become wicked; even if they wrote but did not fill it in with ink, nevertheless they become rabbinically wicked. Granted, if the scribe writes with tattoo writing there is no difficulty, for even if the scribe becomes wicked the Get is valid, as written in the preceding siman. But if the witnesses write with tattoo writing, they become wicked and the Get is invalid. See Rambam at the end of Hilkhot Avodah Zarah and Yoreh Deah siman 180. Perhaps the case is where they acted unwittingly; see Tosafot, Gittin 20. Chelkat Mechokek wrote that this applies specifically when the Get is written with tattoo writing and the witnesses also signed so. But if the witnesses signed with ordinary writing, it is as though the Get were written on an erased surface and the witnesses on paper, and it is invalid, because there is concern that one may come to forge it. One must say that although the Get is written with tattoo writing and cannot be forged, nevertheless there is concern that he will erase the signatures and forge and write something in the Get. However, this is difficult: why would we assume he will erase the signatures? If so, he could not authenticate the signatures. It is also difficult because the phrase according to the law of Moses and Israel is like firm and established, and forgery would be recognizable. I saw that his words are taken from Bach, and there Bach wrote the reason: if the Get is engraved with tattoo writing but the witnesses signed ordinarily, there is concern that from the outset the Get too was written ordinarily and he forged it. This is difficult: from where do we derive such a concern, since now there is no concern of forgery? It should be compared to what is written in Choshen Mishpat siman 45, that if a document ends in the middle of a line and the witnesses signed, we are not concerned for forgery. Therefore it appears that there is no concern in this. What the Tur wrote, that the witnesses were engraved, implies that the witnesses too must be engraved in tattoo writing. One may say this is because we decree signature because of writing, as Tosafot wrote at the beginning of Gittin, folio 4. But according to this, if the witnesses signed on an erased surface and the Get is on paper, it should be invalid for this reason without the reason that perhaps he will erase the Get; this does not imply so from the words of the poskim.
