The Beit Shmuel wrote: if a competent man came and married her, etc.; and in Sefer Beit Hillel he wrote, etc., that we do not penalize her. Regarding the law that if a competent man knowingly married her, the Beit Shmuel should not have copied the words of the Beit Hillel, for what does this have to do with a penalty? Rather, those words were said about the wife of a deaf-mute whose husband went overseas, and she married another man, and afterward the first husband returned. He wrote that according to this, what is stated in Perek HaIshah Rabbah, that she must leave both husbands and each gives her a get, and the reason is lest people say that this one divorced her and that one married her, applies specifically where the first kiddushin was Torah kiddushin. But where the kiddushin was rabbinic, she may return to him. He means that although the reason of lest people say is stated regarding why she needs a get from the second husband, he holds that this is also the reason she is forbidden to the first husband, even though she was coerced or mistaken: since she needs a get from the second, there is concern that people will say, he divorced her and then remarried his divorcee. So too the Beit Hillel derived in siman 159 from Rashi's explanation; see there. Therefore he discussed the wife of a deaf-mute, for even if people say so, according to Torah law she is permitted, and in truth we do not forbid her even if she married in error. He wrote further that the reason stated in the Gemara, that in the first clause, because she committed a prohibition, they penalized her, etc., means the prohibition of a married woman created by Torah kiddushin, but through rabbinic kiddushin they did not penalize her. End quote. It follows that where she married knowingly, perhaps he admits that she is forbidden for his reason of just as, etc. Perhaps his language is abbreviated. In any case, even his law of just as, etc., requires proof, for from where do we know that through rabbinic kiddushin she becomes forbidden to the adulterer? However, in my humble opinion both of them overlooked the Rambam's words in chapter 10 of Hilkhot Gerushin, halakhah 5. He wrote: the same law applies to a woman regarding whom witnesses came and said that her husband died, and she married, and afterward he came, whether he was competent or a deaf-mute whose kiddushin is not complete: she must leave both, needs a get from each, and is forbidden to both forever. This is from the Yerushalmi. However, regarding the wife of a deaf-mute who committed adultery, I found in the Mishneh LaMelekh, chapter 11 of Hilkhot Ishut, s.v. I saw fit to examine, that he wrote: if you say according to the view that the wife of a deaf-mute who committed adultery is permitted to her husband, as Rabbi Yechiel Bassan discussed at length in this doubt, bringing proof from the Maggid Mishneh, chapter 3 of Hilkhot Issurei Bi'ah, s.v. one who has relations with a minor girl, and from what the Tur wrote in siman 155, even if he did not have relations after she became an adult. He wrote that the reason is: since a yevamah, who is subject to a negative commandment, if she committed adultery is nevertheless permitted to the yavam, the bond of rabbinic kiddushin should not be stronger than the bond of Torah kiddushin, etc. It appears that his view is to compare her to a yevamah to the public: if she committed adultery she is permitted, but if she married another, whether inadvertently or intentionally, she is forbidden. The proof from the Maggid Mishneh is sound. But see Ritva at the beginning of Ketubot, who explains, in the sugya of the claim of virginity, the Rambam in chapter 2 of Hilkhot Sanhedrin: his reason is that he explains the Yerushalmi as referring to a minor who can perform mi'un; then, if she committed adultery, we say she does not want to be betrothed to her husband, and the adultery itself is the mi'un. According to this explanation, the opposite is proven: only when she can perform mi'un is she permitted, but if she grew up, even if he did not have relations with her and she is betrothed only rabbinically, nevertheless if she committed adultery she is forbidden; see there. In my humble opinion, his explanation is compelled by the language of the Rambam in halakhah 6. As for the proof from the Tur in siman 155, from his words that she is permitted to return to the first husband, see there: from the sugya from which the Tur derived his law there is no necessity, for one may say that when the Gemara asks, granted, etc., but if he betrothed through intercourse, what can be said, and answers that the rabbis acted, etc., it does not answer that therefore she is permitted to the first husband, but only that then she does not need a get from the second. Examine there carefully. If so, what the Mishneh LaMelekh decided, s.v. know that what we were forced to say above, that a competent kohen married to a deaf woman whose marriage is rabbinic is not forbidden to him as a zonah, although for a kohen coerced and willing relations are the same, nevertheless it is obvious to him that she does not become a zonah through a rabbinic prohibition, and his proof from chapter 18 of Hilkhot Issurei Bi'ah, namely that she does not become a zonah through prohibited relations with rabbinic relatives, can be rejected. That case is different, for one can decree lest it be confused with the wife of an ordinary kohen. And what he wrote, that our teacher explicitly ruled in chapter 2 of Hilkhot Sanhedrin and chapter 3 of Hilkhot Issurei Bi'ah that a minor capable of mi'un who committed adultery is not forbidden even to a kohen - according to the Ritva's explanation above, there is no proof from this at all, and the matter still requires study. See Chiddushei HaRashba, Yevamot 51b, at the end of s.v. that which Rabbi Shimon said, where he wrote: the Raavad answered that since he is a minor and has no complete marriage, she is not forbidden to him by adultery. This implies that according to Rashi, who explained in HaIshah Rabbah that the case was arranged so that she should not become forbidden to him, it speaks where she acted inadvertently. Although in HaIshah Rabbah 96b, s.v. harei zeh, Rashi himself explained that because a yevamah is bound to him, the rabbis treated it like ma'amar, and the Rashba there, s.v. matnitin, inferred that Rashi follows the view of Tosafot in Yevamot 68 and Kiddushin 19 that even a yevamah is not acquired to him by Torah law. Nevertheless he holds that, were it not inadvertent, she would have become forbidden to him through adultery; see there. So too Tosafot in Perek Raban Gamliel, 51b, wrote: one cannot say that those marriages are only rabbinic and they were not concerned to forbid her, for whatever the rabbis instituted, they instituted in the manner of Torah law.

Beit Shmuel wrote in the name of Darkei Moshe: and all the more so, etc. One should not ask: do we not hold like Rava, who says in Temurah that whenever the Torah says not to do something, if he did it, it is ineffective? For these are the words of Rashba, Kiddushin 66b: and if you ask, do we not hold like Rava, etc., Tosafot answered that since the verse revealed regarding a High Priest, he shall not take, one might have thought to derive from it to the prohibition he shall not take concerning a married woman, that if he did it, it is effective. If so, one can say that since we see that even with those liable to karet, the Gemara had to derive from another place that kiddushin does not take effect with forbidden relations, by implication with all other prohibitions we derive from a High Priest with a widow, and Rava agrees that it is effective. All the more so, since it is included in the Gemara’s derivation from when there shall be, etc., and is there a hated one before Heaven, etc., and the Merciful One said, when there shall be. Therefore the verse he shall not profane is needed in Temurah only for R. Akiva, and this is clear.

One who is half slave, etc. Chiddushei HaRashba wrote: one must examine this, for we say in Yevamot 45 that if one who is half slave had relations with a married woman, the child has no remedy. Necessarily, we must say his kiddushin is kiddushin, for if not, even he would not produce a mamzer according to the view that where a gentile or slave had relations with a Jewish woman, the child is fit. The Gemara explains the reason: we learn mamzer from the case of one's father's wife. Just as with one's father's wife, although he has no kiddushin with her, in general he does have kiddushin, excluding a gentile and slave, who have no kiddushin even in general. Therefore, if you say that for one who is half slave, etc., kiddushin does not take effect, then even when he has relations with an ordinary married woman the child is not a mamzer. There is another version there in the name of Rav Hai Gaon z"l: like one's father's wife, just as with a married woman whose offspring is attributed after him. Even according to this language, if you say his kiddushin is not kiddushin, his offspring is not attributed after him, as we say here, from where do we know he inherits? Rather, Rava here was merely rejecting, and the truth of the matter is that his kiddushin is kiddushin and his heirs inherit in the full sense. So Tosafot explained. End quote. In our Tosafot I did not find this. In Yevamot they wrote: and both derived it only from a married woman, etc. Some of my teachers wrote that according to that version it is not precise, for it is all the more so. I say that it can be reconciled, for the one who validates the child says as follows: one can only make the matter depend on whether kiddushin takes effect regarding one who has kiddushin generally; since he has kiddushin with others, and here he has none, it is evident that this is because of the severity of the forbidden relation, and it is for him like his father's wife. But regarding a gentile and slave, the fact that they have no kiddushin with a Jewish woman may not be because of the severity of the forbidden relation, but because they are not subject to kiddushin at all, even with one of their own kind. End quote. His words require explanation, for this is difficult: it is satisfactory regarding an unmarried woman, where one can say this; but where he has relations with a married woman, we see the severity of the prohibition in that even a Jew's kiddushin would not take effect. When a gentile or slave has relations with her, where did the severity of the forbidden relation go? At first glance one must say this is an external reasoning: the prohibition of a married woman was not stated with respect to them, so the severity of the forbidden relation is not found for them except in what we find, that kiddushin does not take effect at all, and this is true even with an unmarried woman and not because of the severity of the forbidden relation. If so, why was it difficult to him from one who is half slave? Granted that kiddushin does not take effect from his slave side, nevertheless from his free side at least the severity of the married-woman prohibition applies to him, and mamzerut properly applies. But here it implies that he first asked according to our version, and necessarily he retracted. In truth, what I wished to force, that the prohibition of a married woman does not apply to them, is not correct. It might be satisfactory for a gentile, though even this does not accord with the conclusion of the poskim who disagree with Rabbeinu Tam at the end of siman 178; but regarding a slave, why should it not apply by Torah law? He is obligated in all mitzvot in which a woman is obligated, and it is written, they shall be cut off, equating woman and man. So too it is explained in Niddah 47 in Tosafot, s.v. masar lehu. Therefore he holds that according to our version it is a scriptural decree: anyone who has no kiddushin in any manner, his children are not mamzerim, and he properly asks the question. See Sefer Atzei Arazim, who with difficulty resolves the question.
