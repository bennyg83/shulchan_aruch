From those forbidden by negative commandments. See Noda BiYehudah Tinyana siman 50. The questioner wanted to distinguish between kiddushin by money or document, which takes effect with women forbidden by a lav because the act of kiddushin alone involves no transgression, and kiddushin by intercourse, which should not take effect because the rule follows Rava that wherever the Torah says "do not do," if one did it the act is ineffective. Noda BiYehudah rejects this as mistaken. If so, the Talmud in Temurah could have found a simple practical difference between Abaye and Rava in one who betroths a woman forbidden by a lav through intercourse. Likewise, one who betroths by intercourse on Yom Kippur, or according to the view that intercourse with a virgin on Shabbat is forbidden because of wounding, would not effect kiddushin. This is untenable; there is no distinction between kiddushin by money, document, or intercourse.

The rule that a prohibited act is ineffective does not apply here. Shechitah performed on Shabbat or Yom Kippur is valid, because that rule applies only to an act that can never be done permissibly. The substance of kiddushin with a woman forbidden by a lav could have been done permissibly by money or document; he merely performed it through a forbidden method, so the kiddushin stands. Further arguments support this: Noda BiYehudah elsewhere writes that the Torah's verses concerning kiddushin show that kiddushin can take effect even when done contrary to a prohibition, as with a high priest betrothing a widow. R. Akiva Eiger also writes that the rule of ineffectiveness applies only where voiding the act would undo the transgression, whereas here the transgression of forbidden intercourse remains even if the kiddushin is void; this point may depend on the dispute between Rambam and Ra'avad about lashes for intercourse with women forbidden by lav without kiddushin.

The editor-son adds a proof from Ketubbot 74: although kiddushin by intercourse cannot be done through an agent, conditions nevertheless apply to it because the modes of kiddushin are compared to one another. Therefore, since kiddushin with women forbidden by a lav takes effect through money and document, it also takes effect through intercourse. Sha'ar HaMelekh writes likewise and answers Ran's statement that forbidden intercourse should not acquire: Ran said that only regarding rights such as inheritance and found property, lest the sinner profit, not regarding the validity of kiddushin itself. See also Tzla'ot HaBayit siman 4.

<b>An apostate Jew who betrothed.</b> See Noda BiYehudah Tinyana siman 162, concerning a troubling case in an Islamic country: a young man came to a community presumed to be an unmarried Jew, persuaded a widow to marry him, stayed three weeks, married her with chuppah and kiddushin, then took all she had and fled. It was then learned that he had been an apostate for years, leaving the woman in danger of permanent agunah. A rabbi suggested annulling the kiddushin from the outset. Noda BiYehudah responded forcefully that this must not be accepted. In that case, the man behaved as a Jew for three weeks and betrothed her as a Jew, so all would agree we must consider that he may have contemplated repentance; his later return to apostasy does not prove his state retroactively at the time of kiddushin.

Even when the apostate is presently before us as an apostate, no one takes account of the dissenting view cited by Beit Yosef in the name of Ba'al HaItur. The settled halakhah is that when an apostate Jew betroths, his kiddushin is valid, and Kenesset HaGedolah writes that even one who disagrees regarding yibbum concedes that his kiddushin is kiddushin. Nor can one argue that because she did not know he was an apostate, she did not betroth herself with that in mind. Even by a man, where the maxim that he is content with any wife does not apply, and even where the prohibition has no permitted remedy, all authorities agree that kiddushin to a woman forbidden by a lav whom he did not recognize nevertheless takes effect. All the more so for a woman, who is presumed content with any husband, and where the prohibition can be remedied because he can repent at any moment, the marriage is not a mistaken kiddushin. The conclusion is that she is a married woman and remains an agunah until she obtains a get from him.

<b>Or the first divorces her.</b> See Beit Shmuel s.k. 17, who writes that if two brothers betrothed her, one before emancipation and one after emancipation, and both died, she is permitted to the third brother. In the glosses of R. Akiva Eiger to Shulchan Arukh he notes that this law is explicit below at the end of siman 174.
