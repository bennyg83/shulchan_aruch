**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Who fed her husband.</b> See Baer Heitev, who wrote that if she vows and does not fulfill her vows and she is barren, etc. At first glance, according to what the Rash and the Baer Heitev write below in seif katan 7 in the name of Mahari himself, that in our time the matter requires very careful scrutiny, there is no practical difference in this doubt if she is barren, for even when she is not barren we do not rule in our time to remove her without a ketubah because of vows. This is also explicit in the rulings of Mahari there, where he wrote: at first glance the law is with her, for so Maharam inferred in Mordekhai where he violates the cherem, as Rama wrote in seif 3. However, one may distinguish, for here he can claim: had you not been negligent, a miracle might have occurred for me, as happened for Sarah our mother, peace be upon her; and so we find in Perek HaMekabel that one may say a miracle might have occurred for me. Still, it is not clear to me to distinguish this way. In any event, nowadays the matter requires very careful scrutiny, etc.; see there. One must say that Beit Shmuel copied this because it is relevant where it is known that the generation has become fit. See Responsa Chatam Sofer, Even HaEzer II, siman 171.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>That she served him while she was a niddah.</b> See Beit Meir above, siman 96, seif 12, who cites Responsa Panim Me'irot, part 2, siman 12, regarding a husband who claimed that his wife served him while she was a niddah. She admitted it, but claimed it was done with his knowledge. The ruling there was that the husband must swear that he did not know and that she caused him to stumble, and he divorces her without a ketubah. Beit Meir writes that this ruling is correct based on the Shulchan Aruch there, that where she is suspect, the opposing party swears and is exempt.

Although Choshen Mishpat, siman 92, seif 9, brings two opinions about such a case, and the Shach concludes primarily that she collects without an oath, this is not decisive. The main law appears to follow the Responsa Rashach cited by the Shach, that the possessor can say kim li like the first view. If so, here too the husband can say kim li. According to the Shach’s conclusion, however, a certain scholar answered well that she should take without an oath, and Beit Meir’s response from Choshen Mishpat does not appear comparable.

From Panim Me'irot it is at least clear that if she claims the husband also knew of the transgression, her claim has substance and she can make him swear on it. At first glance, Responsa Mahari HaLevi, brother of the Taz, siman 39, printed also in Teshuvot Geonei Batra'ei, siman 26, implies the opposite, that she cannot make such a claim unless she brings witnesses. He proves this from the Mordechai, Hagahot Maimoniyot, Hagahot Ashri, and Rashba, all of whom write that if she violated an oath and brings witnesses that he too violates it, he cannot remove her without ketubah; they all specify that she must bring witnesses. But one may distinguish: Mahari HaLevi speaks where witnesses testify that she transgressed; in that case she cannot claim he also transgressed unless she brings witnesses. Panim Me'irot speaks where there were no witnesses that she served him as a niddah, only her own admission; perhaps even Mahari HaLevi agrees that she can immediately claim he also knew, with a migo that she could have said she did not serve him as a niddah, and she can make him swear. This requires study. Noda BiYehudah Tinyana, siman 26, in a similar case, also makes this distinction.

<b>And that he ate it based on her statement.</b> See Chelkat Mechokek, who infers that witnesses are also needed that he ate it. Regarding serving him as a niddah, one must then force the explanation that he either admits it or witnesses found her serving with him. Beit Meir challenges this: according to the Ra'avad, who holds she is not believed against herself even to lose the ketubah, the case can be established only where witnesses found them, which is very forced. Furthermore, even according to those who disagree with the Ra'avad, the Ran writes that the Mishnah was not established as a case of admission because that is uncommon. If so, it is difficult to establish "serving him as a niddah" only through admission or the even more uncommon case that witnesses found them together.

Therefore Beit Meir writes that the Shulchan Aruch’s language is not exact. It is enough to have witnesses that she said, "I am pure," or "it is tithed," and then he is believed that he stumbled through her. Specifically where there are no witnesses at all she can claim she never said it; and even if she said it, before he ate she can say, as the Rosh writes, "had he wanted to eat, I would have separated him." But once there are witnesses that she told him, he is believed that he stumbled through her. What the Shulchan Aruch says, "if there are no witnesses and she denies that she fed him," means she denies that she said anything to him.

Responsa Mahari HaLevi initially also wanted to say that witnesses are needed only that she told him, and he brought proof from the Ran. Later he wrote that from the Tur’s wording, "and there are witnesses that she told him it was corrected and that he ate it," it implies the same by niddah: witnesses must testify that she said "I am pure" and that he had relations with her then. He rejects the proof from the Ran by saying the Mishnah could speak where he says he had relations with her then and she does not deny that, but says she was pure. He further writes that if there are witnesses that he had relations with her after she was established as niddah, but we do not know whether she said she was pure, it appears at first from the Tur that this is insufficient. Nevertheless, with niddah one can distinguish from untithed grain: with grain she can say she did not know she fed him or ask why he ate it; but with niddah, the husband relies on her and she knows herself, so witnesses that she said "I am pure" may not be required. She cannot simply say the husband knew too unless she brings witnesses, as above.

<b>And she is accustomed to this.</b> Ba'er Heitev writes that the Chelkat Mechokek and Beit Shmuel wondered: if they did not warn her, etc.; and if they warned her, etc. From Darkei Moshe, who explains the Rashba as speaking where they warned her, it implies that the Rama requires that she be accustomed even after warning. But Beit Shmuel, s.k. 11, writes that this is specifically in the Rashba’s case, where there was room to attribute the act to anger and quarrel; therefore even with warning it must be habitual, for then we do not attribute it to any external cause. Likewise below the Rama writes of a woman accustomed to seclusion, where one might attribute it to her business dealings. But where there is no such cause, even one time makes her one who violates Dat.

Beit Meir explains the Rashba simply based on Terumat HaDeshen, siman 242, who was uncertain whether habitual conduct perhaps does not require warning. To resolve the Rama, he writes that regarding loss of ketubah, everyone agrees it depends on warning, not on habit. But because the Rama later rules that one who violates Dat can be divorced against her will, and that may apply even without warning, he derives from the Rashba that without warning she is called a violator only if she is habituated. Thus after warning there is no distinction whether she is habituated. Responsa Rabbi Akiva Eiger, siman 101, writes that from many early authorities there is strong support for the Rama that if she is not habituated, there is no mitzvah and he cannot divorce her against her will. In siman 100, Beit Meir writes that two times is called habitual for this matter.

Beit Meir also disagrees with Mahari HaLevi, who concluded that if she is accustomed to violate Dat with witnesses, beit din compels divorce. Beit Meir writes that there is no coercion by beit din for one who violates Dat, even habitually and with witnesses; the difference is only that when she is habituated, even without warning he is obligated and commanded to divorce her against her will, while one time does not make her a violator except after warning. Noda BiYehudah’s case above, where she served as a niddah for over half a year, also concludes only that it is a mitzvah to divorce her and he may divorce her against her will, not that beit din compels, though that case involved her admission and not witnesses. Regarding cursing, it is reported in the name of the Gaon Maharach of Brisk that this applies specifically when she curses with a Divine name or substitute name, as in the laws of one who curses himself, his fellow, or his parents. The Talmudic phrase about a lion may itself be a Divine reference; this requires study.
