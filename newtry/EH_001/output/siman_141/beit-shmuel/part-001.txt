<b>She is doubtfully divorced.</b> According to the Maggid Mishneh, the doubt is whether this man can be transferred from being the husband’s agent for delivery, appointed by the husband, into becoming the woman’s agent for receipt, since the husband’s agency has not yet been completed. One should not ask why here she is doubtfully divorced while in siman 142, seif 13, it is not a get at all. Here, at the outset there was an agency fit to return to the husband; there, there was never an agency fit to report back that the agency had been completed.

**** ENGLISH ****
(ד) <b>And some say that the woman cannot, etc.</b> In the Talmud two reasons are explained. The first reason is concern for the husband's disgrace if she herself does not receive the get; according to this there is a Torah-level concern. In the responsum of Rashba cited by Beit Yosef at the end of the siman, it is likewise explained that the husband may not appoint an agent to give the get into the hand of the woman's receiving agent because of the woman's disgrace, when he himself does not wish to give it into her agent's hand. What the Rav wrote in the gloss, that if she said to receive it from her husband one may not receive it from his agent, must mean that when she said to receive it from her husband there is insistence according to everyone, even according to the view that is not concerned for disgrace. But according to Rav, who is concerned for disgrace, even if she did not say to receive it from her husband, one may not receive it from his agent. So wrote Taz. The second reason is that it resembles a courtyard that comes afterward, meaning as if he placed it in another's courtyard and she then acquired that courtyard; according to this, there is only a rabbinic concern. There are also practical differences: if the husband is not particular, then according to the reason of disgrace there is no objection. If she appointed her agent at the outset, then the concern of a courtyard that comes afterward does not apply, but the concern of disgrace still applies, even though she had appointed the agent beforehand. If she was preoccupied when the get was brought to her and because of that preoccupation appointed an agent for receipt, some say that in such a case there is no concern for the husband's disgrace. Ran wrote that even in such a case there is concern for the husband's disgrace. Responsa Rivash, siman 55, wrote that the essential law is that a woman can appoint an agent; therefore, where there is some concern of prohibition if he sends it by an agent to her hand, it is preferable that she appoint a receiving agent to receive it from his agent.

<b>מצטרף עם ע"א</b>. הן לענין הקבלה מצטרף הוא ודי אם קיבל הגט לפני ע"א והוי כאלו קיבל בפני שנים והן להעיד על שליחתו שהוא ש"ק די אם ע"א מעיד על שליחתו כ"כ בדרישה וב"ח וכן בש"ה דאי אם ממנה אותו לפני ע"א כי מצטרפים השליח ג"כ עם העד וכן בשעה שמוסר הגט ליד האשה די אם מוסר הגט לפני ע"א והחולקים הוא הרא"ש וס"ל השליח הוא במקום הבעל והאשה לכן אין מצטרפים אותו כלל לא להעיד על שליחתו ולא בעת שמוסר לה הגט וצריכים שני עדים בעת כשממנה אותו וכן בעת המסירה אבל מ"מ נאמן הוא לומר שעשה אותו לשליח בפני עדים כמ"ש בסמוך ואם שנים הם שלוחים לכ"ע נעשו עדים והיינו דתניא הן הן שלוחין והן הן עדים דרישה וב"ח:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>He joins with one witness.</b> Regarding receipt, the agent himself joins with one witness, and it is enough if he received the get before one witness; it is as though he received it before two. Likewise, to testify about his agency as an agent for receipt, one witness is enough because the agent joins with him. So wrote the Derishah, Bach, and Shiltei HaGibborim. The dissenting view is the Rosh, who holds that the agent stands in place of the husband and the wife; therefore he does not join at all, neither to testify about his appointment nor at the time he delivers the get to her. According to that view two witnesses are required both when she appoints him and when he gives her the get. Nevertheless, he is believed to say that she appointed him as agent before witnesses, as written nearby. If there are two agents, according to everyone they become witnesses, and this is the meaning of the baraita: they themselves are the agents and they themselves are the witnesses.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>He does not need witnesses of receipt.</b> Meaning, he does not need witnesses who saw him receive the get, but he does need witnesses to her statement appointing him as her agent for receipt. So wrote the Beit Yosef and Kesef Mishneh. Although he is a third party, since the get is in his hand, nevertheless for her to be divorced immediately upon his receiving it we require witnesses that he is her agent for receipt. If there are witnesses that he is her agent for receipt, then even if the husband did not know he was such an agent, we say that the husband presumably delivered it to him so that she should be divorced immediately if he is indeed her agent. When there are no witnesses that he is an agent for receipt, we do not say that she should at least be divorced from the day he arrived with the get by a migo that he could have given it to her, because perhaps he does not want to receive the get when he does not know that the husband gave it to him to give to her. This is implied by the Rosh. The Beit Shmuel then discusses the apparent contradiction between this law and later laws about whether the husband can contradict the agent, and concludes that this case must be speaking of an agent bringing a get from place to place, where the agent is believed; but in one city, even if the husband is not present, we are concerned that he may come and contradict him.

(ב) <b>And if the sender denies, etc.</b> Necessarily one must say that the sender denies and says, I gave it to you as a deposit, while the agent says it was for divorce. For if he says, it fell from me and you found it, the Maggid Mishneh wrote in the name of Rashba that he is not believed. If so, it is difficult: when they are not in one city, why can he contradict him? According to Rif's approach, as written in the responsa and in Rashba, the sugya speaks of an agent for delivery, and according to all opinions when they are not in one city the agent is believed; yet here he wrote plainly that he can contradict him. One should not say that here it speaks where the Get is not in the agent's hand, and therefore he can contradict him, because then it is difficult: where is the Get? If the Get is in her hand, the Mechaber wrote below that when the Get is in the woman's hand it is as though it were in the agent's hand. If the Get was torn, then even if the sender admits he gave it for divorce, she is in doubt, as written below. Nor should one say that the sugya speaks where it is known that he is her agent for delivery, and likewise below, and only there do we say he cannot contradict him unless the reasoning assists him, namely, if it was for divorce he should have given it into her hand; therefore when they are not in one city he cannot contradict him. But if we do not know at all whether he is his agent, he can contradict him. For in any event he is a third party holding the matter, so why can he contradict him and say he did not give it for divorce? Nor should one say that it speaks where he says it fell from me and you found it, and he is not a third party at all; for although Rashba holds he is not believed to say it fell from me, Hagahot Maimoniyot does not hold so, but follows the Rosh, who holds that he may say it fell from me, as written in the responsum cited by Beit Yosef in Choshen Mishpat siman 55. What the Tur wrote below in siman 152, that when the Get is in the woman's hand the husband cannot say it fell from me, may be because a Get in the woman's hand is different. And what the Tur wrote there in Choshen Mishpat, that if he says to the third party, you stole it from me, he is not believed, may be because a claim of theft is different, but he is believed to say it fell from me. Yet Hagahot Maimoniyot wrote this law, and wrote there that the husband can contradict him when the Get is not in the agent's hand, as written nearby. If he held like the Rosh, it would be understandable; even when the Get is in the agent's hand he could also contradict him and say it fell from me. Rather, necessarily he does not hold like the Rosh. I also wrote nearby that it is proven not like the Rosh, who is concerned for falling, and not like the poskim who hold that he is believed to say I gave it as a loan. Perhaps it speaks where the Get is not in the agent's hand and not in the woman's hand, but the agent delivered the Get to her before witnesses of delivery, the witnesses are before us, and the Get was torn. Therefore, if the husband admits that he is his agent, she is divorced; but if he denies him, even though they saw the Get in his hand, it does not help. This is as written below in the gloss, that what they saw the Get in the agent's hand does not help. Necessarily one must say that when he delivered the Get to her, he did not say that he was the husband's agent; for if he had said so at that time while the Get was in his hand, the agency was in his hand and he would have been believed when he said he was an agent for delivery, and the husband could no longer contradict him, as written there in Choshen Mishpat. Hagahot Maimoniyot wrote that the husband can contradict him because the third-party holding left his hand; this implies that if the Get is in the agent's hand, he cannot contradict him. At first glance this is difficult: if the Get is in the agent's hand, why can he not contradict him? The husband has a miggo that he could object and tell him not to deliver the Get to her; if so, why is he not believed to say he gave it as a deposit with a miggo that he could object? One must answer that a third party is believed even where there is a miggo to nullify the matter. So too it is implied from what is written in Choshen Mishpat, that the third party is believed like two witnesses; if so, it is like a miggo against witnesses.

<b>However, the agent is believed, etc.</b> So wrote Ri"v, and the Rosh also wrote at the beginning of Gittin that even if the husband contradicts him, the agent is believed. This cannot mean that the husband says the get fell from him, for the Rosh holds he can say that, as written nearby. Rather it speaks where the husband says he gave it as a deposit. Darkei Moshe explains that the agent is believed when the get is in his hand. If the get is not in the agent’s hand, the Rama’s words would be difficult, for why should he be believed and why are we not concerned that the husband will come and contradict him? Therefore the Rama must be speaking where the get is in the agent’s hand, even though the Mechaber speaks where it is not. It must also be speaking of an agent for delivery from place to place; otherwise the husband could contradict him. The Rosh and Ri"v appear to hold that when an agent for delivery brings a get, he cannot be contradicted even in the same city, because since he is believed to say "it was written and signed before me," he is believed also to say he is an agent for delivery. However, the Tur and Beit Yosef do not imply this. From the Rosh it further seems that this credibility applies specifically where he must say "it was written and signed before me." In Eretz Yisrael, where that declaration is unnecessary, he is not believed to say he was appointed agent for delivery, though Ramah holds he is believed even there. The Rambam also appears to hold like Ramah, as Rivash explains, that the agent is believed to say he is the husband’s agent even where the get is verified and he does not say "before me." All this concerns an agent for delivery; an agent for receipt is not believed to say he was appointed if the get is not in his hand.

(א) <b>Immediately, etc.</b> At first glance it implies that just as we do not require immediacy when it is known that he killed himself, and so too it appears from the Tosefta, which states regarding a doubt whether he killed himself: if he fell immediately, I say he fell of his own accord; if he fell after some time, we do not write, lest the wind pushed him. This implies that if it is known that he fell of his own accord, we are not concerned that at that time he did not intend to kill himself. One can say that the Tosefta teaches that even in a doubtful case, if he fell immediately, it is a get. It also follows from the Tosefta that even in a doubtful case where he did not fall immediately, it is nevertheless a doubtful get, as it says there that we do not write because perhaps the wind pushed him; this implies it has not left the realm of doubt. We do not say there is a double doubt: perhaps the wind pushed him, and even if he killed himself, perhaps at that time he did not intend to kill himself. Therefore the Mechaber ruled in the next seif that where there is doubt and he did not fall immediately, it is a safek get.
