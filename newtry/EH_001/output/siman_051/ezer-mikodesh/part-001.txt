<b>In the gloss, "new conditions."</b> The documents customarily used at marriages, namely later tena'im documents, a half-male document, and a chalitzah document: if one delivered a declaration of compulsion before accepting them by symbolic acquisition, it requires analysis whether they are treated like a gift, for which we do not require that the coercion be known to us, or like a sale. I have not now examined this at all. It appears to incline toward sale, since the marriage is completed only on the basis of these writings. Moreover, sometimes through great obstinacy people cause changes in these writings, or they omit them from being written at all, and the marriage proceeds without a symbolic acquisition over these writings. Nevertheless, since most people are particular about them, and only through them is the match completed, it is like a sale.

It goes without saying with a half-male document, which stands opposite the chalitzah document; but even with the tena'im, even where the obligation is only on one side, it nevertheless has the character of a real sale. See Nachalat Shiv'ah regarding writing cancellation of declarations in documents, even with an unspecified symbolic acquisition. This requires some analysis.

Some make changes regarding later tena'im, afterward evading payment through stratagems; and some make stratagems regarding depositing the dowry. There is a slight defense for this from the language of the Sages, "free your slave and give her to him." According to several views, freeing a slave involves an aspect of violating a positive commandment even when it is not done only for the benefit of the emancipator, and so it also sounds from the case of freeing a slave to complete a quorum. Yet this is no proof at all, for it is forbidden to save oneself from death with another's money, as the Sages said in Bava Kamma regarding lentils or barley, "the warriors broke through," etc. All the more so here, where even regarding a maidservant with whom a prohibition had been committed, there is discussion of how the emancipation worked; how much more with an adult woman presumed fit. One may say that they did not mean specifically "free your slave," but spoke in an exaggerated manner for those who hold that emancipation is always forbidden.

Even if in that case the prohibition of emancipation was permitted, just as one may sell a holy Torah scroll in order to marry off a poor orphan girl, this has no bearing on deceiving people's minds in monetary matters, for deception is equivalent to the prohibition of theft and robbery, as at the beginning of the chapter Eizehu Neshekh. Only when one acts innocently, trusting in the Blessed Name that He will provide the means to pay everything he promises in the later or earlier tena'im, do we hold that no prohibition applies to postponing payment, as I wrote elsewhere in the name of the Sema, section 339. Even though it is money he did not borrow, and the prohibition did not come into his hand, nevertheless, even if through some stratagem of his it cannot be collected in court, since in truth it would have been collectible in court by virtue of his obligation, anything collectible in court is treated as theft or robbery, as above.

With every evasion, the court cannot distinguish the matter; it is entrusted to the heart whether he evades in order to pay. Usually the court can arrange the affairs of a debtor, and his evasion does not help him even when he intends to pay afterward. Once the matter comes before judges and they rule that he must pay immediately, if he does not fulfill the judgment of the court, it appears that even the Sema would agree that he may not evade in order to pay later. This requires analysis, and there are many details in it. In any event, there is some ground of merit for one who extends the time with intent to pay when the couple demand, when he will have the money, and in the end, whether he has it and pays or does not later have what to pay, there is a ground of merit for him, since he went innocently, trusting in the Blessed Name.

What I wrote above regarding changes made in dowries: some offer excuses, saying that it is not proper to marry a woman for money, and that books of the God-fearing say not to quarrel over dowry and clothing. They therefore think they are acting under "with the crooked You show Yourself subtle," and that there is no issue of taking money that is not theirs, since people impose upon him a heavy burden of mohar and gifts because they look to money. Similar excuses are offered, including that most of the time the other side later waives it. Nevertheless, one who guards his soul should distance himself from this and anything similar.

I wrote elsewhere that among the people of Israel only a good thought alone is joined to deed; and in truth, because of "from being good," etc., all Israel, and at least the overwhelming majority of those who marry according to money, intend the matter as "in the shadow of wisdom, in the shadow of money." It is therefore like marrying only for the sake of Heaven. If so, Heaven forbid that one engage in any fraud or overreaching in this. Those who are lenient in their own minds rely on a double doubt: perhaps his intent is only for gain, and the fixed fact that worldly people do so creates an evenly balanced doubt; perhaps his circumstances will broaden and he will pay, with further excuses of this kind.

Through this, the quality of "kindness and truth have met" comes into opposition. The world is built on kindness, in the love of women in the form of marriage, all of which is the love of kindness; yet various evasions in words have become customary, until people can scarcely manage without falsehood. This creates opposition to peace. Even the saying that there is no ketubah without some quarrel is only from the side of enmity, "I will place enmity," etc., beginning in separation and ending in union. But when people twist their ways through deceit, there is opposition to peace, for peace kisses and mediates between kindness and truth so that they become united in its hand without deceit.

Through righteousness, which is a measure of piety, one does not join love of gain to love of the mitzvah, but knows clearly that love of the mitzvot is primary for him, and that "if a man would give all the wealth of his house for love, they would utterly despise him." He makes the primary thing primary and the secondary thing secondary. Accordingly, matchmakers may use verbal turns in matters that are destined to become known, and no final loss is caused by the temporary concealment; rather, through many friends they teach him not to make the secondary primary. Thus righteousness kisses and mediates. Through this, righteousness and kindness, even where there are verbal reversals, become a path toward the way of truth. When "truth springs from the earth," and righteousness is observed by the Blessed Name, who examines the innermost thoughts and sees that one makes the primary thing primary in the way of righteousness, then there is no deceit in the visible conduct of kindness. Then "the Lord too will give the good": they will beget a holy soul, and the portion of the Blessed Name, the ten things He gives in birth, will be on the best side; "our land will give its produce," and by being saved from "one who boasts of a false gift," there will be abundance in the world in the best way.

Regarding mezonot at his table, where the custom is to obligate oneself toward the couple of his children: the Urim, section 60, wrote that he must give her at the time of childbirth the expensive foods customarily given then, in the name of Shevut Yaakov. It seems to me that in these countries the practice is to give only for the first birth. If in some places the custom is known that they give only for one birth, plainly there is nothing beyond the custom. But where no specific custom is known, it appears that one cannot exclude this from his obligation to support them during that period. Even though we hold there that he is obligated only in foods for the time of their health, the reason is that most women are ordinarily in proper health, whereas regarding childbirth most women become pregnant and give birth, as the Sages said. Even though in monetary law we do not follow the majority, this is merely clarification of the matter: people accept obligations according to the ordinary pattern of most people. Likewise, regarding the kind of foods and the blessing of the household, it is simple according to all that we decide by the ordinary pattern of most people. So too here. The holy book Shevut Yaakov is not before me now to examine it, but apparently his reasoning follows this style. Accordingly, the same would apply even to several consecutive births, insofar as that accords with the ordinary pattern of most women; but if one woman is found to give birth every year, this requires analysis.

Likewise, regarding support of the child, whether it is imposed upon the one who obligated himself to feed the couple, the matter is also decided by custom, for we have not heard of anyone refusing to feed his grandchild during the time he gives mezonot to his children, the couple. Perhaps there is no fixed custom in this, for when milk and bread are bought for the children and they are not in the house, perhaps the couple are told to buy it for their children from their own funds. But after the fact, if he already fed the children as well and then wishes to claim payment for it, or perhaps even when he wishes to deduct it from what he owes the couple, it appears possible to decide by custom that we have not heard of anyone deducting or claiming for this. This requires analysis.

When there is conflict between the couple and he feeds only one of them for a time, the calculation follows the number of persons, as in Choshen Mishpat 60; see also Even HaEzer 114. If the one because of whom the conflict occurred, or the one who was able to object, claims that now that the conflict has ended, the mezonot should replace the earlier period, and meanwhile the price of food has risen or fallen, or there is a difference because the children have grown and he fed them as above, it appears that the phrase written in documents, "consecutive years," is for the benefit of the couple, so that he cannot postpone them to a later time. Although we hold that "Passover" without specification means the first Passover, for otherwise the document or condition would be entirely void, people have already become accustomed to write even superfluous language in documents to clarify for those who err. But with respect to the recipients, it appears that the choice is theirs to delay and postpone receiving until the time they wish. Perhaps, however, just as payment against a creditor's will is called payment, so too here he may advance it as he wishes. This requires analysis.

One man gave his daughter, before they bought her what is called a shterin tikhel, his own shterin tikhel. She wore it for one year, as had been agreed between them, and then for an additional year beyond the agreement. He afterward said to her that if she did not pay him such-and-such a sum, it would become hers absolutely at a certain time, and then he retracted. On a quick reading, I have not presently seen regarding movables that pulling or lifting before him should help even without his saying, "take possession and acquire," as we hold regarding land. Even with land, where possession before him helps, the Sema explains that the reason is because his failure to protest reveals his intent. In the case above, that does not apply, for even without sale or conveyance we saw that he did not protest during the second year. Although there is room to say that he should have protested, since her lifting was certainly with intent according to the first condition, and we hold that anyone who acts does so according to the first understanding, and therefore he should have protested against her now taking more than before, nevertheless one can say that he knew that without his saying "pull and acquire," there is no conveyance through her pulling or lifting.

The obligation to protest is only from the presumption that a person does not see his property being taken and remain silent, like the presumption that a person does not throw away his money for nothing. Here, however, it is known that he was not particular; it is like cases where they have no presumption against one another with respect to land because they are not particular with one another, such as a son with his father's property. This requires analysis.

In the above case, after two years, the daughter lent money to her father, money that she had for him to buy her a shterin tikhel, and then there was discussion of the fixed time. It appears simple that one cannot say that his failure to protest proves conveyance, for the lifting does not prove acquisition; if there is no conveyance, the debt remains in force, and until it is paid she wears the shterin tikhel according to their agreement.

Yet because it would have been proper for them to make a heter between themselves, on account of "he shall not hire from him for less," even where the object does not stand to be rented and he does not usually rent, and all the more since, had he paid the money prepared for buying a shterin tikhel, the father presumably would not from then on have left his own shterin tikhel in his daughter's hand, whether the shterin tikhel stood to be sold, or his wife stood to adorn herself with it, or it stood so that he could borrow money against it as collateral while they used it for adornment meanwhile by way of a heter, or because it would have been easier for him to borrow against a pledge. Since they erred in this and did not make a heter accordingly, one could say from the presumption that a person does not leave the permitted and eat the forbidden that he presumably preferred the lifting to operate as acquisition, so that from then on there would be at least no trace of prohibition.

But perhaps he could deduct for the adornment, and then there is no prohibition. On the contrary, if he fixes it in the amount of the loan, he will not later be able to deduct for the adornment and say that he would have taken less than this, with part included for the adornment. It is difficult to say so where the discussion was only in the form of a purchase at this value. Even if his daughter may later pay him for the adornment, there is reason to say that we should not assume their intent was by way of "buy and sell and be called a merchant"; it would have been better for him to deduct immediately. All the more so since consumed theft is severe, and all the more where the prohibition is committed at the time of conveyance, apart from charging for what is not proper then. Even if they intended to deduct afterward, perhaps that does not help.

Still, reason inclines that he could not have relied on his daughter deducting it for him, since they did not discuss this at the outset. It also appears that the presumption that one does not leave the permitted does not apply here, since he had no thought to ask about it and would have said it was permitted, especially now that a heter iska is easy to make and people have no concern about it, and he could have made one himself, but thought it entirely permitted.

If a document written by a father to his son contains an obligation after the mezonot to give him something, and there is a separate writing stating that the duration of the mezonot is two years, and the couple does not live with him but he pays them according to the known "blessing of the household" rate, it appears that the time for payment of the document is immediately when they are no longer supported by him. Even though the mezonot document was certainly written first and close to this document, this is like the distinction between the wording "a year" and "months" and the like, where we interpret laymen's language. Since he could have said "months" and said "year," he lost the intercalary month. So too here: since he could have mentioned "at the end of two years" and mentioned only "at the end of the mezonot," the boundary is only according to the time they are actually fed there.

There is also a reason for this from common practice: so long as they are in his house and fed there, the arrangement is to wait with money and money's worth. But when they depart from that house, or even when they are fed there from their own means, which is in their eyes like living at a distance, the situation changes. Even if it is known that the town scribe wrote the language on his own, and it is possible that at the time of the symbolic acquisition the language was in another style, nevertheless it is relevant here that once he has testified he cannot return and testify again. We judge that the language at the time of the symbolic acquisition was like the document, or at least that it was unspecified or ambiguous in both directions, and he relied on it being as the scribe would write, as is customary with ordinary signatures; see Choshen Mishpat 48. The same applies to standard document forms, even when the parties to the symbolic acquisition know nothing at all about clauses of trustworthiness and many powers of attorney. Their ordinary intent is only that it be written according to the scribe's will in the best manner.

Regarding the custom to give mezonot to a couple after the wedding: it appears to have something of the form of Yissachar and Zevulun, since support prevents "a millstone around his neck" and thereby he studies well. I wrote elsewhere that it requires analysis whether the couple acquires the dowry that is deposited. If they do not acquire it until after the marriage, there is an element of charity in the dowry given, as written in the name of Mahari Weil, that what one promises to give his children for their marriages is like a vow of charity, since at the time of the match the couple does not have two hundred zuz. So too where the custom is to pay the dowry little by little: to satisfy the possible obligation, perhaps the measure of two hundred zuz is specific and not according to the amount needed for livelihood over time. It is proper that the money not be conveyed to the couple until the marriage, and then everything is acquired at once, for even if one hundred people give one thousand zuz at once, all of it is charity.

By this, the clothing and movable property given to the couple at the time of marriage also have the status of charity, because they acquire everything at once, or the clothing before the money. Clothing is not counted toward the two hundred zuz, and jewelry, when it is customarily worn also on weekdays, is light with respect to the calculation. In matches where the custom is that a wealthy person prepares the wedding provisions for each member of the couple, both fathers, the bride's father and the groom's father, can nevertheless fulfill charity through the preparations, by having the wealthy one convey to his in-law the movable property he prepares for him. Charity applies to that conveyance, when the in-law's receipt qualifies as charity; afterward, when the in-law conveys the movable property to the couple, he too fulfills charity thereby. Perhaps the ordinary assumption is that they act so that matters will be as proper as possible, according to correct wisdom; because of honor they act without specification, but it is agreeable to them that the possible good not be prevented from taking effect through the unspecified arrangement.

With mezonot given to the couple, if all parties wanted the money not to be acquired by the couple until after the period of mezonot, the mezonot too would, according to all, have an aspect of charity. But it is uncommon that they would agree to this. Immediately after the marriage it must be known that the money belongs to the couple for purposes of disputes that sometimes arise between them. In any event, what the Sages said, that one who feeds his children is in the category of one who performs charity at all times, applies only under the condition written in Shulchan Aruch on this matter, and the essential point is only when it is for the sake of a mitzvah. Therefore, even when the couple acquires the money, if the intent of the mezonot is for the sake of study, and even when the couple's own money is abundant and sufficient to support themselves, since learning requires prayer, and there is rescue here from "a millstone around his neck" and from "the one occupied with it will be praised," there is a mitzvah in giving the mezonot, and it is support of the holy Torah. The form of Yissachar and Zevulun applies even when both are wealthy, whenever it saves the learner from burdens and business concerns, through concern that the principal not be consumed. So too, it is a form of support and assistance, greatly increasing the good.
