And in the Tur it is written here to permit, and the reason is that it is a davar she'eino mitkaven. And that which they forbade it in the Mishnah is according to Rabbi Yehudah, who holds that a davar she'eino mitkaven is forbidden; but we hold like Rabbi Shimon, who holds it is permitted. And it is similar to sellers of garments nearby, which is permitted. And in siman 300 at the end, the Beit Yosef brought the words of Rabbeinu Yerucham, who asked on what the Rosh wrote there, that it is forbidden to wrap kilayim on his hand when driving the animals — for this is a davar she'eino mitkaven like here. And it appears to me to answer: even though we conclude in Perek Kol Sha'ah (Pesachim daf 26) that according to Rabbi Shimon it is permitted when it is possible to separate from the issur, and he does not separate, except that he does not intend to benefit from it, and they bring a proof from this of sellers of clothing here, that it is possible to do like the modest who sling it over, etc., and he does not do so, and even so it is permitted — nevertheless, that is only where the issur is already done, except that this one does not separate from it for the time being; but certainly he should not make an issur of kilayim to put it on always on the reliance that he will not intend to wear it except for driving the animals, since it is made for this forever, this is the main putting-on of it, and it is called a manner of wearing. This is what appears to him very correct from the side of reason and from the side of the proof. And many wearied themselves to find an answer to this difficulty, and what appears to my humble opinion clear I have written according to the view of the Tur.

<b>And in rain, because of the rain, and so forth.</b> Rambam writes first, regarding tailors carrying garments, essentially the wording cited here. Regarding clothing merchants, however, he says: provided that in the sun they do not intend that the kilayim garments on their shoulders protect them from the sun, and in the rain they do not intend to warm themselves with them. It is surprising why Rambam does not use the same wording in both places. It is also surprising why in the latter case he changes from "protection from the sun" to "warming in the rain" rather than saying "protection from the rain." It appears he speaks in the ordinary manner. By strict law there is no distinction; intentionally protecting oneself from rain is also prohibited. But when a clothing merchant carries a garment on his shoulder, it is not natural to say he protects himself from rain merely by putting cloth over his shoulder while the rest of his body becomes wet. Protection from sun is different: he can raise the garment above himself and thereby cast shade over his whole body. In truth, therefore, any intended benefit from sun or rain is forbidden. This is why Tur and Shulchan Aruch omit the distinction, even though Shulchan Aruch otherwise copies Rambam's language here. The detail concerning merchants has no practical legal difference. I write this because Derishah cites Rambam's phrase "to warm himself with it" and questions whether one who does not seek warmth but only wishes to prevent rain from damaging his clothing may do so. He then compares it to garments used during haircutting, which are permitted when they lack a head opening even though they protect one's clothing from hair, and suggests the same may apply to protection from rain. I am astonished if these words truly came from him. The haircutting garments prove the opposite. Rosh, cited by Beit Yosef, says that when people are being shorn they spread a sheet over themselves so their clothing will not become dirty; the Yerushalmi says prohibition applies when it is intended as clothing, meaning when it has a head opening. Thus, when there is a head opening it is prohibited even though its sole purpose is to protect the garments from dirt. The same applies here: this is a genuine garment, and if one covers himself with it to protect his clothes from rain, the prohibition certainly applies, as Tur and Shulchan Aruch imply. This appears entirely clear to me. See seif 10.

<b>Or around the Torah.</b> Hagahot Ashri in Perek Bnei HaIr has this wording: a cloth spread in the Aron Kodesh beneath Sifrei Torah or around the Torah. Rambam's Commentary to the Mishnah at the end of Kilayim, however, explains "cloths for books" as cloths used to hold a Sefer Torah during reading, and says they are subject to kilayim. Therefore, it appears clear that one may not make the mantle of a Sefer Torah, commonly called a mantel, or the cloth wrapped directly around the Sefer Torah itself, from kilayim. We see that the synagogue attendant frequently holds it in his hands, and there is concern he may warm himself with it in cold weather. Hagahot Ashri is not discussing such a cloth. <b>Ardilerin, and so forth.</b> Tur writes in the name of the Geonim that people commonly wear them beneath their shoes. The soles are covered with processed goatskin, while opposite the heel some are made of wool. There is no kilayim prohibition because the heel is hard and does not become warmed. Derishah asks from seif 3, where a large garment with kilayim on one side is prohibited even on the other side. Why then may the rest of the foot benefit from footwear containing kilayim at the heel? He gives lengthy answers. In my opinion there is no difficulty. The rule in seif 3 applies specifically to something that has the status of clothing. As explained above in seif 10, any soft item worn as clothing is prohibited even without actual warmth. Something that is not considered clothing is prohibited only if it actually warms the body. Here the item does not have the status of ordinary clothing because leather lies beneath it and only the area opposite the heel contains wool. If that wool produced warmth it would be prohibited on account of the warmth, but since there is no warmth and this is not a garment, it is permitted. This also resolves Rambam's language cited by Tur and Rama here: a shoe made with kilayim and lacking a heel may be worn because the skin of the foot is hard and one does not benefit from it like from the rest of the body's skin. Many have asked why Rambam specifies that the shoe lacks a heel. According to what I have written, it is clear. It appears to refer precisely to our slippers, commonly called pantofel, where there is no shoe leather surrounding the heel at all. Therefore, one may place kilayim beneath the upper leather over the foot or beneath the sole where the foot rests. Since there is nothing surrounding the heel, the kilayim material within the slipper is not treated as a garment in its own right. The only possible prohibition would therefore be actual warming, and the foot does not receive such warming. This explains the common practice of wearing slippers lined with cloth on all sides even when a gentile sewed the lining with linen thread. If, however, the same lining is placed within a full shoe that also has leather surrounding the heel, it is prohibited as a kilayim garment. We regard the cloth sewn inside as though it were an independent garment, despite the leather exterior. My father-in-law's words here contain an obvious slip of the pen. He says the novelty is that even though it has no heel it is prohibited, whereas the law is precisely that it is permitted. Beit Yosef similarly says that where it has a heel and therefore appears like a garment, and so forth. He says "appears like a garment," not that it is literally a garment, because it is covered by leather, as I have explained.
