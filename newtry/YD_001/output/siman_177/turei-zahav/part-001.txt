<b>As an idle worker, etc. </b>In the Gemara it states: the tanna taught "as an idle worker"; what is "as an idle worker"? Abaye said: as an idle worker of that work from which he refrained. Rashi explained: meaning if he first did heavy work and profits greatly and this is light work, they estimate how much he would forgo from his wages to do this work; which is more than what they give to do it, for a person does not forgo great profit except a little even though it comes to him with more trouble. Tosafos challenged Rashi and explained: therefore it appears to explain "as an idle worker" meaning as one who sits completely idle, etc.; however in chapter Elu Metziyos regarding returning a lost object where it teaches "he takes his wages as an idle worker"; it appears like Rashi's explanation from that they challenge there "behold he is not idle" because it implies to them as one who sits completely idle; end of their words. It appears from Tosafos's words that they have this version in the Gemara "behold he is not idle"; and likewise the Rosh regarding returning a lost object like Rashi's explanation; meaning such as work for which one takes four zuz and to leave it and sit idle he would take one zuz, and to toil in returning a lost object he would take two zuz and he reaches one zuz in wages for returning the lost object; end quote. Tosafos challenged this: if there like Rashi he takes wages for returning a lost object, and in HaMekabel we say he was an unpaid guardian of a lost object who has no benefit; one may say there is no concern if he takes wages since he is engaged in other work and loses, etc.; end of their words. The implication of Tosafos's words that they wrote "as one who sits completely idle" is as Beit Yosef wrote: they estimate how much a person wishes to take in order to be idle from his work completely; that now whether he gives him now light work or heavy work his measure is equal, meaning they assess his usual work how much this one would wish to take and sit completely idle. And Tur wrote: and not all his wages but they look at a person who is idle and has no work; how much he would wish to take and engage in this business; and even if he had work for which they give him good wages and he refrains from it; we do not say how much this one would wish to take and refrain from his work and engage in this business that he would take only much; rather they estimate an idle person as I explained; end quote. And behold Maharshal in Sefer Chokhmat Shlomo agreed to Tur's explanation; he challenged according to Beit Yosef's explanation: what is different in truth between the law of ribbis and the law of returning a lost object, and why did it not challenge there as well "behold he is not idle" since from the language "as an idle worker" it challenges; see there in chapter Eizehu Neshech. And it appears there is no difficulty at all; for this challenge "behold he is not idle" is not the plain Gemara challenging it, but Abaye himself challenges it and answers "as an idle worker of that work," etc., in the manner Tosafos are accustomed to explain everywhere there is a difficulty and afterward an amora is mentioned who is also the questioner; and as Tosafos wrote in chapter Elu Metziyos in what is written "how many did Rabbi Yitzchak establish four cubits," etc. And if so here on Abaye himself there is what to challenge: why was he not challenged in chapter Eizehu Neshech regarding giving the recipient's wages where they also said "as an idle worker"; one may say he holds in avak ribbis they were lenient as the Rosh wrote in this chapter; and likewise he himself according to Tur's explanation here for his leniency; meaning they assess an idle person how much he takes from this work; and in returning a lost object they also assess according to the first work like Rashi's explanation; and I wondered: for behold the Rosh wrote explicitly in chapter Eizehu Neshech "but here in avak ribbis the Sages were lenient," etc. And in Derishah he challenged Tur's explanation: since he pays him for the work of returning a lost object even though he was already engaged in work; as if he were idle; why did Tosafos challenge specifically on Rashi's explanation, behold according to them too he takes his wages and there is no difficulty; for in truth according to Tosafos per Tur's explanation he takes nothing for returning a lost object if he is an idle person; only if he was already engaged in work it is proper he not lose because of the returning and therefore they give him wages for refraining from his work in this manner: they assess how much accrues to him in wages for the returning work and likewise give him for the loss he has from his prior work. But according to Rashi who takes one zuz more as I copied in the Rosh's name; certainly it is difficult "behold he takes wages for returning a lost object"; and on this they answered there is no concern since in any case he has a loss and must take; there is no concern if he also takes for the returning; and my father-in-law, of blessed memory also elaborated to challenge and answer between the explanations; and in my humble opinion both Beit Yosef's explanation and Tur's explanation are true; for certainly the implication of Tosafos is like Beit Yosef's explanation as I wrote above; and likewise it appears in Tosafos of the Rosh that Beit Yosef brought; and as I will explain shortly; and it is that according to Beit Yosef's explanation we say how much one worker wishes to take in order to be idle from his work completely; it works out for a master of work: we know the measure of compensation for his labor for this; but what shall we do with a completely idle person who accepts an iska; how shall we estimate compensation for his labor? Therefore in this we assess according to what an idle person takes to do this business; for if less than this we do not know his measure; necessarily we assess according to what an idle person takes from this business, which is less than what a plain person takes; and even for Rabbi Meir who is lenient in the Gemara and holds his wages whether much or little; he is lenient only with a master of work that he not calculate according to his work when he already engaged; but nevertheless he is not lenient from what an idle person takes, meaning even though the way of workers in that city is to take four gold coins; only that an idle person from all work takes less than that; that lesser sum the giver must give the recipient; and in this Bach agrees with Tur; except if he is a master of work and his work was completely refrained, Tur is more stringent to give him that loss. And what Bach challenged on Tur in what he wrote that they assess an idle person how much he would take in this business; and if so everything is according to the weight of the business the recipient accepts; and why do we say in the Gemara "it is necessary"; for if the latter clause were not taught, meaning when he gives him money to buy produce for profit together, we might say it does not suffice as an idle worker since the trouble is great; and if according to Tur's words how could we say so; behold he pays him that trouble that an idle person wishes more in order to do heavy work than light; there is no difficulty: for we might say since there is great trouble in this we do not assess his wages as if he were idle but we assess with a plain person how much he would take in this business. But in what Tosafos challenge Rashi one cannot answer so; for according to Rashi there is no leniency at all in the world; rather the Mishnah follows Rabbi Shimon and teaches us the stringency that he must specifically pay all his loss.

This is avak ribbis, and even the tzon barzel arrangement of seif 1 was only close to profit and remote from loss; avak ribbis is not recoverable through beis din. And if so R' Yeshaya's words have a source from the Talmud. But the reverse; that the giver buys the doubt from the recipient for a fixed amount; is certainly permitted. But if the giver gives some sum in an iska to his fellow to deal and profit and if he profits he will give a fixed amount to the giver and if he does not profit he will give nothing; there is no prohibition in this; and such is the practice every day in all communities who make mamranut and write on them that it is an iska document for such-and-such sum principal and profit according to heter iska such-and-such sum; and no doubt or flutter of prohibition is heard on this, only that liability for loss must be on both.

Compensation for waiting applies only where paying earlier would benefit the borrower and delaying payment causes him to pay more, so that the lender's money increases with each day of delay. <b>Lest he die, and it will result, etc. </b>The Tur writes that he likewise may not write a promissory note against him, because if the document does not state that it is an iska, the entire amount is treated as a loan and is close to profit, etc., even if they orally stipulated otherwise, since we follow the written document and the whole amount appears to be a loan. This is difficult, for it would then seem that even where there is profit he may not take it. Why, then, did the Gemara say the prohibition is because perhaps there will be no profit? It is also difficult that the Gemara gives the reason that perhaps the giver will die and the document will fall into the hands of his heirs, for even while the giver is alive it should be forbidden to collect such ribbis. The Tur's words appear to mean as follows. He first states that one who gives an iska to his fellow should not combine the principal and profit in one sum. That is, he should not write in the document, 'Reuven received from Shimon such-and-such a sum,' where that sum includes principal and anticipated profit. Although we could say he received it as an iska, there is nevertheless a prohibition because he converted the anticipated profit into principal, and perhaps that amount of profit will not be realized. The Tur then writes that he should not write even the principal alone as a promissory note, meaning in the language of a loan. The reason is that such a document enables him to demand the entire principal even if the principal suffered a loss. Although witnesses know that it was arranged as an iska, the written obligation is clear while the limiting condition exists only orally. Since the document leaves open the possibility that he can collect the entire principal despite a loss, it is close to profit and remote from loss. Therefore, even if no loss actually occurred, the document contains a forbidden feature and may not be used for collection. This resolves the difficulty from the Tur's citation in Choshen Mishpat, siman 176, seif 18, of a responsum of the Rosh, which implies that one may take profit when the principal was written in a document. There, the document did not use language of debt, but was written in the first manner described here. This is clear to one who studies the Rosh's responsa, klal 89, which is the source of the Tur's ruling that one should likewise not write a promissory note, etc.

For example a quarter in profit for the recipient; we say if there will be damage the recipient accepts two-thirds of it, meaning four; and if he wrote he takes three parts in profit and the giver a quarter, damage comes upon the recipient twelve. This law if it is explicit in the document which share he takes in profit and the loss share was not mentioned; but if half loss; meaning it is written in the document the recipient takes half in loss and his share if there is profit was not mentioned; we say two-thirds in profit, meaning if there is profit of twenty-four gold coins he takes two-thirds of all the profit, meaning sixteen gold coins. But one should not explain two-thirds in profit here from the sum that would accrue to him if there were damage as we explained in the first clause two-thirds in loss; for then the recipient would take only eight gold coins in profit; and this is a prohibition that specifically the recipient must take more in profit than his loss share. But if a quarter in loss is written on the recipient and the profit share was not mentioned otherwise; two-thirds in profit, meaning we say if there were loss the giver would accept loss of three parts, meaning eighteen gold coins; that sum we calculate in profit now and the recipient takes two-thirds of those eighteen gold coins, meaning twelve gold coins which is half; thus is the primary version and not versions that read "he takes a third"; for that version is according to Ramban, who holds whether for profit or loss we follow the recipient's share; and it results at the end of this seif the recipient would take only eight gold coins. But the Rambam does not hold so; rather in this it is not proper that he take only eight gold coins and the giver take sixteen gold coins; therefore we calculate here as the giver's share from which he takes two parts, meaning twelve gold coins; and this is according to his loss share and the addition of his fellow's third. And the rule in this: regarding loss we follow only the share accruing to the loan; for the recipient pays only a third of the loss accruing to his share; and regarding profit we follow the deposit share; meaning the recipient takes another third beyond what accrues to the deposit share in addition to what accrues to his share. The reason is explained in Kesef Mishneh in chapter 6 of Hilchos Sheluchin: and never does the recipient lose except a third of the loss affecting his share; and the other third the giver assists him; and in his compensation for his labor; and never does the giver take except two-thirds of the profit affecting; the other third he gives the recipient as his compensation for his labor; and likewise always according to this value whenever loss is mentioned in less than half. However if it is written that he accept three parts in loss; then one cannot say at all two-thirds in profit; rather he takes in profit according to the value above according to his loss share and the addition of a third in his fellow's share; and it results he takes twenty gold coins from the profit.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>He shall take nothing. </b>The reason is that his compensation was initially dependent upon earning a profit, and no profit was earned. In the latter clause, however, they stipulated to divide profit and loss equally, and he must receive compensation as a worker. From the outset he relied upon that compensation. Had there been profit, his worker's compensation would first have been taken from the common funds, and they would have divided the remainder. Now that there is no profit, he nevertheless does not lose his worker's compensation and may take it from the principal. Since he must also replenish his share of the loss from the principal, he takes only one-half of his compensation from the principal and loses the remainder. This is explained in the responsum of the Ra'avad cited by the Beit Yosef. The Levush supplied another reason of his own, but it is entirely incorrect.
