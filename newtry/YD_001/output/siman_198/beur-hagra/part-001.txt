<b>(Addendum) Most of her hair.</b> Rambam writes this in the name of the Geonim, but disputes them and holds that the head is counted together with the body, so that only a majority of the body constitutes an interposition. The Raavad, in his glosses, agrees with the Geonim, and this is also the view of the other halachic authorities. It appears to me that there is proof for their position from Bava Kamma 82a, which states that by Torah law one must inspect lest the hair be knotted, etc. Knots are found only on the head, yet by Torah law an interposition requires a majority together with being particular about it. One cannot say that the head is combined with the body, for the Gemara then says, 'alternatively, lest there be something repulsive,' etc., implying that the first concern is solely for knots. (End of addendum.)

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>She should not stand, etc.</b> This follows the Mishnah in chapter 5 of Mikvaot, Mishnah 2, 'provided that,' etc. See the Rash there. It must therefore be explained as a rabbinic decree, etc. The Raavad writes that this is why Niddah speaks specifically of standing on an earthenware vessel, because such a vessel does not contract impurity from its outer surface and cannot become impure through treading, as stated in chapter 9 of Shabbat, 84b. Likewise, a basket of this type cannot contract impurity at all. With a wooden vessel, however, even its outer surface, or any object fit for sitting or lying upon, is included in the decree concerning bathhouses, like the bench in the Mishnah cited above. The same applies to planks and similar objects mentioned there. That Mishnah cannot be explained as a concern that the woman will be frightened, since it discusses vessels. It must therefore be because of the decree concerning bathhouses. A practical difference exists even regarding a person: if the concern is fear, the immersion is valid after the fact, but if it is the bathhouse decree, the immersion is invalid even after the fact. This is the view of the Rashba and Ran. The Rosh and Rambam, however, hold that the bathhouse rationale was rejected in the final analysis, and that the Mishnah refers specifically to the outer surfaces of vessels, which were prohibited lest one immerse inside them. See the Rosh at the end of siman 9, 'provided that she did not,' etc., and the view introduced as, 'some explain,' etc.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>If they entered, etc.</b> The Tosefta in chapter 8 states: If pebbles or splinters entered the cracks on the underside of his feet, Rabbi Akiva says they do not constitute an interposition because that area is considered a concealed part of the body. It further states there: regarding a plaster, poultice, or bandage upon a concealed part of the body, Rabbi says it constitutes an interposition, while Rabbi Yose son of Rabbi Yehudah says it does not. It appears that Rabbi Akiva follows the view of Rabbi Yose son of Rabbi Yehudah. We, however, rule like Rabbi, as stated in Niddah 66b: although actual contact of water is not required, etc., and as ruled by the Semag.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And if she immersed, there are, etc.</b> According to our version of the Tosefta at the beginning of chapter 8: If his feet were covered with dust and he descended and immersed in a mikveh containing forty seah, he is pure; if he rubbed them or entered hot water, he is impure. This version is difficult, for logically the reverse should be true. The concluding clause likewise states: If a kettle was filled with coals and one immersed it, it remains impure; if he rubbed it or immersed it in hot water, it is pure. The opening clause should therefore be corrected accordingly. The Rash copies it this way in chapter 10, Mishnah 4, and the Mishnah cited above compares the two cases. Rambam does not record the clause concerning rubbing in the case of a person, and omits the Tosefta's ruling concerning a kettle entirely. This requires study. (End of addendum.)
