If some people are particular about this, although most people are not, and this woman too is ordinarily particular about it at other times but is not particular now, it constitutes a chatzitzah. Likewise, even if she is never particular about it, if most people are particular about it, it constitutes a chatzitzah. However, if only some people are particular about it and she is never particular, it does not constitute a chatzitzah. See what I wrote in seif 18.

<b>ואם הוא חופה רוב הגוף כו'. </b>דדבר תורה אינו חוצץ אלא ברובו ומקפיד עליו וגזרו רבנן ברובו שאין מקפיד משום רוב המקפיד וגזרו על המיעוט המקפיד גם כן משום רוב המקפיד אבל במיעוט ואינו מקפיד דתרתי לטיבותא לא גזרו כלל דהוה גזירה לגזירה. והא דכ' רמ"א אחר כך גזירה אטו דברים החוצצים והיינו אפילו במיעוט התם קאמר לכתחלה אבל דיעבד לא גזרינן גזירה לגזירה:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And if it covers most of the body, etc. </b>According to Torah law, a substance constitutes a chatzitzah only when it covers most of the body and one is particular about it. The Sages decreed concerning a substance covering most of the body even when one is not particular, because of a case in which it covers most of the body and one is particular. They likewise decreed concerning a substance covering a minority when one is particular, because of a case in which it covers most of the body and one is particular. However, when it covers a minority and one is not particular, there are two grounds for leniency, and they made no decree at all, for that would be a decree upon a decree. What the Rama writes later, that this is a decree because of substances that constitute a chatzitzah, even when they cover only a minority, refers to the ideal practice. After the fact, however, we do not make a decree upon a decree.

שזכרתי אבל ודאי לענין המנין הם שוים דכמו שלענין שער עצמה תלוי הדבר היאך נקשרו השערות אם אחת אחת או שתים שתים ה"נ כן הוא אם כרכה חד שער על שערה חוצץ והיינו אפילו אם כרכה הרבה שערות סביב זה שלא במקום זה אחת בחברתה זה מיקרי נמי נימא אחת דמה לי אחת בפני עצמה או עם חברתה וכמ"ש ב"י אח"כ ושפיר פסק כרבי יוחנן שמביא ב"י ובחנם כתב כאן שפסק כרבה בב"ח אלא שאח"כ סיים הר"ן דכאן בכריכת שער דעלמא אין חציצה לפי שאין עושין כריכא זו בנימא א' והטור כ' דאם עשאוה משער אחת הוה חציצה ואין חילוק דין ביניהם אלא במציאות הענין ועפ"ז דברי הש"ע מבוארין דמ"ש בסעיף ד' חוטי השער אינם חוצצין היינו בדין הכריכה שזכרנו וגם שם יש חילוק היאך נעשית הכריכה אם משער אחת או יותר ושם הוה חציצה אם נעשית הכריכה משער אחת ואפילו לא הוה רובא ואינה מקפדת דהוה כמו חוטי צמר דסעיף ב' דמייא לא עיילי שם עד שתרפם וכאן סעיף ה' מיירי מדין השני דהיינו שערותיה עצמן ובזה תלוי במקפדת וכמו שסיים כאן הש"ע:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Two hairs, etc. </b>The Tur records this law twice in this siman, once here and a second time where he writes that when most of the hair is knotted, one strand, etc. His intent is that this law is likewise found twice in the Gemara. One source is in the chapter Bameh Ishah, where all agree that hair does not constitute a chatzitzah. This means that if she wound the hair of her head with other hair, it does not constitute a chatzitzah because it cannot be tightened well. The second source is in the final chapter of Niddah, cited by the Beit Yosef, where it states: One strand constitutes a chatzitzah; regarding two, I do not know; three do not constitute a chatzitzah. That source concerns the woman's own hair. If most of her hairs were tied individually, only then does it constitute a chatzitzah. This is the case discussed later by the Tur. Here, however, he discusses external hair attached to her hair. A practical distinction between the two cases is that here, whenever the attachment constitutes a chatzitzah, it does so even over a minority, like the woolen threads of seif 2, as I wrote there. Therefore, the Tur did not write here that most of her hair must be affected, as he writes later. There, since he discusses her own hair that became knotted, a majority is specifically required, for otherwise she is not particular. The Beit Yosef writes concerning the Tur's words here that this case concerns one who is very particular, etc., but that is imprecise. This consideration does not apply here, but only later, for the reason stated. Nevertheless, regarding the number of hairs, the laws are equal. Just as with her own hair the matter depends upon how the hairs were tied, whether one at a time or two at a time, so too if she wound one external hair around her hair it constitutes a chatzitzah. This applies even if she wound many hairs in separate places, provided that each winding consists of one hair, because one hair can be tightened well. But if she used two or more hairs for each winding, it does not constitute a chatzitzah. This is what the Tur means here by, "When does this apply? When they consist of two hairs," meaning each individual winding, as I explained. These words accord with the Ran cited by the Beit Yosef, who writes: "They agree concerning threads of hair that they do not constitute a chatzitzah. This applies specifically to hair placed upon girls' heads, for hair cannot be tightened well. But tying one strand to itself or one strand to another can be tightened, for we say in the final chapter of Niddah that one tied strand constitutes a chatzitzah, etc. Even winding these hair threads around her own hairs is permitted, because it is impossible for these threads not to contain more than one strand, etc." End quote. Thus, he too writes that the first law concerns winding hair around her own hair, while tying one strand concerns her own hair. His words, "or one strand to another," are likewise called one strand, for what difference is there whether it is tied to itself or to another strand, as the Beit Yosef writes later. The Tur therefore properly ruled like R' Yochanan, whom the Beit Yosef cites, and the Bach needlessly wrote that he ruled like Rabbah. The Ran concludes that external hair wound around her hair does not constitute a chatzitzah because such a winding is not made from a single strand. The Tur writes that if it was made from one strand, it does constitute a chatzitzah. Their legal rulings do not differ, only their assumptions about the facts. Accordingly, the words of the Shulchan Aruch are clear. What he writes in seif 4, that threads of hair do not constitute a chatzitzah, refers to the winding discussed above. Even there, however, the law depends upon whether the winding was made from one hair or more. If it was made from one hair, it constitutes a chatzitzah even if it affects only a minority and she is not particular, like the woolen threads in seif 2, since water cannot enter until she loosens them. Here, in seif 5, the Shulchan Aruch discusses the second law, namely her own hair, and this depends upon whether she is particular, as the Shulchan Aruch concludes here.

According to our ruling in siman 532, where cutting on Chol HaMoed is permitted, she may cut them normally on Chol HaMoed. On Shabbos and Yom Tov she should not cut them at all, but should clean them thoroughly. This appears entirely clear to me. **** ENGLISH ****
<b>A partially detached fingernail. </b>The Beit Yosef discusses this at length because he found it difficult to understand why there is a chatzitzah when a minority remains attached more than when a majority remains attached. He was therefore compelled to explain that the case concerns some other intervening substance upon the detached portion. The Mordechai, cited by the Beit Yosef, writes: "When most of it remains, it constitutes a chatzitzah, specifically because most is considered as the whole. Rather, the law concerns the separated portion. If most of the nail remains and only a minority has split, the opening is narrow and water cannot enter the split. But if most has split away, the opening is widened and water can enter there, so it is fit for water to reach. Regarding a partially detached limb or flesh, even when most has separated, one does not leave it hanging, for that causes great pain. Rather, she constantly holds it as close to the body as possible, and therefore that place is not fit for water to reach. This appears to be the proper explanation, with God's help.

According to R' Shimon, loosening the hands suffices; according to the first Tanna it does not suffice, because of a decree lest he fail to loosen them. Therefore, he ruled that one must specifically rinse his hands first. I already wrote in siman 120, seif 2, that he may rinse his hands even with detached water, unlike the Rama there. Here, R' Shimon maintains that one holding utensils for immersion ordinarily presses his hands upon them so they do not fall, and likewise when holding another person, thereby preventing the liquid upon his hands from forming any connection. The Rashba and Tur are certainly reliable, especially in a rabbinic matter, since according to Torah law only a substance covering most of the body and about which one is particular constitutes a chatzitzah. There is another dispute between the Rambam and Ramban on one side and the Rashba on the other. According to the Rambam and Ramban, as I proved above, even a tight grip does not constitute a chatzitzah if the hands were rinsed first. The Rashba explicitly writes that although the halacha follows the first Tanna, one must not press excessively, but should hold in the normal manner, and we are not concerned that he might hold tightly. The Mordechai appears to agree, for he rules that one should not place a full utensil inside a bucket of water and immerse it in a pit, because the weight of the utensil creates a chatzitzah, as I cited in siman 120, seif 2. This accords with the Rashba, since the utensil's weight resembles a person gripping it tightly. Accordingly, when the Shulchan Aruch rules here like the Rashba that loosening helps, and then writes that rinsing her hands first helps even without loosening, it appears to me that although she did not loosen her grip, she still must not hold her companion tightly. Otherwise, prior rinsing would not help. We merely do not suspect that she will grip tightly, as I explained in the Rashba's view. He writes correctly, for an explicit Mishnah teaches that one may hold a person, and all the poskim rule accordingly. They were not concerned that she might grip excessively. This is certainly so according to the Rambam and Ramban, who maintain that even a tight grip causes no harm whenever she first rinsed her hands.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And not upon boards, etc. </b>The Gemara states that Rava said: A woman should not stand upon an earthenware utensil and immerse. Rav Kahana initially thought that the reason was the decree concerning bathhouses, for in their bathhouses people would sit upon earthen platforms resembling earthenware utensils, and one might come to say that immersion in them is valid. According to that reasoning, standing upon a thick wooden beam would be permitted. Rav Chanan of Nehardea told him that the reason there is that she is afraid she may fall and therefore may not immerse properly; upon a beam too she is afraid. The Beit Yosef cites the Ra'avad that even according to the conclusion, the concern for bathhouses remains, based upon the Mishnah stating that one may not immerse upon a bench. There, fear cannot be the reason, since the Mishnah discusses utensils. He therefore explains that the Mishnah likewise prohibited this because of the decree concerning bathhouses. Thus, one may not immerse upon anything bearing the status of a utensil, even a flat wooden utensil, since it is susceptible to impurity. An earthenware utensil itself does not contract impurity through contact, since it becomes impure only through its airspace, and a thick wooden beam likewise would otherwise have been permitted. Rava therefore taught that one should not immerse even upon these because of fear. The practical difference between the bathhouse decree and fear is that anything prohibited because of the bathhouse decree is invalid even after the fact, because an observer may mistakenly conclude that immersion in a bathhouse is permitted. When the reason is fear, everyone knows that she must immerse properly; the concern is merely that she may inadvertently become frightened and fail to immerse properly, and therefore it was not invalidated after the fact. This appears to be the reason for the distinction. Accordingly, the Ra'avad wrote, as quoted in the Shulchan Aruch, that one may not immerse upon anything susceptible to impurity. He mentions boards fit for midras impurity, because midras impurity applies only when one intended them for that purpose, and sometimes even an act is required, as stated by the Rambam in chapter 25 of Hilchos Keilim. There he writes that boards of a bathhouse that were trimmed do not contract impurity because they are not made for sitting. Certainly ordinary construction boards sold for building do not contract midras impurity. The Ra'avad nevertheless holds that since they could contract midras impurity if designated for making a bench or the like, we must be concerned that an observer might mistakenly say that the same law applies even after they were designated for sitting and became subject to midras impurity, and from this he may come to immerse in bathhouses. This is the intent of the Ra'avad's phrase, "boards fit for midras," and so too writes the Ritva, as cited by the Beit Yosef. According to this, the custom in our countries to place boards in a mikveh for the immersing woman to stand upon is very difficult. The Levush discusses this at length. Earlier authorities already addressed it, namely Maharam Padua in responsum 31, in response to Maharar David Vital. His principal argument is that our mikveh boards are not deemed designated for midras, since they are made to stand upon for immersion. On the contrary, this removes them from the status of utensils and makes them like the roof of a house, particularly once they are fixed with nails, since they never previously bore the status of a utensil.<br><b>Furthermore, </b>since these boards are placed upon beams and water floats over them, they are like part of the structure. Thus, even if they had already been utensils according to Torah law, they are now nullified, etc. <i data-commentator='Nekudot HaKesef' data-label='♯' data-order='2'></i>His words are exceedingly difficult. The Gemara did not say that the bathhouse decree is removed except regarding a thick wooden beam, not ordinary wood or even flat wooden utensils. This is the Ra'avad's principal inference, from which he learned the law for anything fit to contract impurity. Furthermore, what merit is there in the fact that it never previously bore the status of a utensil? An observer may nevertheless err, as explained above. This is why the Ra'avad wrote, "fit for midras." Most astonishing is his statement that even if it previously had the status of a utensil, it is nullified because the water floats over it and it is like part of the structure. It is obvious that even actual construction does not nullify a utensil, as shown by a pipe that was hollowed and afterward affixed, which invalidates a mikveh. Maharam himself wrote this immediately earlier in that responsum, so how could he forget it within the same discussion? He further justified the custom on the basis that we follow those who hold that flat wooden utensils do not contract midras impurity. This is a valid reason, as will be explained, but it does not accord with the Ra'avad, who explicitly includes even flat wooden utensils and derives it from the Gemara's specific reference to a thick beam. In truth, one cannot criticize Maharam for that responsum, because he did not see these words of the Ra'avad and Rashba, as he himself writes there. My revered father-in-law, of blessed memory, writes that all of Maharam Padua's words are correct. It is surprising that he says the primary permission is that the boards are fixed with nails, but if not fixed they are forbidden. Maharam explicitly permits them even when not fixed, writing that the water itself renders them fixed. Mahari Katz of Cracow likewise sought permission on the ground that they are utensils made to remain stationary, meaning not for use but merely to remain in place, and therefore they do not contract impurity. According to him too, there is permission even when they are not fixed with nails. His reasoning is even more difficult, for although a utensil made to remain stationary does not contract impurity through contact, it certainly can contract midras impurity, as I cited from the Rambam concerning bathhouse boards, which certainly contract midras impurity if designated for sitting. In sum, my mind is not settled by any permission for mikveh boards according to the Ra'avad and Rashba cited above, and the Levush properly wondered about the custom according to their view. It appears to me, however, that we have a clear permission from another direction. Other poskim do not agree with the Ra'avad. They maintain that when the Gemara attributes the prohibition to the bathhouse decree according to Rava, the prohibition is not due to susceptibility to impurity at all, but to other reasons that do not apply to these boards. This is proven from Rabbeinu Shimshon, cited by the Beit Yosef in this siman. He asks why a spring led over a trough, meaning an affixed stone utensil, is invalid. Why should it be worse than a utensil immersed inside another utensil in a mikveh, when the outer utensil has an opening the size of a wineskin spout? He answers that there is a distinction between detached utensils, in which immersion is valid when they are connected to the mikveh, and fixed utensils over which the spring flows, because this may lead to error. Sometimes they are fixed at the water's source without an opening the size of a wineskin spout, and all water passing over them becomes invalid. It is possible that this is likewise the reason for the Mishnah's prohibition against immersion upon a bench, and also the reason for the bathhouse decree discussed above, which concerns earthenware fixed there to prevent mud. They prohibited it for the reason explained. The Rosh responds that there is no difficulty. The comparison to immersing even within the utensil itself is imprecise, for there the inner utensil itself is immersed within the mikveh, since the outer opening is the size of a wineskin spout. But one may not immerse within a utensil connected to a spring, lest one come to immerse in a vessel containing 40 se'ah without any connection. We must understand the Rosh's distinction. It appears that when one utensil is inside another and both are immersed in a valid mikveh, there is no reason to decree lest one immerse in a utensil without a connection, since both utensils are now within valid mikveh water and no mistaken inference follows. By contrast, when a spring flows over a trough, the water continually passes over the trough, and sometimes the spring or mikveh connection is interrupted. One may then mistakenly immerse there even at that time. The Beit Yosef writes that the Rosh disagrees with Rabbeinu Shimshon, for according to the Rosh even detached utensils may not be used. Nevertheless, in a responsum concerning a channel connected to the floor of a mikveh, the Rosh cites Rabbeinu Shimshon's explanation that the decree is because it might sometimes be placed at the water's source, etc. He writes that this concern does not apply to a channel. Furthermore, an ordinary wooden channel made for water has no rim and is a flat wooden utensil, which does not contract impurity and does not invalidate through drawn water, as the Beit Yosef cites shortly. His words appear to mean, first, that the concern that it may be placed at the spring's source without an opening the size of a wineskin spout does not apply to a channel, since every channel has such an opening. Second, we decree that something might be placed at the spring's source only concerning a complete utensil whose validity depends upon an opening the size of a wineskin spout. A flat wooden utensil does not contract impurity, so there is no concern that it will invalidate the water if placed at the source. The boards in our mikvaos are certainly no worse than such a channel. Just as Rabbeinu Shimshon's concern does not apply to the channel, it does not apply to these boards. Likewise, according to the Rosh's reason for prohibiting even detached utensils, lest one immerse in them without a connection, this applies only to a trough in which one could immerse without connection to the spring, since water could remain within it. It does not apply to boards or a channel, for without connection to the spring there is no water there at all. This appears entirely clear to me. All the more so according to the Beit Yosef's conclusion regarding the Tur's words, "My father the Rosh made no distinction," that the Rosh is not concerned with susceptibility to impurity but only with fear, there is obviously no concern regarding boards. Everything I have written to permit boards applies likewise to wooden steps in a mikveh, which are found throughout our countries, for the same reasoning governs both. I found written in the name of Maharal of Prague: "Wooden steps are permitted, because the reason is the decree lest one place the bench at the spring's source, and steps are not made for water to pass through them." End quote. This is precisely as I have written. Therefore, according to the established custom, we certainly do not follow the Shulchan Aruch in this seif, which rules like the Ra'avad and makes the entire matter depend upon susceptibility to impurity, including flat wooden utensils and anything fit for midras. Rather, we follow Rabbeinu Shimshon and the Rosh, according to whom these boards are entirely permitted and are exactly like a channel attached to the mikveh floor. One who invalidates the boards must likewise invalidate the channel, since it too is a flat wooden utensil, yet in truth both are unquestionably valid. Even if the boards are laid without being fixed by nails, everything is permitted, as ruled by Maharam Padua and Mahari Katz, though not for their reasons, but for the reasons stated above.

<b>וא"צ להרחיק כו'. </b>בב"י הקשה הא כבר נתן שיעור אחר דהיינו כעורכת כו' ונ"ל דהטור נגרר אחר לשון הגמרא שאמר ר"ל לא תטבול אלא דרך גדילתה פירוש הילוכה כדתנן האיש נראה כעודר ומוסק זיתים כו' והיינו דלענין נגעים אמרינן שיעור ההוא לענין ראיית כהן ה"נ לענין טבילה אלא דלאו כל אדם בקי בהנך שיעורים היאך הם נתן שיעור שישער כל אדם בעצמו היאך מרוחקים איבריו קצת כדרך הילוכו ואידי ואידי חד שיעורא וכן נמי דעת הטור שאחר שכתב השיעור הנזכר בגמרא אמר שכיון שאין כל אדם יוכל לשער כזה יטעה לומר שצריך להרחיב הירכים הרבה וכן הרחקת הידים מן הגוף יהיה הרבה כדי שיצא מידי ספק לזה אמר שא"צ אלא תשער כפי שרגילה בשעת הילוכה ומו"ח ז"ל פירש שבנשים שחצניות מיירי שמגביהין ידיהן על צדיהן ודרך רחוקה היא שיתן התלמוד והפוסקים שיעורא להנשים כאלה שזה דרך בנות ציון שגינה הכתוב:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And she need not distance, etc. </b>The Beit Yosef asks that the Tur already gave another measure, namely like a woman kneading, etc. It appears to me that the Tur followed the Gemara's wording. Reish Lakish said that a woman should immerse only in the manner of her normal growth, meaning her normal posture while walking, as we learned: a man is viewed like one hoeing and harvesting olives, etc. Just as that measure applies regarding the examination of negaim by a kohen, so too it applies regarding immersion. However, not everyone knows precisely how to evaluate those measures. Therefore, the Tur gave a measure that each person can estimate for herself, namely that her limbs should be slightly separated as they ordinarily are while walking. Both descriptions refer to the same measure. This is likewise the Tur's intent. After recording the measure stated in the Gemara, he explains that since not everyone can estimate it, a woman might mistakenly think that she must spread her thighs very widely and distance her arms far from her body to avoid doubt. He therefore states that this is unnecessary. She should estimate the separation according to her normal manner of walking. My revered father-in-law, of blessed memory, explains that this refers to haughty women who lift their arms out to their sides. It is far-fetched to say that the Gemara and poskim established their measure according to such women, for this is the conduct of the daughters of Zion that Scripture condemned.
