(סימן ש"כ בשו"ע סעיף ו') <b>והקנין יהיה שיקבל פרוטה מהעובד כוכבים ויקנה לו המקום.</b> בשו"ת אמונת שמואל סי' מ"ה מיישב המנהג גרוע שנוהגים לכתחילה בכסף משום דבדיניהם מעות קונות וא"י לחזור בו. ועוד דכמה פוסקים סוברים כן ויישב שם דברי מ"ב שהביאו בא"ח סי' תמ"ח ובשו"ת שער אפרים סי' ע"ט כתב להקל בס' בכור שנמכר אף בכסף לחוד משום שכתב הרמב"ם דבח"ל בכור דרבנן. וכ"כ החינוך פ' בא ועיין בהגהת מרדכי שלהי חולין דבכור בזה"ז דרבנן וא"כ הוי ספק דרבנן. וכ"ש כשחלוב' או מל"ת ואין לומר דהוי איתחזק איסורא דהעמד האם בחזקת שלא ילדה דאדרבה אזלינן טפי בתר חזקת היתר דולד גופיה קודם שנולד מדניזיל בתר חזקת האם. וכמ"ש בשו"ת זכרון יוסף חי"ד סי' י"ט וע"ש סי' כ' וכ"א. ועיין קצה"ח סי' ר"ד דאם העובד כוכבים סייב לישראל הוי ככסף וסגי במשיכה לחוד:<br><b>ודע</b> דקנין חליפין מהני נמי גבי בכור לר"ת כמ"ש בתה"ד סי' ר"ע וזה נעלם מבעל הש"ך בח"מ סי' קכ"ג שכתב דגבי בכור לא כתבו הפוס' היתר דחליפין וכבר כתבו בתה"ד. גם העיד בתה"ד שם שלא מצא גאון דפליג על ר"ת וא"כ יש לסמוך על זה עכ"פ בדיעבד. ושוב מצאתי שהרגיש בזה בשו"ת שער אפרים שם וכ"כ בס"ס צ"צ:
**** ENGLISH ****
(Siman 320, seif 6) <b>The acquisition should be that he receive a perutah from the gentile and sell him the place.</b> Responsa Emunat Shmuel, siman 45, defends the poor custom of relying initially on money alone, because under gentile law money effects acquisition and the purchaser cannot retract. In addition, several poskim hold that way. He also reconciles the ruling of Masat Binyamin cited in Orach Chayyim siman 448. Responsa Sha'ar Ephraim, siman 79, is lenient regarding a doubtful firstborn that was sold by money alone because Rambam writes that firstborn sanctity outside Eretz Yisrael is rabbinic, and Sefer HaChinuch in Parashat Bo says similarly. Hagahot Mordechai at the end of Chullin also says firstborn status nowadays is rabbinic, making the matter a rabbinic doubt. This is all the more so if the mother had previously been milked or if a gentile speaks incidentally. One should not argue that there is an established presumption of prohibition because the mother is presumed not to have given birth. On the contrary, the fetus itself had a prior presumption of permissibility before birth, and we follow that more readily than the mother's presumption. Responsa Zichron Yosef, Yoreh Deah simanim 19 to 21, writes similarly. See Ketzot HaChoshen, siman 204, that where the gentile owes the Jew money, this can function like money, so meshichah alone suffices.<br><b>Know also</b> that kinyan chalipin works for firstborn animals according to Rabbenu Tam, as Terumat HaDeshen, siman 270, writes. This escaped Shach in Choshen Mishpat siman 123, who says the poskim did not mention chalipin as a permitted mode of acquisition in the firstborn context. Terumat HaDeshen explicitly did, and he further testifies that he found no Gaon disagreeing with Rabbenu Tam. Thus at least after the fact one may rely on it. Sha'ar Ephraim later notes this point as well, as does Tzemach Tzedek.

<b>Question.</b> Reuven was owed twenty rubles by a gentile. In MarCheshvan he demanded payment, and the gentile said he had no cash but offered him a cow that had never calved, saying it would certainly give birth that winter, for a price of fifteen rubles, with the balance of the debt remaining. Reuven inspected the cow without the gentile's knowledge. Later the gentile told him to accept the cow at that price, on condition that it remain with the gentile and be fed by him until calving, after which Reuven would take it home. Some time later Reuven decided he wanted the cow before calving so that it would give birth in his own home. He entered the barn, tied a rope to its horns, and led it through the courtyard, but it broke away in the middle of the courtyard and ran back into the barn. All this occurred without the gentile owner's knowledge. Later Reuven returned to the village with Avraham and they stayed several days. Avraham saw the cow and advised Reuven to take it home because it was close to calving. They hired another gentile to bring the cow to Reuven's home. It is unknown whether the original gentile owner knew of this. On the road, Avraham suggested that Reuven sell the cow to the gentile transporting it, lest the cow calve before Reuven remembered to make a proper sale exempting the offspring from firstborn status. Avraham negotiated the sale. The purchaser gave Reuven a small deposit, and Avraham guaranteed the remainder, since Avraham himself owed that gentile at least that amount. No kinyan sudar was made. They continued to town, Reuven paid the transporter for his work, and the gentile left. Later the cow gave birth to a male calf. They slaughtered it without consulting a sage, sent the hide to a tanner, salted and cooked the meat, and ate one meal from it. Only afterward did people inform them that halachic consultation had been required. By then the hide had become mixed among many other hides and could no longer be identified. What is the law of the remaining meat, the salting and cooking utensils, the utensils used for eating, and the mixed hides?

<br><b>Answer.</b> The opening of the second chapter of Bechorot shows that according to R. Yochanan, who holds that biblically money acquires from a Jew, the verse is expounded to mean that with one's fellow Jew acquisition is by money, while with a gentile it is by meshichah. Rabbenu Tam, cited by Tosafot there and in Bava Metzia 48a, rules like R. Yochanan against Reish Lakish, and concludes that Rav Ashi also holds that meshichah acquires from a gentile but money does not. Rosh, Hagahot Or Zarua, Mordechai in the name of Ri of Vienna, Responsa Maimoniyot, and Nimukei Yosef rule similarly. Later authorities accept this at least after the fact: if a Jew sold an animal to a gentile by meshichah, that transfer exempts it from firstborn status. It follows conversely that where a Jew purportedly purchased from a gentile through money alone without meshichah, the acquisition should not take effect. Taz explicitly writes this in the name of Responsa Rama. Here Reuven never performed valid meshichah into his own domain or a simta. His earlier attempt to lead the cow through the gentile's courtyard, and the later taking by a gentile transporter, do not constitute proper meshichah or mesirah. Moreover, there is no agency for a gentile. Therefore, according to Rabbenu Tam, Reuven never acquired the cow.

Even according to Rashi, Tur, and other authorities who hold that money does acquire from a gentile, Reuven may still not have acquired it. He did not pay cash; the gentile merely offered the cow in satisfaction of an existing debt. A debt generally does not function as money for acquisition even in contexts where money would otherwise acquire, such as renting land or triggering mi shepara with movable goods. Shach writes this regarding land as well. It is therefore quite possible that even Rambam, who sometimes treats a debt as money, does so only between Jews, not in purchase from a gentile.

<br><b>Furthermore,</b> there is an additional uncertainty: every acquisition requires intent to acquire by that act. Here Reuven did not say that the debt should presently effect acquisition. The gentile initiated the proposal, and after inspection Reuven merely said that he would accept the cow under the stated arrangement, language that can refer to a future transfer. It is possible he intended acquisition only later, after calving. Thus there are multiple doubts: perhaps money does not acquire from a gentile; perhaps an existing debt is not equivalent to money; and perhaps Reuven did not intend to acquire through the debt at all. Nor was there valid mesirah, since the owner never told him to take possession in that manner and the earlier movement occurred without the owner's knowledge. The later gentile transporter could not serve as Reuven's agent because a gentile is not a valid agent for a Jew.

<br><b>Beyond this,</b> even if we assume Reuven did acquire the cow from the first gentile through money, he later sold it to the second gentile using essentially the same mode of acquisition, namely money. Therefore, either money acquires in transactions with gentiles, in which case Reuven first acquired the cow and then the second gentile acquired it from him; or money does not acquire, in which case Reuven never acquired it from the original gentile and it remained the first gentile's property throughout. Either way, the calf should not be treated as a firstborn belonging wholly to a Jew. The fact that the second gentile gave only a deposit does not defeat the sale because Avraham guaranteed the balance. A guarantor appointed at the time of the transaction does not require a kinyan sudar, and in addition the arrangement may have had the force of ma'amad sheloshtan because Avraham himself owed the purchaser money. Thus the parties clearly relied upon the transaction.

<br><b>The conclusion</b> is that the calf should be ruled not to have firstborn status. At minimum, even if one treated it as a doubtful firstborn, the salting and cooking vessels that had already become eino ben yomo by the time the question was discovered may be permitted. The hide mixture is also only rabbinic after one hide became mixed among many of its kind, since biblically one against two of the same type is nullified. Pri Megadim permits where a Torah doubt became rabbinic before the uncertainty was discovered, particularly in a case of loss, even if the earlier ignorance was due merely to the owners' lack of awareness. Levushei Serad writes similarly. Here these grounds permit in any event. Even if the doubt had become known before mixture, once the hide entered a one-to-two same-type mixture we may rely on those authorities who hold the original monetary transaction with the gentile was ineffective and the calf was never a firstborn, just as analogous egg-blood mixtures are permitted. Levushei Serad discusses these principles at length.
