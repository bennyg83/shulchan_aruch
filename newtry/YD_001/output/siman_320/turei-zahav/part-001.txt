**** ENGLISH ****
<b>One part in a thousand.</b> Beit Yosef cites Tosafot at the beginning of Bechorot that even a partnership in the ear can suffice. Tosafot at the end of Avodah Zarah, however, write that one should be stringent and transfer to the gentile a part whose absence would render the animal treifah, such as the lung or head. The gentile need not pay its full proportional value. Acquisition for even one perutah suffices despite the part being worth much more, because we clearly know the Jew fully intends to transfer ownership in order to exempt the animal from firstborn status. Derishah asks why this differs from priestly gifts in siman 61, where if the gentile owns only the head, only the cheeks are exempt, and so on. In my opinion there is no difficulty. Regarding a firstborn, Scripture says "the first issue of the womb in Israel," from which we derive that the entire animal must belong to Israel. Similarly, regarding theft, "and he sells it" is expounded to require sale of the whole. Thus if a significant part is removed from Jewish ownership, the entire firstborn status falls away. With priestly gifts, however, there is no such Scriptural linkage. Wherever the gentile has a share, only that corresponding gift is exempt; the remaining gifts are independent.

<b>Or he should receive money, and so forth.</b> Responsa Mahari Segal, siman 174, rules that if the sale was made by only one mode of acquisition, either money or meshichah, the offspring remains a doubtful firstborn even after the fact, because we do not know which view to follow. Responsa Rama, siman 87, says this requirement of both money and meshichah is only initial. After the fact we certainly follow Rabbenu Tam: since the poskim rule that money acquires from a Jew, meshichah acquires for a gentile. Therefore, if the Jew never pulled the cow from the gentile, it plainly remained the gentile's property. There is no distinction between a gentile purchasing from a Jew and a Jew purchasing from a gentile. Piskei Mahari, siman 130, adds that although transferring ownership of an unborn fetus normally fails because it is not yet in the world, where the cow is already pregnant and the fetus is certain to be born there is some basis for effectiveness, analogous to the rule in Perek HaMafkid where one is treated as saying, "My cow shall be acquired by you immediately before it is stolen." If another factor for leniency exists, such as evidence that the cow had previously been milked, it may be combined. Mahari further writes that if the gentile undertakes to feed the cow in exchange for the calf, that undertaking is like payment of money for the calf.
