<b>Another.</b> But those who bury the deceased in a cemetery close to the city and then return from the city gates do not begin mourning until they are told that burial has taken place. This matters, for example, when burial occurs on Erev Yom Tov. Shach. Another practical consequence concerns the custom, mentioned above in siman 374, that parents do not accompany their first child to burial. If this occurs on Erev Yom Tov, they do not begin counting from the moment they turn back from the funeral procession but only when they are informed that burial took place. If the burial notification comes before twilight with some time remaining, the festival cancels the seven-day period. If they are told only during twilight, they must observe seven days after the festival. The standard here for a "distant place" is not a fixed mileage but a place distant enough that those remaining behind do not know when burial will occur. This follows Rambam. Ramban writes more generally that when the deceased is taken from one city to another, once the relatives hand him over to those transporting him and turn away, they have despaired of further involvement, and that despair is like closing the golel. Ramban then says Rambam appears similar. Behag gives a different standard, that once the deceased leaves the city and is beyond the relatives' immediate involvement, mourning takes effect even if only one hour remains before the festival. Beit Yosef rules like Rambam. Radvaz likewise follows Rambam and Ramban, but adds that if burial itself occurs during twilight before Shabbos, mourning takes effect even for relatives who did not accompany the bier and the day counts as day one. Likewise, if it is Erev Yom Tov, the festival cancels the seven-day period even if the relatives themselves return only after Shabbos or Yom Tov has begun. See also Responsa Darkei Noam and Raanach.

<b>Who are here.</b> Shach writes that Ramban makes clear that this applies specifically if the head of the household can reach the grave within three days. If he cannot arrive within three days, those remaining at home begin counting immediately, while the head of the household who is at the cemetery begins only when the golel is closed, and they do not follow him. We follow the head of the family only for stringency, meaning that those at home do not begin until burial where the law requires following him. If the head of the family remains with those at home, however, his presence does not change the rule for those who accompanied the deceased; they still begin only when burial occurs. Tur, by contrast, holds that everything follows the head of the household. If he remained at home, both groups begin counting from the time the deceased left. Shach writes that Ramban's position appears otherwise; see there.

<b>And some say.</b> Shach discusses these laws at great length. His practical conclusion is as follows. One whose relative dies and who learns of the death in his own location begins counting for himself from the time he learns, even if he later travels to the place of death and joins relatives who began mourning earlier. He does not shorten his own mourning because of them. Likewise, if he joins relatives who are neither at the place of death nor burial, each person counts from the time he personally learned. But if he did not know of the death until he arrived among relatives at the place of death or burial, and he came from a nearby place, defined as within a journey of ten parsah, then even if he arrives on the seventh day, if he still finds comforters with the head of the household, even if they have already begun to rise to leave, he counts together with them. Since the head of the household counts with them, he too is drawn into their count. If the head of the household is not there, he counts for himself. Likewise, the head of the household himself, when arriving from a nearby place, counts for himself. If at the time of burial some relatives accompanied the deceased to the cemetery, those who went count together with those who remained at home, whether the mourners are older or younger, whether the cemetery is near or far, and whether they return within three days or only on the seventh day, provided they return while comforters are still present. If they return on the seventh day after the comforters have already left, those who went to the cemetery count for themselves from the closing of the golel. Likewise, if a relative arrives from elsewhere and finds that the head of the household had gone to the cemetery, once the head returns, even on the seventh day, and counts with those at home, the newcomer is drawn after him as well, provided the newcomer came from a nearby place. This applies where some relatives remained at home. If no relatives remained at home, he counts for himself unless he arrived within three days. If the head of the household does not return within the seven days and therefore counts for himself from the closing of the golel, then one arriving from a nearby place likewise does not count with those at home from the time the deceased left, but begins for himself from the time he learned. All this concerns a head of household who went to the cemetery in a place where he does not live. If he went to the place where he lives and remained there, then even those who stayed behind and turned away from the deceased follow him and begin counting from the closing of the golel. This applies only if he can reach that place within three days. If he cannot, those remaining at home begin immediately, while the head of the household at the cemetery begins at closing of the golel. If those at home and the head of the household began their mourning at the same time, whether from the departure of the deceased or from closing of the golel, then one arriving from a nearby place counts with them in every case, even if he arrives on the seventh day while some mourning is still observed, provided the head returned before the comforters rose and is counting with them. But if they began together and the head later went to the cemetery to attend to the deceased's needs, such as erecting a monument or building the grave, and he did not return before the newcomer arrived, or returned only after the comforters had already risen, then if the newcomer arrived within three days he counts with them, while if he arrived after three days he counts for himself. If the head went away for unrelated business rather than the needs of the deceased, even a newcomer arriving within three days counts for himself if the head did not return home. If the newcomer came from a distant place, he counts for himself in every case. Whenever the law is that he counts with the others, he continues to count with them even if he later returns to his own home.
