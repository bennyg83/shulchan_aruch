<b>When a report emerged.</b> See Baer Heitev, seif katan 7, that the rule refers to circumstances strong enough to permit the wife to remarry. Chinuch Beit Yehudah, siman 91, discusses a man who disappeared and whose fate was unknown. He ruled that the sons should not recite Kaddish because that could create the same misleading impression as formal mourning. Another sage disagreed and distinguished Kaddish from mourning, but the author defended his ruling and several great authorities agreed. He nevertheless recommends acts that benefit the missing person's soul without creating the same concern, such as leading Birkat HaMazon, receiving maftir, occasionally leading prayers when doing so does not displace actual mourners, giving charity, or hiring Torah study on his behalf. Shevut Yaakov, part 2, siman 114, questions this from Ketubot 62, where R. Yannai ordered beds overturned based only on an inference concerning death, even though such evidence would not permit remarriage. He distinguishes between a husband who disappeared to an unknown place, where mourning is not observed without evidence sufficient for remarriage, and one who traveled to a nearby place where the truth can become known during the mourning period; in the latter case people need not be prevented from mourning because the matter is readily discoverable. Knesset Yechezkel, end of siman 53, writes that although Shulchan Aruch says wife and children are not allowed to mourn, once it is publicly known that responsa are being sent to rabbis concerning the case, he permits the sons to arrange a minyan at home and recite Kaddish. This itself creates publicity and avoids the concern that an outsider will later testify vaguely that the man died. He likewise does not prevent the wife and sons from wearing ordinary weekday clothing during that time, provided they do not wear formal mourning garments. Chatam Sofer, siman 344, discusses an unmarried youth lost in water without visible boundaries. Since there is no agunah concern, should mourning be observed? R. Moshe ben Chaviv in Ezrat Nashim takes it as obvious that one still does not mourn, lest one create a secondary decree, and Shach appears similar. Shevut Yaakov, part 1, siman 103, strongly holds that one does mourn an unmarried person. Chatam Sofer explains that the dispute is not whether such probability ordinarily justifies mourning, but whether to extend the protective decree beyond the agunah context. Therefore, one who follows the lenient view regarding the decree and mourns has a substantial basis. Mishkenot Yaakov, siman 69, goes further and argues that many Rishonim maintain that whenever death is established by a majority probability, mourning is required even if the evidence would not permit remarriage. He notes an apparent tension with siman 339, seif 2, concerning a relative reported dying, and explains that the sources themselves reflect differing views. Responsa Maharam, Raavan, Rosh, Tur, Mordechai, and Rashba all support mourning based on a majority probability. Thus there is substantial basis to mourn even for one lost in water without visible boundaries, especially where a body was found and identified by appearance and clothing signs but the testimony is not yet technically complete for remarriage. Kaddish is even less directly comparable to mourning, as Shevut Yaakov notes.
