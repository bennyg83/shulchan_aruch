<b>And they permit.</b> Radbaz, part 2, siman 741, writes that the custom is to be stringent and treat the gravestone itself as included in the prohibition of benefit. Birkei Yosef recounts a widow who had a stone placed on her husband's grave and later wished to replace it with a finer one. She proposed giving the original stone back to the stone seller and adding money for the new one. The question was whether this is permitted since she personally gains no worldly benefit and is spending more for her husband's honor, or forbidden because the seller takes the old stone as part of the price and later profits from selling an item prohibited in benefit. His teacher ruled that she may neither sell nor exchange the stone in any manner because this is legally benefit. R. Yosef Navon, Kiryat Melech Rav, part 2, siman 27, inclined to permit it in theory but not in practice. Chatam Sofer, siman 335, discusses stones from an old cemetery wall that collapsed and was rebuilt with a better wall. Could communal leaders use the old stones to build an entrance structure for a hospital, given the stringent custom regarding gravestones? He permits this plainly for several reasons and rules that the gabbaim, who function like the seven communal leaders, may redirect the old stones and bricks to another public need.

<b>A grave that was found.</b> Chavot Yair, in the responsum of R. David Oppenheim, discusses a synagogue whose building collapsed. When foundations were dug to rebuild it, human bones and several graves were discovered in irregular positions around the site. Some were spaced farther apart than the measurements of an ordinary burial complex, others unusually close, and no wooden boards, pottery, or recognizable grave structures were found. An elderly gentile speaking incidentally reported that gentiles had been buried there during a Swedish war. Several questions arose: whether the site must be treated as an established cemetery, whether the remains could be moved, whether the area needed full examination to protect kohanim from tumah, how extensive the excavation must be, and who should bear the expense. After a lengthy analysis of Rashi, Rambam, Tosafot, and Ri, he concludes that the site need not be treated as an established Jewish cemetery. Nazir teaches that where burial differs materially from Jewish burial practice and suggests gentile burial, we do not establish cemetery status from doubt. Here the irregular positioning, absence of customary Jewish burial indicators, and presence of multiple bodies in one grave all support gentile burial. Moreover, Masechet Semachot states that burials made in wartime or plague conditions do not create ordinary cemetery presumptions, and the incidental gentile testimony further supports the conclusion because the prohibition against moving graves is rabbinic. The surrounding area therefore need not be examined for tumah, kohanim may walk there, and the earth around the bones need not be removed as part of the corpse. Even if the remains were certainly Jewish, earth attached naturally to the ground is not prohibited in benefit; all the more so here, where they were probably gentiles. Building a synagogue there is also not considered prohibited benefit because mitzvot were not given for personal enjoyment. Still, out of reverence for a sacred house of prayer and to remove all concern, he advises excavating and examining the entire synagogue footprint to approximately the normal grave depth and removing any bones found. Outside the synagogue no further examination is necessary. A spot where removed bones were reburied should either be moved again or clearly marked so kohanim avoid it, because even definite gentile graves are preferably avoided and here there remains some possibility of Jewish burial. The cost of the synagogue excavation should be borne by the entire community, including Israelites and Levites, since communal infrastructure expenses are shared, Jews too are subject to tumah concerns on pilgrimage, and they are obligated to enable Birkat Kohanim. See also Even Shoham, simanim 10 to 12.

<b>A grave for his father.</b> Shivat Tzion, siman 58, discusses someone who ordered a gravestone for his father and had the father's name engraved upon it, but circumstances prevented placement of the stone for several years and the grave's location was eventually forgotten. Later the son himself died. Could the son's children erase the grandfather's name and use the stone on their father's grave? One rav prohibited, comparing a gravestone to a grave itself, which Rama treats as prohibited in benefit. He nevertheless suggested the heirs might sell it for another deceased person's grave, like a grave excavated for one's father. Shivat Tzion agrees that it should not be used for the son's grave but disputes the proposed sale. The statement that another person may be buried in a grave excavated for one's father refers to a grave dug in ownerless land or a public cemetery where anyone already has burial rights. It does not mean that a son who dug a grave for his father on his own property may sell or give that grave away. The same applies here, so the gravestone should not be sold. Even though the son has now died, grandchildren remain obligated in their grandfather's honor according to Rama. Nevertheless, Shivat Tzion concludes that one should not criticize a rav who permits selling the unused gravestone for another deceased person, because major authorities support leniency: Rosh holds a gravestone does not become prohibited at all, Tosafot holds that where no prohibition took effect there is no separate paternal-honor bar, and Maharik holds grandchildren are not obligated in a grandfather's honor. The entire prohibition is likely rabbinic. He further notes that Rambam, Tur, and Shulchan Aruch phrase the law specifically as "one who excavates a grave for his father," following the first Tanna rather than Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel's broader baraita concerning stones prepared for one's father, and this depends on whether the rule that the halachah follows Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel applies only to Mishnah or also to baraita.
