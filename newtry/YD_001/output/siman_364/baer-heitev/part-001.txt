**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And Rambam.</b> Shach explains Rambam as follows. A grave that is "found," meaning one whose existence was previously unknown, may be cleared, because presumably the body was buried there without the landowner's permission. After it is cleared, the surrounding place remains impure where there is concern for additional graves. By contrast, a known grave, where it is known that only that grave is present, may not be cleared. If it was cleared, its place is pure because it is known that no other graves are there. Beit Yosef explains likewise.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Who fell.</b> Maharil writes that if blood flowed from the deceased, he should not be washed because the blood would thereby be removed; rather, he is buried in the clothes he was wearing. Bach writes that this applies specifically where he died while still wearing those clothes. If he fell but did not die immediately, and only died several days later after removing those clothes, and no blood is then flowing because the bleeding stopped and he died in bed as a result of the fall, he is washed and given shrouds like any other person who died in bed. This differs from someone killed by a gentile. Even if, when the body is found, the bleeding has stopped, he is buried as found in order to arouse indignation and vengeance, a consideration not relevant to one who fell from a roof. Likewise, if a woman dies in childbirth while blood is flowing from her, she is not washed but buried in her clothing. If she dies only after several days or weeks and the bleeding has already stopped, she is treated like other deceased people, washed and dressed in shrouds. Bach further explains the underlying reason concerning one who was killed: all his blood has already left him and is absorbed into his clothing, and perhaps a revi'it of the blood through which the soul departed is absorbed there. He is therefore buried as found and even his shoes are not removed, lest the life-blood be absorbed in them. For this reason, when someone is killed, people dig up the earth at the place where the body was found and collect any blood that spilled nearby, burying the blood-soaked earth with the deceased, lest it contain the blood through which the soul departed. The same reason applies to one who fell from a roof or was crushed by a building and suffered wounds from which blood flowed, and likewise to a woman who died in childbirth, since many die because internal organs were injured in labor and the blood absorbed into the garment may include blood shed at the departure of the soul. But where there were no wounds and no blood at all, such people are not buried in their ordinary clothing; rather, they are washed and given shrouds like other deceased persons. Accordingly, one who dies on the road from severe cold should have his clothes removed, be washed, and be dressed in shrouds, because he has no wound and no blood flowed. The same applies to one who drowned while dressed. Maharil writes similarly. Shach notes that in many communities he saw the practice to wash every woman who died in childbirth, dress her first in shrouds, and then place her ordinary clothing over the shrouds, with the sheet above them.
