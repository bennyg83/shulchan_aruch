**** ENGLISH ****
And the Sages already forbade, etc. — these are the Rambam's words in chapter 8 of Laws of Meat, for he ruled like Rav that meat that disappeared from sight is forbidden; and so is the view of Rif and many authors. Only the Rambam adds: even if found in the hand of a gentile — and this is at first glance against the Gemara, which challenges Rav who forbids meat that disappeared from sight from that which we learned: nine shops, etc., and regarding found one follows the majority; and the Gemara answers: found in the hand of a gentile is different — and according to all it is permitted. And the Rav Maggid Mishneh already addressed this and wrote that the Rambam followed the Jerusalem Talmud, which states that even in a gentile's hand, once it disappeared from sight it is forbidden; and the Rambam adopted the Jerusalem Talmud's words and set aside our Gemara's sugya — and this is what is astounding, that he is against the world's sugya and the pillar of ruling is to follow our Gemara's words and not heed the Jerusalem Talmud. And here I say: were it not for the Maggid Mishneh and those who came after him, I would say that the Rambam's intent does not disagree with our Gemara; rather they said in the Gemara that Israelite slaughterers must announce if there is treifah, and the authors gave the reason that the norm of the law among Israelite slaughterers is that one may buy from a gentile, for certainly most are kosher and from the majority it separates — only that if treifot happen to an Israelite slaughterer they would not be permitted to sell to a gentile lest he sell it to an Israelite; therefore they enacted announcement. And in a place where they do not announce it is forbidden to buy from a gentile; and the Rambam holds that certainly by law one should not forbid found in a gentile's hand because of meat that disappeared from sight, for he did not remove his eyes from it; but the Sages saw fit to forbid any meat not supervised by an Israelite — what did they do? They thought to nullify the announcement so they would not announce, so that automatically there would be no permission to buy from a gentile; therefore the Gemara says for the law where they would announce: found in a gentile's hand is permitted and there is no concern of meat that disappeared from sight. But in the Jerusalem Talmud Rav forbids — meaning: a pretext was found so they would not announce, and meat fell to the butchers; and if so, automatically it is forbidden by law; and this is what the Rambam wrote, that the Sages forbade all meat because it has no announcement at all, as he never mentioned announcement anywhere in the Rambam. And it must be said: since it is forbidden because it disappeared from sight, the announcement is nullified — this is what I would say. But the Maggid Mishneh does not appear from his words to hold thus; rather he holds simply: since meat that disappeared from sight is forbidden, how does announcement apply — therefore the Rambam omitted announcement, for it is not in reality; and in such a case where one stands and sees and buys from butchers and does not remove his eyes — this is not a case for which they would enact announcement; and if so, the challenge returns to its place, that in the Gemara we say that in a gentile's hand meat that disappeared from sight does not apply; and likewise in the Gemara in many places they say 'they announced' and 'they did not announce' — this is not in reality; and see below s.k. 10 what is written there. And what I would say briefly on this is that there are two aspects in the matter: certainly according to Rashi's explanation, regarding found in a gentile's hand removal of sight does not apply, for presumably the gentile did not remove his eyes; however according to what the Maggid Mishneh wrote explaining the Jerusalem Talmud, there is more concern for a raven regarding found in a gentile's hand, since otherwise there is a side of doubt perhaps he took treifah from the shops — see there. And even though the Rambam wrote 'even if all sellers are Israelites' — nevertheless it deals in a place where they do not announce, as written; and if so, what difference does it make that the Israelite slaughterer perhaps slaughtered treifah and sold to the gentile — for it is permitted to sell from this; one may say: if there is no treifah at all in the shops and all are Israelites — then even the Rambam agrees it is permitted, as appears from the Maggid Mishneh's words; and they did not forbid only because of concern for a raven. And with this the Rambam's words in Mishnah Shekalim are understood, that he explained: pieces are permitted — meaning in a city that is entirely Israel; but if there is a gentile in it — forbidden because of meat that disappeared from sight. Taz wondered: in an Israelite city too; and according to what I said it is settled: in a place where there is a gentile we are concerned lest Israelites sold him treifot, not so when entirely Israel — there is no concern, and they were not concerned for meat that disappeared from sight — requires further study, for it appears that in his composition the Rambam retracted — requires further study. And it appears that this depends on two views among the authors in that which we say: everything that separates from the majority is presumed to have separated from the majority — whether this is definite and decisive with no measure of doubt at all, and we are witnesses that from the majority it separates, as Tosafot imply in the first chapter of Ketubot and in many places; and accordingly the Maggid Mishneh's view in what he wrote that for doubt of a raven we also combine doubt that perhaps he took from treifah shops — for behold most shops are kosher and from the majority it separates — if so, this is certainly from kosher shops and there is no doubt at all. However according to what I said in Asifas Zekenim in the first chapter of Bava Metzia regarding 'one of the appointed leaped into it,' etc., and he wrote in the name of the Rosh: even though we say that from the majority it separates — nevertheless it is doubt, only the Torah permitted this doubt — see there. And accordingly the Maggid Mishneh's words are clear: even though most shops are kosher — nevertheless there is doubt perhaps treifah separated. And it appears that in these views Rav and Shmuel disagreed at the end of the first chapter of Ketubot and chapter 9 of Yoma, whether we follow the majority for pikuach nefesh or not — for Rav explains from the majority idolatry, and one does not desecrate Shabbat for it, for from the majority it separates and an idolater is certainly present; and this is because he holds like the aforementioned Tosafot view that there is no doubt at all here. But Shmuel holds we do not follow, etc., and we desecrate Shabbat for him — it implies he holds that nevertheless it is doubt, and doubt of pikuach nefesh overrides Shabbat; for if there were no doubt here, from where in the Torah would we desecrate Shabbat for him. And it is not difficult that our Gemara's sugya follows Rav, who holds there is no remnant of place for doubt at all, as I wrote — therefore it answers well regarding found in a gentile's hand we are not concerned for a raven, for he did not remove his eyes, as Rashi explained; and the Maggid Mishneh's view that there is also doubt perhaps because of treifah — is not so, for according to Rav there is no doubt at all. But the Jerusalem Talmud follows Shmuel's conclusions above, from which it is proven that he holds that nevertheless there is doubt in it; and if so, the Maggid Mishneh's view is correct from the outset to be lenient regarding a gentile's matter, where there is also doubt perhaps from treifah shops; and we who rule like Shmuel rule like this Jerusalem Talmud, and the Rambam's words are clear — requires further study. In the second case it appears to me from ancient times what they challenged greatly from that which we say there in the Gemara, and this that Rav said meat that disappeared from sight is forbidden was not stated explicitly, but from his inference — for this man was a tanna to the first part: he threw one and brought up two; Rav said to him: such things happen here and we forbid. They said in the Gemara: if from his inference — what? And they answered: there were gentile hides — know that his prohibitions are more common, end of his words. Be that as it may, Rav did not say explicitly that meat that disappeared from sight is forbidden, but inferred from the inference of the aforementioned incident; and the Gemara challenged him that one cannot learn from it; and see Tosafot who challenged that perhaps we do not learn from this because he did not remove his eyes, and Rav pushed it off. If so, according to the Gemara everywhere one cannot rule like Rav, since we do not find it stated explicitly from him, only by inference — and it is difficult on Rif and Rambam and other authors who ruled meat that disappeared from sight is forbidden — from where is this, since Rav never said it; and it requires further study at first glance, for I did not find in any author who addressed this. And what I would say must be thus: certainly in the essence of the law that meat that disappeared from sight is forbidden — it is implied explicitly from Rav, who said limbs found in the borders are permitted, and Rav explained because of nevelot, but prohibitions stand — meaning Rav. In several places Shach wrote s.k. 7 in the name of poskim and Taharat HaKodesh that any person, even if he is not a Torah scholar, is believed; however, specifically regarding pieces that he saw well from the beginning, end of his words. And behold it does not imply thus in the Gemara and Tosafot there Chullin daf 105: we say Rava said — at first I thought siman is preferable to teviyat ayin, for we return a lost object by siman and not by teviyat ayin; now that we heard these teachings they say: nevertheless teviyat ayin is preferable, etc. And Tosafot challenged in the section 'and not,' etc.: that which we do not return a lost object by teviyat ayin — there the reason is lest he lie; and for a Torah scholar in truth we do return; and emend: that which we return for a Torah scholar — this is complete teviyat ayin and not any teviyat ayin; and those incidents in the Gemara were not complete teviyat ayin — for if complete teviyat ayin, obviously why need questioning; and nevertheless they permitted — behold Tosafot's view is to permit in the case of meat that disappeared from sight even with inferior teviyat ayin — meaning: that he did not see it well from the beginning (as those amoraim are not accustomed to see it from the beginning and to examine closely). And so the poskim; and Yeshuot Shmuel: this is called teviyat ayin — any teviyat ayin that he did not examine initially; and nevertheless they declared it kosher — and this is clear, not like Shach. And behold Tosafot still did not explain all that is needed to settle the difficulty: if teviyat ayin is preferable to siman — why permit this meat with any teviyat ayin; why not return a lost object with any teviyat ayin for a Torah scholar, where there is no concern he lies. And it appears the Rav Taz had this intent: otherwise there is no place for his challenge, that perhaps we do not return a lost object by teviyat ayin because we are concerned he lies; rather his intent is as above — inferior teviyat ayin; and emend to distinguish regarding the prohibition of meat that disappeared from sight that they were lenient. But if so, how do we say teviyat ayin is preferable to simanim — behold the opposite: here we rely only on prohibition, and on siman everywhere we rely. And Taz's words are in any case obscure, for he did not bring Tosafot's words at all. And what appears is inferior teviyat ayin — meaning there is another object that also has teviyat ayin like this one, with slight difference between them; therefore one who saw it from the beginning and goes down to examine to the finest point feels and discerns the difference between them, even though their teviyat ayin seems equal; but one who did not see from the beginning cannot understand the difference and errs easily between object and object. And one may say regarding lost meat: we are concerned a raven exchanged it and by chance another piece happened such that it too is close to the first one's teviyat ayin — we are not concerned this happened with raven exchange that precisely a piece equal in teviyat ayin happened; not so object and teviyat ayin — one may well say perhaps some similar object happened. And how can we give him based on doubt. In these words Mordechai's words are understood plainly, who wrote an incident where he gave a chicken to a gentile, and the chicken owner alone had teviyat ayin and no other — and they declared it kosher, for we do not rule like Rav that meat disappeared from sight is forbidden. And Beit Yosef wondered: what difference to him whether the halacha is like Rav or not — since he recognizes it by teviyat ayin, according to all it is permitted; and the later ones stumbled in explaining him. And it appears plainly: certainly this is teviyat ayin that the chicken owner had — teviyat ayin of any kind; complete teviyat ayin is not in the category of questioning, as Tosafot wrote. And behold in the Gemara we find, as Tosafot wrote, that they permitted even with any teviyat ayin; however one may say: this is in a majority of Israelite slaughterers, where there is no complete prohibition concern, only concern that a raven exchanged this one — here they were lenient even with any teviyat ayin, as I wrote. But if there is concern of exchanging meat with treifah in a place where most slaughterers are gentiles, found in a gentile's hand — by law forbidden in this; one may say we do not rely on any teviyat ayin; however this is if we rule like Rav that meat disappeared from sight is forbidden; if so, one may say all those amoraic incidents were in such a case and they permitted because of teviyat ayin; but regarding found in a gentile's hand and most bakers are gentiles — we do not rely on inferior teviyat ayin; but if we do not rule like Rav — then those who lost meat were presumably among a majority of gentile slaughterers, and if so behold this is a doubt of prohibition, and nevertheless they permitted with inferior teviyat ayin, as Tosafot wrote — it implies even there it helps. And behold the chicken that was in a gentile's hand, and regarding birds most slaughterers are gentiles, for each slaughters for himself at home — and there is good concern for exchange of good for bad, and it is a complete doubt as the later ones wrote; if so, one may say the teviyat ayin the chicken owner had does not help regarding a clear prohibition; therefore Mordechai says: since we do not rule like Rav to forbid meat that disappeared from sight — therefore those incidents were among a majority of gentile slaughterers where there was complete prohibition concern, and nevertheless they permitted with teviyat ayin; if so, even for a chicken, even though there is complete prohibition concern — nevertheless we rely on teviyat ayin. And it is not difficult; and Mordechai's words are clear. Here at the conclusion of this siman I said I would write what the Rav Peri Chadash challenged on the Rambam, and I intended to write on this in siman 1 regarding that which we rule R' Chanina's view that majority and near follow the majority, and Tosafot's view as implied from the Gemara's words, even in a proven nearness; and they addressed the Rambam, that accordingly the Gemara's challenge in a bird's nest should be in a majority, etc., and we should follow the world's majority; and we need an answer in 'one who dwells among mountains,' etc. However the Rambam in Laws of Forbidden Foods omitted it; and likewise 'found,' etc., that the Gemara also challenges — and we should follow the world's majority; and it answers: because of vineyards, etc.; and the Rambam in Laws of Robbery and Lost Objects omitted this 'because of vineyards'; and likewise the barrel found in an orlah vineyard that he permitted — and the Gemara says: even according to the one who says we follow nearness, nevertheless this is permitted because in that garden it is not hidden; and the Rambam wrote this reason. Even though according to R' Chanina we need not this, for we follow majority even where there is nearness — all these the Rav Peri Chadash challenged. And I add to ask in Beitzah regarding doves of a dovecote in season two, and he found three — the Gemara says: let us say it supports R' Chanina: majority and near follow the majority; and the Gemara pushes off on daf, etc. — this is if R' Chanina does not exist; but for R' Chanina we need not establish it on daf; and the Rambam in Laws of Yom Tov brought 'on daf'; and the main point: he wrote season two and found three — behold this is a prepared doubt; and if we follow the majority even where there is nearness, there is no doubt here, for certainly it came from outside and they are certainly not from the prepared ones; and therefore it must be said the Rambam does not hold this law of R' Chanina for halacha — requires further study. And many things were said in unraveling these challenges. And the clearer shorter way is what we say in chapter 2 of Niddah: found in the corridor — its presumption is impure that it came from the source; and Rav Huna said: found from the alley inward — certainly impure; from the alley outward — doubtfully impure. And Abaye challenged in both ways: if we follow concern — therefore from alley outward is doubt, lest from the room, and it was thrown from the alley inward is also doubt, lest from the roof, and perhaps it urinated; and if we follow presumption — here certainly impure and here certainly pure. And Abaye answered: truly, if we follow concern — here and here is doubt; and if we follow presumption — here certainly impure and here certainly pure. And the Rambam holds to settle the difficulty. For Rav Huna the challenge is presumption, and he holds R' Huna's view that R' Chanina's 'majority and near follow the majority' means where there is no proven nearness; but where there is proven nearness it is only doubt whether to follow nearness or majority — and see Tosafot Bava Batra in our sugya 'they fell,' etc. — see there. Therefore R' Huna holds: from alley inward, even though the roof is near the room, majority and proven nearness do not exist — for if we say it came from the roof, it must be that it urinated outside the room; and what proven nearness is there. But from alley outward the difficulty is resolved, for there severing the cord does not harm, for it already passed between the sections; and so Tosafot Eruvin daf 7 (and Rama wrote in siman 57 that a vertebra removed even where severing the cord does not disqualify — nevertheless disqualifies; and Lechem Mishneh wrote: I do not know his source; and from what is written the matter is compelled; and at that time Tosafot Eruvin were hidden from him). But according to the above that Beit Shammai concede treifot, there is no proof here that Beit Shammai hold treifah does not depend on tumah; and in truth, even though treifah is from severing the cord — nevertheless it defiles only in deficiency of the spine itself — halacha leMoshe miSinai of two vertebrae; if so, it is still not compelled to say at the end of Keifli; if so, Levush should not have challenged and counted among Beit Shammai's leniencies — one may say: Beit Shammai concede treifah with two vertebrae in one vertebra, but nevertheless it does not defile until two vertebrae are lacking; but now that it answers at the end of Keifli — it is difficult how it teaches anonymously; it must be said that otherwise it would be treifah from deficiency and cord severance — nevertheless it does not help Beit Shammai until the spine is lacking; and accordingly it is not so, for one depends on the other; if treifah it does not defile — it challenges well and one may count, etc. And if so, also the above difficulty is resolved — there is no need for small ribs, how does it conclude it deals with them, for there too between sections severing the cord disqualifies. But with all this, one may answer before weighing, for we lean stringent in every case, since the essence of treifot depends on uprooting half a vertebra; and what we challenged — why does the Gemara cut off that count specifically and count among Beit Shammai's leniencies — one may say another way: they said 'so that it be taken from the living and die' — there are two explanations: (1) it dies and is not already dead, which is nevelah with death; accordingly both hold one vertebra — treifah, two — nevelah, and on this Beit Shammai hold specifically deficiency must be nevelah, and Beit Hillel hold treifah suffices; if so, no difficulty, and count leniencies, etc., for they did not disagree regarding treifah at all. But now that it establishes at the end of Keifli and challenges how anonymously — it must be said that when a vertebra is removed it is gistera and nevelah; and the mishnah implies only treifah; accordingly the above is unnecessary and it challenges well — count and requires further study.
