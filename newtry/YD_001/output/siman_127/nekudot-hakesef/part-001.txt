<b>(סימן קכ"ז בש"ך ס"ק י"א) ומלמדים אותו וכו'. </b>ול"נ דהאי מלמדים אותו הוא כמו פתח פיך לאלם ור"ל שאומרים לו אם אינך יודע אמור איני יודע והטעם הוא דלמ' הבע"ד אינו יודע הדין אם אומר איני יודע דמהני משום הכי שותק הבע"ד ואילו הוי ידע הדין שאינו יודע מהני הוי אמר איני יודע ע"ז קאמר מלמדים אותו לומר איני מאמינך ר"ל איני יודע וק"ל:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>(Siman 127, Shach, seif katan 11) And they instruct him, etc.</b> It appears to me that 'they instruct him' is an application of the principle, 'Open your mouth for the mute.' This means that they tell him: if you do not know, say, 'I do not know.' The reason is that perhaps the litigant does not know the law, namely, that saying 'I do not know' is effective, and for that reason he remains silent. Had he known that saying 'I do not know' is effective, he would have said so. Accordingly, when the text says that they instruct him to say, 'I do not believe you,' it means that he does not know. This is clear.

<b>(שם בש"ך ס"ק י"ג) קשה דמאי מהני וכו'. </b>הגאמוז"ל לשיטתו אזיל שכתב בס"ק י"ד כשבע"ד אינו בפנינו אפילו בלא העד המכחיש אין העד שאומר נתנסך נאמן ע"ש ול"נ דס"ל להרב דכשאומר העד שלא בפני בעל דין שנתנסך כאלו לא העיד כלום ולמה ליה להכחישו וגם הלשון הכחשה שייך בדבר ברור שמכחיש אותו שמדבר שקר אבל אם אין מכחישו ש"מ דמהני ובזה מתורץ ג"כ קושית הגאמוז"ל בס"ק י"ד ועוד תימה רבא ע"ש. אבל אחר העיון היטב ליישב גם מה שהקשה בס"ק י"א ובס"ק י"ג נ"ל לפרש הג"ה על מכונו ונקוט האי כללא בידך דהרב סובר אם העד הוא גברא מהימן ליה דהיינו שמאמינו אסורה לו וכ"כ בת"ח כלל ך' דין ו' וז"ל אם בא ראובן והגיד לפני שמעון שראה דאפילו הבעלים שותקים לאו כלום הוא ולפע"ד נראה דאין מהר"ן שם ראייה דהתם בקידושין שאני דהא אפילו מודה האשה שקדשה בינו לבין עצמו אינו כלום לר"פ עד שיהא שם ע"א מש"ה בע"א מכחישתו אין עדותו כלום משא"כ בשאר איסורין דנאמן הבעל על שלו וכן מ"ש ואין שיטה זו נכונה וכו' י"ל דכוונתו גבי קדושין שאני כן נ"ל ברור לפע"ד ודוק היטב:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>(Ibid., Shach, seif katan 13) This is difficult, for what does it accomplish, etc.</b> The gaon, of blessed memory, follows his own view, for he writes in seif katan 14 that when the owner is not before us, even if there is no opposing witness, the witness who says that the wine was used for a libation is not believed; see there. It appears to me, however, that the Rav maintains as follows. If a witness states outside the owner's presence that the wine was used for a libation, but says that the owner himself was present when this occurred, the witness is believed even though he now testifies outside the owner's presence. Since he says that the event occurred before the owner, perhaps the owner would admit the witness's account were he present. This is certainly so according to the Rashba and those who follow him, who do not require the witness to say, 'It occurred in your presence.' Even though the Rav rules in seif katan 16 that the witness must say, 'It occurred in your presence,' that expression is equivalent to saying that it occurred before the owner, since the owner is not presently before us. As for the proof brought from Issur VeHeter in seif katan 13, it appears to me that the opposite is implied there, namely, that the witness is believed even outside the owner's presence. Issur VeHeter writes that any person can contradict him. If the testimony were worthless, it would be as though he had never testified, and there would be no need to contradict him. Moreover, the term 'contradiction' properly applies where one directly asserts that the other is speaking falsely. This implies that if no one contradicts him, his testimony is effective. This also resolves the gaon's difficulty in seif katan 14 and the further great difficulty that he raises there. After careful examination, it also appears to me that the difficulties raised in seif katan 11 and seif katan 13 can be resolved by explaining the gloss according to its proper meaning. Keep this principle in mind: the Rav maintains that if the witness is a trustworthy person whom the owner believes, the item becomes forbidden to that owner. He writes similarly in Torat Chatat, rule 32, law 6: if Reuven tells Shimon that he saw non-kosher fish mixed with kosher fish, then if Shimon believes Reuven, Shimon may not purchase them; but if Shimon says, 'I do not believe you,' he may purchase them, etc. However, Tosafot, etc.; see there. The gloss should therefore be read as follows. If the owners do not contradict the witness, the contradiction of another person does not help. This resembles the case in which one witness says that a person ate forbidden fat and another says that he did not eat it, where the person brings a provisional guilt offering because his heart troubles him. Here too, if the owner believes the first witness, the item is forbidden to him, and an owner is believed to forbid his own property. Consequently, it is forbidden to others as well, because the wine became forbidden through the owner's acceptance of the witness. If the owners are not present, however, the contradiction does help. Were no one to contradict the first witness, and were we to believe him, the item would be forbidden, as stated above in the name of Torat Chatat. Here, however, another witness contradicts him and the owner is absent, so the contradiction is effective. Some maintain that the contradiction of one witness is nothing. Since we believe the first witness, the contradiction of the second does not help even when the owners are absent. This view is derived from the Rashba's statement that one witness against another is nothing, as stated in Kiddushin, etc. The Rashba says that when the owner contradicts the witness, the witness cannot forbid the owner's property. This implies that the reason is specifically that the owner himself contradicts him, whereas if a second witness contradicts him, the first witness remains believed, as explained. The phrase, 'although there is a response to his words,' should be understood as follows. The Rashba cites Rabbeinu Tam's still broader ruling that the owner may say, 'I do not believe your words,' although there is a response to Rabbeinu Tam's position. This means that the Rashba does not agree with Rabbeinu Tam that once the owner says, 'I do not believe you,' the testimony becomes entirely ineffective. According to the Rashba, the contradiction of one witness is nothing. When the owner himself contradicts the first witness outside the witness's presence, the contradiction is likewise ineffective if we believe the first witness. When the owners are present and remain silent, however, the Rashba agrees that the item is forbidden because their silence constitutes admission. As for the Ran's view, cited to the effect that even if the owners remain silent the testimony is nothing, in my humble opinion there is no proof from the Ran. The case in Kiddushin is different, because even if the woman admits that she was betrothed privately, the betrothal is ineffective according to Rav Pappa unless two witnesses were present. Therefore, when there is only one witness and she contradicts him, his testimony has no effect. Other prohibitions are different, because an owner is believed concerning his own property. Likewise, the statement that this approach is incorrect can be understood as referring specifically to Kiddushin. This appears entirely clear to me; examine it carefully.
