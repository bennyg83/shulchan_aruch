<b>So that people should not say, etc.</b> There. See Tosafos there, s.v. “Forbidden relations,” etc. Rambam, however, does not accept their explanation, for he maintains that relatives on the father's side are permitted, as stated in seifim 2 and 3. So it appears there. This is incorrect, for even according to Rambam one must adopt Tosafos' explanation. A mother's sister and the wife of a maternal brother are permitted to a non-Jew but forbidden to a convert, necessarily because of possible confusion. According to Rambam, relations with one's mother and maternal sister are prohibited because people might say that one left a more severe sanctity for a lesser sanctity, whereas a mother's sister and the wife of a maternal brother are prohibited because of possible confusion. Here the Mechaber listed only those women who had already been forbidden to him while he was a non-Jew. The practical difference is that with those women he must divorce even after the fact, whereas with the latter women he need not do so, as stated in seif 2. See what I wrote there.

<b>How so, etc.</b> Rambam writes in chapter 9 of Hilchos Melachim that six forbidden relations apply to Bnei Noach: his mother, his father's wife, a married woman, his maternal sister, a male, and an animal. This is derived from the verse, “Therefore a man shall leave his father,” meaning his father's wife; “and his mother,” literally; “and cleave,” but not to a male; “to his wife,” but not to another man's wife; “and they shall become one flesh,” excluding an animal. Scripture also states, “She is indeed my sister, the daughter of my father, but not the daughter of my mother, and she became my wife.” These are Rabbi Akiva's words in Sanhedrin 58a. Yet according to Rabbi Akiva one's sister is permitted, as explained there on 58b. See Rashi there, s.v. “Come and hear,” and also, etc. Rather, Rambam ruled in accordance with the Sages on 57b. A baraisa supports Rabbi Yochanan: “Every forbidden relation,” etc.; and the Sages say, “Many,” etc. It appears that the word “many” should not be read there. Rather, the text means: there are offenses for which a Jewish beit din, etc. This is the case explained at the end of the baraisa, “If he came,” etc. The only such cases are his sister and a betrothed maiden. The reference to a betrothed maiden requires study. The prohibition of his sister is derived from “Therefore a man shall leave,” etc. Rabbi Akiva must interpret that verse with difficulty, whereas the Sages explain it according to its plain meaning.

Toras Kohanim on Parashas Kedoshim states concerning “A man,” etc., “It is a kindness.” Lest you say that Kayin married his sister, Scripture states, “It is a kindness,” teaching that the world at its beginning could be built only through kindness. This accords with Sanhedrin 58b: “Come and hear: Why,” etc., and not with the Gemara's rejection there, and not with Rashi's explanation on 57b, s.v. “Many,” etc. Ramban likewise writes this, except that he maintains that all maternal relatives are forbidden according to the Sages. The only difference between Ramban and Rambam is a mother's maternal sister, as Ramban explains.

There are twenty two forbidden relations. Four are not based on kinship: a male, an animal, a married woman, and a niddah. All are forbidden to a non-Jew except niddah, whose prohibition does not apply to them. Eighteen are based on kinship, as stated at the beginning of Yevamos: fifteen women, etc., and six forbidden relations, etc., totaling twenty one together with the other categories. A brother's wife is counted there three times: a paternal brother's wife, a maternal brother's wife, and the wife of a brother who was not yet born. A sister is counted twice, a paternal sister and a maternal sister. Seven relations are therefore one's own relatives, seven come through one's wife, and four are wives of relatives whom the husband makes forbidden to his wife.

For Bnei Noach, all seven relations arising through one's wife are permitted, because marriage does not create this network of kinship for a non-Jew. Likewise, the four wives of relatives are all permitted, except a father's wife, which Rabbi Akiva derives from Scripture. According to Rabbi Eliezer, even those are permitted. Of the seven personal relatives, all paternal relatives are permitted, as stated in chapter 11 of Yevamos, 98a, because a non-Jew has no halachic father. Therefore, Sanhedrin 58b states that a non-Jew is permitted to his daughter, and certainly to his granddaughter through either a son or daughter. Rabbi Akiva likewise permits a father's sister for this reason, although Rabbi Eliezer includes her through a Scriptural derivation.

Ramban maintains that even Rabbi Akiva prohibits a mother's sister. He writes that the word “saying” includes all maternal relatives. Rambam, however, maintains that even according to the Sages no maternal relatives are forbidden beyond one's mother and maternal sister, who are explicitly stated. This is parallel to Rabbi Meir's understanding of Rabbi Akiva. Rabbi Meir and the Sages disagree only about one's sister and about the interpretation of the verse, as the Gemara cited above explains.

Sanhedrin 57b asks, “Does Rabbi Meir maintain that every,” etc. Ramban explains that the question addresses only the latter clause, which states that a ben Noach is not warned, etc., whereas here the baraisa teaches that his sister, etc. The Gemara does not question the father's wife, because the Sages did not decree other paternal relations upon converts. This differs from Rashi there, s.v. “And some teach,” etc. Proof is that the Gemara challenges specifically according to Rabbi Meir. According to Rashi's explanation, the same difficulty would apply to the Sages, who do not disagree about this point. The Gemara answers: “This is not difficult; this is Rabbi Meir,” etc. The baraisa therefore also does not follow the Sages, who disagree only regarding his sister, because they necessarily agree with Rabbi Akiva, as is evident from their conceding to Rabbi Meir concerning offenses punishable by a Jewish court.

In the dispute between Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov and Rabbi Shimon in Yevamos 97b, Rambam rules like Rav Acha bar Yaakov and the first version, unlike Rif. His reason is the statement on 98a: Rava said, “That which the Sages said,” etc., which implies that even rabbinically a convert has no paternal relationship. The Gemara continues that two twin brothers, etc. Accordingly, every relationship containing a paternal element is permitted initially: a father's maternal sister, a mother's paternal sister, and a paternal sister. Although the baraisa there prohibits all of them, it was already explained as following Rabbi Eliezer, who maintains that a father's sister is forbidden to a ben Noach. According to Rabbi Akiva and the Sages, every relationship containing a paternal element is completely permitted. Ramban and Rashba likewise write this.

A mother's maternal sister, however, is rabbinically forbidden to a convert although permitted to a ben Noach. The statement that a non-Jew has no father implies that maternal kinship does exist. Maternal brothers are likewise forbidden, as explained above. Paternal kinship created through marriage is permitted, even a father's wife, according to all opinions, except for the wife of a maternal brother, as explained above. This follows the same rule as one's own relatives. The Gemara's exchange, “What, is it not,” etc.; “No,” etc., concerns only a brother's wife. It does not support Raavad's explanation that it concerns every prohibition arising through marriage, for the Gemara also lists the wife of a father's brother, who is certainly permitted even if she married him after he became a convert. This is Ramban's explanation.

There is a further distinction between the baraisa and the Sages. According to the baraisa, one who married his mother's maternal sister must divorce her. According to the Sages, he may remain married to her, just as Rabbi Meir permits one to remain married to his father's paternal sister, since the prohibition is only because of possible confusion. The same applies to a mother's maternal sister according to Rabbi Akiva and the Sages, and certainly to the wife of a maternal brother. This is Rambam's approach. Accordingly, his words, “If he was married,” etc., refer to the other relatives, etc.

<small>(Collected note)</small> See what I wrote there. Rambam's general rule is as follows. For a ben Noach, his mother, maternal sister, and father's wife are forbidden, while all other relatives are fully permitted, in accordance with the Sages who disagree with Rabbi Meir. For a convert, every paternal relationship is completely permitted, whether it is his own blood relationship or one created through marriage. He may therefore marry even his father's wife, and in this respect his status is more lenient than that of a non-Jew. Conversely, every maternal relationship is forbidden to him, whether personal or created through marriage. Thus, among his own relatives, his mother and his mother's maternal sister are forbidden. Through his wife, his wife's maternal sister, his wife's daughter, her daughter's daughter, his mother-in-law, and his mother-in-law's mother are forbidden, as stated in seif 5 concerning one who marries, etc.; “the same applies,” etc. The same rule applies to these relations, as Tosafos and Ramban write. Through marriage, the wife of his maternal brother is also forbidden. These total nine forbidden relations.

Ramban's approach regarding a convert agrees with Rambam, but regarding a non-Jew he writes that according to all opinions every maternal relation is forbidden, every paternal relation is permitted, and ordinary marital kinship creates no prohibition. The only exceptions are those specifically enumerated: a father's maternal sister according to Rabbi Eliezer, and a father's wife according to Rabbi Akiva. Rabbi Meir, according to Rabbi Akiva, prohibits only maternal relatives whose violation is punishable by a Jewish court, whereas Rabbi Eliezer and the Sages include even relations punishable by kares. Rashi follows another approach. See Ramban's discussion. <small>(End of collected note.)</small>

<b>And some prohibit, etc.</b> This follows Rashi's explanation cited above, according to which the Gemara also challenges from the case of a father's wife and answers, “This is Rabbi Meir,” etc. See Tosafos in Yevamos there, s.v. “His father's wife,” etc.; “However,” etc. The Tur likewise rules this way, and it is proper to be stringent in accordance with both opinions. In all of this, there is no distinction between one who was conceived before conversion and born after conversion, one who was both conceived and born before conversion, or one son who was conceived before conversion, etc. Ramban agrees with this, and Rambam and Shulchan Aruch likewise state the law without qualification. See Tosafos there, s.v. “He married,” etc.

<b><small>(Collected note)</small> And some prohibit a father's wife.</b> This is taken from the Tur, as stated above. The Rav, however, did not examine the matter carefully here, for the Tur disagrees extensively with Rambam. Since the Tur also rules like Rabbi Eliezer, he prohibits one's sister, father's sister, and mother's paternal sister, as stated in the baraisa there. The paternal side is lenient only in relationships created through marriage, such as the wife of a paternal brother, and similarly the paternal sister of a female convert's husband, as Tosafos write and as explained above. Furthermore, one who married his sister, father's sister, or mother's maternal sister must divorce her, as stated there. He must likewise divorce his father's wife, because the Tur rules in accordance with both opinions. In addition, he rules like Rabbi Shimon that a relative connected through both the father and the mother is forbidden just like one connected through the mother alone, unlike Rambam, who writes concerning two twin brothers, etc. <small>(End of collected note.)</small>
