**** ENGLISH ****
<b>To this one, to this one, to this one, etc.</b> This is without the conjunction “and,” so it is as though he said, “Konam to this one, konam to this one, konam to this one,” since he need not say “konam” explicitly for each individual. Beit Yosef writes in the name of Rivash, in responsum 291, that one who vowed or swore not to eat grapes and not to wear silk garments, and then sought annulment for one of them, the second prohibition is not released according to Rambam. It appears from his words that the same applies to one who says, “I swear that I will not eat and that I will not drink”: if he seeks annulment for one, the second is not released. However, if he swore not to eat figs and grapes, or not to drink wine, milk, and honey, and he sought annulment for one of them, the others are released, unless his fellow was pressing him when he swore. Beit Yosef concludes that it has already been explained that some disagree with Rambam, and Darkei Moshe cites this. It appears that if he said, “Konam grapes upon me; konam figs upon me,” even those who disagree with Rambam would regard these as two separate vows, since he said “konam” twice, as below, siman 238, seif 36, in the gloss. The Gemara likewise establishes the Mishnah in the final chapter of Nedarim, 87b, which states, “Konam, if I taste a fig, and grapes, if I taste them, these are two vows,” in accordance with Rabbi Shimon. See there. Ran writes there that according to Rabbi Shimon, who requires an oath for each individual, here too they are not considered two vows unless she says “if I taste” regarding each one. But if she said, “Konam if I taste, neither fig nor grape,” Rabbi Shimon regards it as one vow.

**** HEBREW ****
<b>ודוקא בכה"ג כו'. </b>כלומר דוקא בכה"ג שנדר מתחלה ואח"כ אמר על הנדר שאם ישאל עליו לא יהא יהודי אין להתיר משום שאין מתירין נדר שיש בו נדנוד עבירה אבל אם אמר אם אעשה דבר פלוני לא אהיה יהודי או אפי' אמר בפירוש אם אעשה דבר פלוני יכפור בהש"י אין זו לא שבועה ולא כינוי ולא יד הואיל ואינו מוציא שום רמז שבועה או נדר מפיו וכך מבואר בפסקי מהרא"י סימן קנ"ב וכן בד"מ ודלא כהעט"ז שפירש דבאומר אינו יהודי שזה הלשון משמע שאם ישאל לא יעשה מעשה יהודי לפיכך אסור לישאל לו על נדרו שעושה עצמו לכופר אבל אם אמר אם אעשה דבר פלוני לא אהיה יהודי אפי' היתר אין צריך שזה לשון משמע אם יעשה דבר פלוני שאינו יהודי בעצמותו והיאך ישנה את הידוע שהרי רואים אותו שהוא יהודי עכ"ל ולא כוון יפה:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Specifically in a case such as this, etc.</b> Meaning, specifically where he first made a vow and afterward declared concerning that vow that if he sought annulment for it he would not be a Jew, the vow may not be annulled, because one does not annul a vow containing an element of transgression. But if he said, “If I do such-and-such, I will not be a Jew,” or even explicitly said, “If I do such-and-such, I will deny Hashem,” this is neither an oath, nor a substitute expression for an oath, nor an incomplete formulation of one, since he uttered no wording that even alludes to an oath or vow. This is explicit in the rulings of Terumat HaDeshen, responsum 152, and in Darkei Moshe. This is unlike Ateret Zahav, who explained that when one says he will not be a Jew, the wording means that if he seeks annulment he will not act as a Jew, and therefore one may not annul his vow, since he thereby makes himself into a denier. But, according to Ateret Zahav, if he says, “If I do such-and-such, I will not be a Jew,” he does not even require annulment, because that wording would mean that if he performs the act he will cease inherently to be a Jew, and how could he alter a known reality when everyone sees that he is a Jew? This explanation is incorrect.

<b>ועוד יתירו על מה שאמר וכן לעולם. </b>שאע"פ שלשון וכן לעולם כולל לאין תכלית אי אפשר שיתרבה הנדר מטעם זה ולפיכך כל שהותר ושנה ושלש אי אפשר שיהא כאן נדר א' שלא חל אלא כל נדריו חלים וחכם יכול להתירן ואע"פ שמקצת איסורו עתיד עדיין לחול החכם עוקר הנדר מעיקרו ר"ן:

<b>They must also annul what he said, “and so forever.”</b> Although the phrase “and so forever” extends without limit, the vow cannot multiply endlessly for that reason. Therefore, once it has been released after he repeated it a second and third time, it is impossible that only one vow exists here that never took effect. Rather, all of his vows take effect, and a sage can annul them. Although part of its prohibition is still destined to take effect later, the sage uproots the vow retroactively. Ran.
