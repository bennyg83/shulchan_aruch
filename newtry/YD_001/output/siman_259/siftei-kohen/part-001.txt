**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Or Torah study.</b> In Responsa Rosh, kelal 13, siman 14, the Rosh himself concludes: ‘There is no prohibition here on account of designation. Even according to the opinion that designation has legal effect, that applies only where one weaves a garment for a corpse. This case resembles yarn dyed for weaving, which all agree has no legal effect.’ This requires study. Even if this were a full designation, it is nevertheless permitted to transfer something from a lesser sanctity to a greater sanctity, for one may convert an actual synagogue into a beit midrash, as stated in the Gemara and explained in Orach Chaim, siman 153. Furthermore, when money has been prepared in this manner, it certainly constitutes a full consecration, for otherwise it could be redirected even to a lesser sanctity, as implied in Orach Chaim, siman 153. The Rosh likewise wrote in his responsum, siman 53, page 77, side 3: ‘Should you ask: When money was collected to build a synagogue or beit midrash, or to purchase a Sefer Torah, why may it be redirected only from a lesser sanctity to a greater one? It has not yet been used for a sacred purpose. Likewise, when we say that once the money reaches the treasurer it may no longer be redirected, there has still been only designation. The answer is that collecting money for a synagogue or beit midrash is an actual deed, not mere designation.’ See there. The Bach likewise wrote this in Orach Chaim, siman 153, seif 3. In truth, however, the Tur and Beit Yosef did not cite this concluding passage from the Rosh's responsum. This requires study.

<b>וכל זה במקום שאין מנהג ידוע כו'. </b>ואז אפילו טוען המקדיש טענת ברי שלא הקדישו לאוכל כמיניה לאפוקי מחזקת הקדש אלא א"כ התנה ברבים או בפני עדים בשעה שהקדיש כ"כ מהרש"ל בתשובות סי' ט"ו ומביאו הב"ח וכתבו שם כן גם בס"ת ולמדו כן מדברי מהרי"ק שורש קס"א גבי כלי כסף של הקדש ע"ש אבל בתשובת מהר"י כהן מקראקא סי' כ"א כתב שחלוקים הם כי ס"ת שדרך ומנהג העולם להניח בב"ה וידוע דשם האב נקרא על הס"ת א"כ מי יוציאה מחזקת האב כי מה שהניח הס"ת לשם אין ראיה שהיא של הק"ק כי בזמנים אלו עיקר כתיבת ס"ת להניח לקרות בס"ת ברבים וגם קדושתה גדול ואין ראוי בכל מקום להניח ס"ת ולכן אין ראיה כי אף שהניחו הוא לשם בב"ה אין ראיה שהקדישו אבל הכלי כסף אין המנהג להניחם בב"ה ובאם לא הקדישוהו לא היו מניחים אותם בב"ה רק היו משתמשים בב"ה בשעת הצורך ואחר כך היו לוקחים לביתו וכתב מהרי"ק בשורש קס"א שכלי כסף הנ"ל שהניחהו שם הם בחזקת הק"ק וגם בס"ת כתב בשורש ע' שהוא בחזקת הבעל ששמו נקרא עליו ר"ל על מי שאומרים זה ס"ת של פלוני מטעם הנ"ל והאריך שם בענין מי ראוי להעיד כי בני העיר נוגעין בעדות אם לא יסתלקו וגם שיהא שם גם כן ס"ת אחרת וכן כתב הטור סי' ל"ו שכולן נוגעים בעדות וסברותיו נכונים מתיישבים על הלב וגם במה שחלק בין כסף לס"ת סברא נכונה היא כי קשה להוציא דבר מחזקת בעליו אם לא בהוכחה מבוררת הארכתי בדין ס"ת כי שמעתי שרב אחד פסק גם בס"ת שהיא של הקדש לכן הוצרכתי להביא ראיה ולהאריך בראיות וטעמים דלא כוותיה ע"כ:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>All this applies where there is no established custom, etc.</b> In such a place, even if the donor claims with certainty that he did not consecrate the item for communal ownership, he cannot remove it from its presumptive consecrated status unless, at the time of the donation, he expressly stipulated this publicly or before witnesses. Maharshal wrote this in Responsa, siman 15, and the Bach cites him. They wrote the same regarding a Sefer Torah, deriving it from Maharik, shoresh 161, concerning consecrated silver vessels. See there. Responsa Mahari Kohen of Krakow, siman 21, however, distinguishes between the cases. A Sefer Torah is ordinarily placed in a synagogue, and the donor's father's name is known in connection with it. Who, then, can remove it from the father's presumptive ownership? The fact that he placed the Sefer Torah there does not prove that it belongs to the congregation. In our times, the usual purpose of writing a Sefer Torah is to leave it where it can be read publicly. Its sanctity is also great, and it is not fitting to keep a Sefer Torah in every location. Thus, placing it in a synagogue does not prove that he consecrated it to the congregation. Silver vessels are different, since the custom is not to leave them permanently in a synagogue. Had they not been consecrated, they would have been brought to the synagogue only when needed and afterward returned to the owner's home. Maharik therefore wrote in shoresh 161 that silver vessels left there are presumed to belong to the congregation. Regarding a Sefer Torah, he wrote in shoresh 70 that it remains in the presumptive ownership of the person whose name is associated with it, meaning the person of whom people say, ‘This is so-and-so's Sefer Torah,’ for the reason explained above. He also discusses at length who may testify in such a case, since the townspeople have a personal interest in the testimony unless they formally remove that interest, and provided that another Sefer Torah is also available there. The Tur likewise wrote in siman 36 that all of them are interested parties with respect to the testimony. His reasoning is sound and persuasive. His distinction between silver vessels and a Sefer Torah is also correct, since an item should not be removed from its owner's presumptive possession without clear proof. I have discussed the law of a Sefer Torah at length because I heard that one rabbi ruled that even a Sefer Torah belongs to the consecrated property. I therefore found it necessary to bring proofs and reasons against his ruling.

<b>אבל במקום שנוהגין כו'. </b>כתב הב"ח ונראה דלפי מנהג אותם מקומות גם ב"ח גובה חובו מס"ת שבהיכל דכיון דבידו למכרו בשעת דחקו אף ב"ח יכול לגבות ממנו ודבריו צ"ע דלקמן סי' רע"ד סוף ס"ב פסק דאינו גובה חובו מס"ת כיון דרשאי להתפרנס מן הצדקה וא"צ למכור ס"ת א"כ גם ב"ח אינו גובה ממנה דלא עדיף ב"ח מחייו עכ"ל הרי דבריו סותרים זא"ז ולענין דינא נראין דבריו שבכאן עיקר וכ"כ הטח"מ סי' צ"ז בשם הר"י ברצלונ' וכ"פ בש"ע סוף סכ"ג דב"ח גובו חובו מס"ת וכן כתוב בתשו' מבי"ט ח"ב סי' קל"ח דף ס"ו ע"ד מי שהיה חייב לחבירו בשטר וה"ל ס"ת מונח בב"ה כמנהג ולא מצא ב"ח מקום לגבות מן הלוה יכול ליקח ס"ת כו' ע"ש:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>But in a place where the custom is, etc.</b> The Bach wrote that according to the custom of such places, a creditor may also collect his debt from a Sefer Torah kept in the aron kodesh. Since its owner may sell it in a time of financial distress, a creditor may likewise collect from it. His words require study, for below, siman 274, at the end of seif 2, he ruled that a creditor may not collect from a Sefer Torah, since the debtor may support himself from charity and need not sell it. Accordingly, the creditor should likewise be unable to collect it, for the creditor has no greater right than the debtor himself. Thus, the Bach's two rulings contradict one another. As a matter of practical law, his ruling here appears primary. The Tur, Choshen Mishpat, siman 97, likewise wrote this in the name of R. Yehudah of Barcelona, and the Shulchan Aruch ruled accordingly at the end of seif 23, that a creditor may collect his debt from a Sefer Torah. Responsa Mabit, part 2, siman 138, page 66, side 4, similarly discusses one who owed money by a written instrument and had a Sefer Torah kept in the synagogue in accordance with the custom. If the creditor finds no other property from which to collect, he may take the Sefer Torah. See there.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>We follow the custom, for anyone who consecrates, etc.</b> This requires study. The ruling is taken from Maharik, yet Maharik there appears hesitant and inclined to prohibit it. He wrote: ‘Regarding what you wrote, that such acts occur daily in those regions, since it is clear that sanctity takes effect upon the vessels by force of the laws of the Gemara, and you seek to remove that sanctity by force of custom, surely you know that custom does not override halachah unless it is an established custom instituted by the local sages, not merely by the multitude of householders, as the Mordechai wrote in the name of the Or Zarua at the beginning of the chapter HaPo'alim. He also wrote there that the custom must have been established before it can override the law of the Gemara. The Bach likewise followed the Rama's formulation. This requires study.

<b>אין יכולים לשנותו כו'. </b>כתב המרדכי פ"ק דב"ב בשם מהר"מ דצ"ל שכבר נשתמש בהן והדליקו בב"ה דאי לא הדליקו בב"ה אפי' לדבר הרשות מותר לדידן דקי"ל הזמנה לאו מלתא ונהי דהנודר אינו יכול לחזור בו ולעכבו לעצמו משהקנהו להדליק בו בב"ה דנעשה נדר מכל מקום הציבור רשאין לשנות אפי' לדבר הרשות עכ"ל ומביאו ב"י וד"מ וצ"ע בזה כי שאר פוסקים לא חילקו בכך ואדרבה מדחילקו בין נשתקע משמע דבלא נשתקע בכל גווני אסור לשנות ואפשר לזה השמיטו המחבר והרב האי דינא ונראה דהמרדכי מיירי כשהבעלים מסכימים בדבר וקמ"ל דמדקתני אסור לשנות משמע דאיסורא איכא במלתא אפי' הבעלים מסכימים והיינו דוקא בנשתמש בהן דאל"כ הזמנה לאו מלתא היא ודוק:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>They may not redirect it, etc.</b> The Mordechai, in the first chapter of Bava Batra, wrote in the name of Maharam that this must refer to a case where the items were already used and lit in the synagogue. If they were not yet lit there, then according to our ruling that designation alone has no legal effect, they may be redirected even to a non-mitzvah purpose. Although the donor may not retract and retain them for himself once he transferred them for lighting in the synagogue, because his pledge has become binding, the community may nevertheless redirect them even to a non-mitzvah purpose. The Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe cite this. The matter requires study, because the other authorities make no such distinction. On the contrary, from their distinction based on whether the donor's name has been forgotten, it appears that so long as his name has not been forgotten, redirecting the item is prohibited in every circumstance. Perhaps this is why the Mechaber and Rama omitted this ruling. It appears that the Mordechai discusses a case in which the owners consent. His novelty is that the teaching's phrase ‘it is forbidden to redirect’ indicates an actual prohibition even when the owners agree, but only after the items have already been used. Before use, designation alone has no legal effect. Consider this carefully.

Darkei Moshe commented: ‘It appears to me that the Rosh, and the Tur and Shulchan Aruch in seif 2, disagree, for they wrote that money designated for Torah study may not be redirected for the needs of a synagogue. In truth, Maharik wrote there that no authority could disagree with the Mordechai. Otherwise, the teaching should have distinguished within mitzvah purposes themselves, stating that one may redirect only to a mitzvah of equal importance, but not to a lesser mitzvah. A lamp and menorah are called mitzvah objects because they were given only to provide light for those praying from siddurim and machzorim, which serves the worshippers rather than the sacred object itself. It seems that he held that money donated for synagogue needs has a stronger status than an ordinary mitzvah object, even though the money itself bears no sanctity. The Beit Yosef cites Maharik, shoresh 128, who wrote in the name of a responsum of the Rashba that the ruling concerning a Jew who donated a lamp or menorah applies only where the synagogue already has sufficient lamps and lighting. He further concludes that even if the donor's gift increases the lighting and permits the synagogue to be lit more generously, the community may redirect it so long as the ordinary requirement would still be adequately met without his donation. <b>But one who, etc.</b> This requires study. The Rama appears to equate Responsa Rashba with the glosses of the Mordechai at the beginning of Bava Batra in the name of the abridged Or Zarua, distinguishing between the donor himself and a deceased donor's heirs. The heirs are considered to be in possession of the property, and therefore the rule that a doubt concerning prohibition is resolved stringently does not apply against them. Responsa Rashba, at the end of siman 656, however, does not appear to make such a distinction. The Rashba writes that if it is uncertain whether the donor intended to retract, logic dictates leniency for the heir, since the property is presumed to belong to its owner, and heirs stand in the owner's place automatically. The donor consecrated it only conditionally, and the poor, who claim that the condition was fulfilled, must prove it. This resembles the baraita in the chapter Mi SheMet: the owner may recover the property from them without proof, whereas they may recover it from him only with proof. Although this is a doubt concerning consecrated property, we do not apply the rule that a doubt concerning prohibition is resolved stringently. First, property consecrated for the poor is ordinary monetary property, and we tell them to bring proof and collect, as in the discussion in Chullin 79a concerning priestly gifts from an animal of mixed species. Accordingly, whether the consecration is for the poor or for a synagogue, whenever there is an initial doubt whether the consecration ever took effect and the opposing party has a presumption of ownership, we leave the money with its presumptive owner. Thus, in the chapter Mi SheMet, concerning a dangerously ill person who recovered and ordinarily retracts his gift, the Gemara asks what the law is if he consecrated all his property, declared all his property ownerless, or distributed all his property to the poor. The questions remain unresolved, and the unresolved questions there are treated leniently. The Rambam likewise ruled so. Even according to those who rule stringently there, this case should be treated leniently. There, the consecration certainly took effect, and the doubt is only whether recovery allows him to retract. Here, the doubt is whether the consecration took effect at all, and doubtful consecration cannot remove property from its owner. If the Rama's distinction were correct, what proof could the Rashba bring from priestly gifts and from a recovered donor, where no heirs are involved? Rather, there is no distinction. It is always treated as a monetary doubt and resolved leniently for the party in possession. Mahari likewise implies this explicitly in his rulings, at the end of siman 73, regarding consecration made through an asmachta. He writes that one cannot impose liability merely by applying stringency to a dispute among authorities concerning charity. In Bava Batra 148b the Gemara asks what the law is if a dangerously ill person consecrated, declared ownerless, or distributed all his property to the poor. The Or Zarua ruled that the property remains with its presumptive owner and the poor do not acquire it, since the questions concerning consecration remain unresolved. The Rosh, who ruled that the poor acquire it, relied on another reason, as is evident there. Responsa Rivash, siman 170, likewise makes clear that no distinction of this kind exists. The glosses of the Mordechai also require study. They cite the Sifra and Yerushalmi concerning leket, shikchah, and peah to prove that a doubt concerning charity is resolved stringently. Yet this baraita is cited in our Gemara in Chullin 134a, and Rava explains that the stringency applies because the standing crop already had a presumptive obligation of leket. This implies that without such a presumption, the doubt would be monetary and resolved leniently. Abaye likewise challenges from dough prepared before conversion, which is exempt from challah, whereas dough prepared after conversion is obligated. In a doubtful case it is obligated, and the Gemara answers that a doubt concerning prohibition is resolved stringently, whereas a monetary doubt is resolved leniently. Thus, a doubt involving gifts to the poor is a monetary doubt, not a doubt concerning prohibition. Otherwise, Rava would not have needed the reason that the standing crop had a presumptive obligation. I later found that the Ran, at the beginning of Nedarim 7a, discusses the Gemara's question whether a partial declaration is effective for charity, as where one says, ‘This coin is for charity, and this one also.’ The Ran cites the Rashba, who ruled stringently concerning peah and charity because the question remained unresolved, and the Ramban likewise ruled that every unresolved question concerning prohibition is resolved stringently. The Ran objects that the sugya at the end of the chapter HaZeroa VeHaLechayayim explicitly treats a doubt concerning money of the poor as a monetary doubt, resolved leniently for the defendant. He further cites Yoma 9a and concludes that a doubt concerning money of the poor is not considered a doubt concerning prohibition at all, but a monetary doubt resolved leniently. Their words therefore do not appear correct. This itself requires study, since in the responsum cited above the Rashba also treats the matter as a monetary doubt and resolves it leniently. Mahari's statement that the Rosh ruled the poor acquired for another reason also implies that he agrees that an ordinary doubt concerning the poor is monetary and resolved leniently. Yet at the beginning of Nedarim the Rosh wrote concerning the unresolved question of saying, ‘This coin is for charity, and this one also,’ that an unresolved Torah question is treated stringently. It appears that the Rosh needed another reason in Mi SheMet because the Gemara's question there also involved declaring property ownerless, and all agree that a doubt concerning ownerless property is monetary and resolved leniently, as the Ran wrote there. I later found that Responsa R. Betzalel, siman 15, cites Responsa Ran, siman 1, and Responsa Rivash, siman 160, who ruled that the matter is a monetary doubt and is resolved leniently. It appears there that this is the accepted law. The Mechaber and Rama likewise ruled plainly in Choshen Mishpat, siman 250, seif 3, regarding the question in Mi SheMet concerning one who consecrated all his property, that if he recovered he may retract. Even if the poor seized the property, it is removed from them, following the Rivash's rule that seizure does not help in an unresolved question of the Gemara. See Yam Shel Shlomo, Bava Kamma, chapter 1, siman 5. I later found that Responsa Mahari Weil, at the end of siman 173, writes: ‘Should you argue that because the woman instructed that the money be given to charity, the matter is one of prohibition and a doubtful prohibition is resolved stringently, as in Mi SheMet where the Gemara asks about a dangerously ill person who consecrated all his property and the question remains unresolved, and the Rosh wrote that doubtful consecration is not annulled, there the person consecrated all his property without qualification, so it is possible that he intended the consecration to remain whether he lived or died, and the consecration began during his lifetime. In our case, however, the woman expressly stated that the gift should take effect after her death. It may therefore be said that the charity never began at all, and the assets remain in the heirs' possession. This resembles Rav's rule that money which one cannot recover through the courts does not become consecrated when he attempts to consecrate it. Here too, upon her death, the assets are in the heirs' possession.’ These matters require study.

<b>These are the coins that your father hid, etc.</b> This means that he does not know where his father hid the money. The figure appearing in the dream shows him the money and says, ‘These are the coins your father hid.’ The account in Sanhedrin 30a likewise states that the dream figure came and said to him: ‘There are such-and-such an amount, they are in a particular place, and they are second-tithe money.’ This incident occurred, and the sages ruled that words of dreams neither raise nor lower, meaning they have no legal effect.
