<b>And specifically when he mixed them.</b> This is a major error. Semag initially says this according to Reish Lakish but afterward explicitly writes that according to R. Yochanan there is no difference whether the flour was mixed with their consent or not; the decisive point is whether it is ultimately intended to be divided. Sefer HaTerumah writes likewise. It cites the case of women who gave flour to a baker and asks from the Mishnah that dough jointly owned with another Jew is ordinarily obligated. Ri answers either that the baker mixed their flour without consent, or that they intended to divide it afterward. The Yerushalmi likewise records a dispute whether one who smooths another's grain pile without his knowledge creates an obligation. Reish Lakish challenges R. Yochanan from the women who gave flour to a baker, and R. Yochanan answers that dough made with intent to divide while still dough is exempt. When challenged from the law of a baker, he answers that a baker is different because he may fail to find buyers and end up making one dough. Reish Lakish's position corresponds to Ri's first answer and R. Yochanan's to the latter. Sefer HaTerumah therefore concludes that if two people or students combine their flour knowingly and do not intend to divide but to eat together, the dough is obligated according to both. According to R. Yochanan, even if their flour became mixed without their knowledge it is obligated where there is no intent to divide. Semag, Rash, and Rosh write the same, as explained in seif 1. Rama's ruling is therefore surprising because it follows Reish Lakish. Even Raavad, who at the end of chapter 6 rules like Reish Lakish against Rambam, does so only in the particular case he discusses. He brings proof from the Yerushalmi concerning a woman who wanted to make thin cakes and asked whether she could thereby avoid challah. R. Muna answered that it was forbidden lest she change her mind and make regular dough. From the Mishnah itself one can likewise infer that five women are treated differently for purposes of division, while one woman remains obligated despite dividing. Even on this point Raavad is an individual opinion, opposed by the earlier and later authorities. Rash and Rosh interpret the Yerushalmi's item as sponge-like cakes, as in siman 329, seif 4, and as Mechaber writes here in seif 2 concerning other people. All the more so here. Nevertheless, I am troubled by the general rule in seif 2, because at first glance it appears that even a person in his own home who intends to divide one batch into small doughs would be exempt. Tosafot in Berachot 37b and R. Yechiel appear to say this, and Raavad's use of the Yerushalmi about making thin cakes also points that way. The Yerushalmi's answer about a baker likewise seems to imply this. But that cannot be correct. If so, the loaves of a thanksgiving offering, where three matzot come from each tenth measure, would be exempt from challah, and similarly the wafers of a nazirite offering, contrary to the Mishnah and the established practice of our fathers to separate challah from doughs made for Shabbos and Yom Tov even when divided into many loaves. One must therefore say that the Yerushalmi's exemption concerns intent to divide among many different people. Shach writes this explicitly. Although Raavad's wording itself is difficult to fit this way, upon close examination even he need not be treated as disputing the general rule. Sefer HaTerumah, Semag, Mordechai, and the other poskim all formulate the law of intended division in cases of two students or similar separate owners. The Yerushalmi at the beginning of chapter 4 likewise distinguishes between connections for challah and for tevul yom. It says that where all pieces belong to one woman, items of the same species combine for challah, even though they may not be considered connected for tevul yom. Conversely, some things connect for tevul yom but not for challah, such as sacred meat that developed a crust and is ultimately intended to be cut apart for many kohanim. R. Yochanan says that dough made with intent to divide while still dough is exempt from challah. This must mean division among many people. If it meant division into portions for the same owner, the earlier case of one woman's dough would contradict it. We thus learn that although seif 1 compares two women who are not particular with one woman, the cases are not identical. A single woman's dough remains obligated even if she intends to divide it, while with two women the obligation exists only where they do not intend eventually to divide ownership. The relevant intent to divide is specifically while still dough, as the Yerushalmi says. Although Sefer HaTerumah and Semag sometimes say they intend "to eat together," that wording is not exact. Rashba and Beit Yosef clarify the point, and so does the Shulchan Aruch in seif 2. The later authorities agree.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Two who made.</b> This is the Yerushalmi in chapter 3, cited by Rash in chapter 4, Mishnah 1, and Rosh, siman 11. Raavad questions the law, and Rash likewise raises a difficulty and explains the case as one where challah had already been separated, meaning there was initially no intent to divide. Rosh, Rambam, and Tur, however, record the ruling according to its plain meaning. See siman 324, seif 2, in the gloss. The law specifically concerns intent to divide among many people, as explained above and as the Yerushalmi's distinctions concerning connection for challah show. This is also the meaning of Tur and Rama here in the gloss.
