Charity. Taz wrote: it appears that one should compare the maaser that people separate from monetary profit to the law of maaser ani of grain and fruits. Bach wrote that the maaser of our money has no obligation, neither d'oraisa nor derabbanan. I wonder at this, for in responsa 249 it is clear that the obligation is complete, as all poskim wrote. I will quote the Tur briefly regarding maaser ani, namely: A man and woman came to ask, etc. (as written in siman 251 seif 9)—when he distributes in the field there is no benefit-of-use for the owners, but what is distributed in the house has benefit-of-use for the owners, and he gives it to any poor person he wishes. From this it appears that they relied on why we compel the groom at the time of receiving his dowry to give maaser, for we find similarly regarding maaser ani that we extract from his hand against his will—only that they distribute the maaser to the relatives of the groom and bride, for they take precedence over other poor people in this. I saw some say that specifically from the dowry that the bride gives to the groom he separates maaser, but not from what the groom's father gives his son—and I wonder from where this distinction comes; is there an obligation on money? Rather it is a personal obligation. What difference whether his father or father-in-law gives it to him—certainly corrupt reasoning is one who says so. And if a father and son or two brothers who are partners, and one of them is poor, the other gives him his maaser ani; and two poor people who received a field in partnership—each separates maaser ani on his share and gives to his fellow, and vice versa. One does not pay a loan with it, nor pay rewards—for example, if his friend did him a voluntary favor, it is forbidden to pay his reward from maaser ani; but one may pay from it an act of gemilus chasadim, such as a recovery meal for a mourner, and one must inform him that it is maaser ani. And one does not redeem captives with it—meaning, when there is otherwise an obligation upon him, such as that they assessed upon him to redeem him; but if he is not obligated to redeem him otherwise, he may certainly give his maaser ani to this one, for there is no greater charity than this, for captives are entirely in this category. And one does not give from it charity that the townspeople assessed upon him, for he is already obligated in it—this is paying his debt. And they give it to a fellow townsman who occupies himself with communal needs and sustain him, and they send him the maaser ani by way of honor and as a gift—even though they do not send him charity from the charity fund, because it is undignified for townspeople to sustain their sage from charity; but maaser ani that they send him from the threshing floor is not conspicuous as maaser ani, but appears as an offering and gift by way of honor. And all this appears to apply also to maaser that they separate from profit that God gave to human beings. And above in siman 251 Beit Yosef wrote in the name of Mordechai that maaser ani is like one who separates charity—end of Taz's words and Shach.
