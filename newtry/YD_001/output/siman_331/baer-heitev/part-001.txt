<b>Charity.</b> Taz writes that the ma'aser people separate from monetary profit should be compared to ma'aser ani of grain and fruit. Bach writes that our monetary ma'aser is not obligatory either by Torah law or rabbinically. I am astonished, because at the beginning of siman 249 it is explicit that it is a full obligation, as all the poskim write. Taz and Shach then cite Tur's laws of ma'aser ani in summary. If a man and woman come asking for support, the rules of priority are as stated in siman 251, seif 9. When ma'aser ani is distributed in the field, the owner has no right to choose its recipient. When it is distributed in the house, the owner may give it to whichever poor person he wishes. From this Taz finds support for the custom of compelling a groom, when he receives his dowry, to separate ma'aser, just as ma'aser ani can be extracted from the owner against his will. The ma'aser may be distributed to poor relatives of the bride and groom because they take precedence over other poor people. Some say that ma'aser is separated only from the dowry given by the bride, not from what the groom's father gives him. Taz rejects this entirely. The obligation is upon the person, not upon the specific source of the money. There is no reason to distinguish whether the gift came from his father or father-in-law. If a father and son, brothers, or business partners include one poor person, the other may give him his ma'aser ani. Likewise, two poor sharecroppers may each separate ma'aser ani from his own share and give it to the other. One may not use ma'aser ani to repay a loan or to compensate someone for an ordinary favor. It may, however, be used for an act of kindness such as the first meal provided to a mourner, provided the recipient is told that it is ma'aser ani. One may not use it to redeem captives where he is already personally obligated to contribute a fixed amount, because that would be paying his debt. If he had no prior personal obligation, he may certainly give his ma'aser ani for redeeming captives, for there is no greater charity. One may not use it for a charitable assessment already imposed upon him by the city, because that too is paying a debt. It may be given to a communal scholar who occupies himself with public needs, supporting him and sending the ma'aser ani respectfully as a gift. Although such a scholar should not be supported openly from the communal charity fund, ma'aser ani sent from the threshing floor is not recognizable as charity and appears instead as an honorable gift. All these rules likewise appear applicable to ma'aser separated from financial profit granted to people by Hashem. Beit Yosef above, siman 251, cites Mordechai that ma'aser ani is comparable to money designated for charity.
