**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And the other lands.</b> See Tosafot, Kiddushin 36b, who explain why the Gemara needs a particular derivation. Tosafot in Avodah Zarah 59a write in the name of Ri that produce outside Eretz Yisrael was never biblically obligated. This accords with the Gemara there and with Rabbi's permitting Beit She'an in Chullin 6b and Caesarea in the Yerushalmi. Statements that tithes apply outside Eretz Yisrael, including Berachot 36a, Yom Tov 12b, and Bechorot 27a concerning terumah outside the Land, refer to rabbinic practice. Rabbenu Tam has another approach: only demai was exempt outside Eretz Yisrael, while definite untithed produce remained obligated everywhere. On his approach there is no difference between lands near or distant from Eretz Yisrael, because demai is exempt everywhere except places sanctified by those who returned from Babylonia. He also explains elsewhere that vegetables are especially exempt. See Tosafot in Kiddushin, Avodah Zarah, and Chullin. They conclude that nowadays produce outside Eretz Yisrael is exempt, based on the Yerushalmi in chapter 4 of Challah: the sages of the Diaspora separated terumot and ma'asrot until later authorities abolished the practice.

<b>Nowadays.</b> R. Yochanan and Reish Lakish in Yevamot dispute only according to R. Yose. According to the Sages, even R. Yochanan agrees that the current obligation is rabbinic. See Tosafot in Yevamot. The Yerushalmi at the beginning of chapter 6 of Sheviit expressly debates whether those returning in Ezra's time became obligated by Torah law or voluntarily accepted tithes. R. Yose bar Chanina derives a Torah obligation from the verse comparing the later inheritance to that of the ancestors, while R. Elazar says they accepted tithes upon themselves. The later Yerushalmi in Terumot follows R. Elazar, saying a pot that cooked terumah is treated according to the view that they voluntarily accepted tithes. Rash cites this, and many Yerushalmi passages proceed similarly. The Bavli in Pesachim likewise says to leave aside terumah nowadays because it is rabbinic. Rambam and Shulchan Aruch therefore rule that even in Ezra's period terumah and ma'aser were rabbinic. The statement in Yevamot that the Sages disagree with R. Yosi does not mean they hold the second sanctity was later abolished. Chullin establishes that the second sanctity was not nullified. Rather, R. Yosi derives a renewed Torah obligation in Ezra's time, while the Sages hold there was no renewed biblical obligation, only a rabbinic one that nevertheless did not lapse. Rambam further distinguishes the sanctity of the Mikdash and Jerusalem, which remained from the first sanctification, from the agricultural sanctity of the Land. This accords with Megillah, Zevachim, and Shevuot. <b>(Likkut) Nowadays.</b> Sefer HaTerumah likewise brings proof from Midrash Rabbah, Midrash Ruth, and the Yerushalmi that tithes in Ezra's time were accepted voluntarily. Makkot 23b also points this way, although Rashi explains differently. Sefer HaTerumah adds that even if acceptance creates a strong obligation, terumah nowadays is rabbinic because Ezra's agricultural sanctity did not create a biblical obligation. Yet the sanctity of the Temple and Jerusalem did not lapse. He marshals many passages in Zevachim, Megillah, Shevuot, Makkot, Temurah, and elsewhere for this distinction. Although some sugyot seem to connect the two sanctities, the halachah need not follow that linkage. This also resolves apparent contradictions among R. Yochanan, R. Yose, R. Yehoshua, and Reish Lakish. Sefer HaTerumah further writes that nowadays, since the agricultural obligation itself is rabbinic, gentile ownership in Eretz Yisrael has stronger power to remove agricultural obligations, comparable to Syria. The same applies to Sheviit nowadays. This resolves Tosafot's difficulty in Sanhedrin concerning sharecropping under taxation.

<b>A gentile... unlike.</b> These words are very difficult. Even where a Jew purchases land from a gentile together with produce that grew under gentile ownership, if it had not yet reached the tithing stage while under the gentile, it becomes obligated, even in Syria, as stated in seif 7. All the more so here. The difficulty comes from a misunderstanding of Rambam. Rambam says that a gentile who acquires land in Eretz Yisrael does not remove it from the mitzvot; if a Jew later repurchases it, it is not treated as a new individual conquest but remains obligated in terumot, ma'asrot, and bikkurim by Torah law as though never sold. He then separately states that in Syria gentile ownership does remove agricultural obligations. The first point means that according to the opinion that gentile ownership removes sanctity, a later Jewish repurchase would resemble individual conquest and be only rabbinically obligated, like Syria. The second point introduces the distinct laws of Syria that Rambam explains later. It does not create an exemption in the present case. Gittin 47a itself proves this: even in Syria a Jew who purchases from a gentile can become obligated. Rambam fundamentally rules that in Eretz Yisrael there is no gentile acquisition to remove sanctity, because R. Chanina and R. Yehoshua ben Levi follow that view in Bechorot. Apparent contrary passages can be explained as Syria or as R. Meir's position. The Yerushalmi differs in some placements, but Rambam's final Mishneh Torah ruling follows the Bavli. Produce remaining in gentile ownership is certainly exempt according to everyone, while produce purchased by a Jew can be obligated as explained. In Syria, by contrast, all agree gentile ownership has legal force, with practical consequences in seifim 7 through 10. Here there is little practical distinction nowadays because even Jewish produce in Eretz Yisrael is only rabbinically obligated according to seif 2.

<b>And likewise.</b> This is Tosefta Terumot, chapter 2. I will copy here the portions of the Tosefta relevant to this siman. In chapter 1 of Demai: one may not sow tevel, cover tevel with earth, or work together with a gentile using tevel. In chapter 7: if a kohen or Levi sold produce of his field to an Israelite while still attached to the ground, the tithes remain theirs. If he sold it detached before final processing, the tithes remain theirs; after final processing, they belong to the purchaser. The purchaser separates terumah and terumat ma'aser, gives them to a kohen, and keeps the remainder. If an Israelite sold produce to a kohen or Levi before final processing, the tithes remain the Israelite's; after final processing, they belong to the kohen or Levi, who separates terumah and terumat ma'aser and gives them to whichever kohen he wishes. One who gives his field to a gentile, Samaritan, or a person not trusted regarding tithes under a sharecropping arrangement must tithe on his behalf even if the produce had not yet reached the tithing stage. One who gives it to an am ha'aretz before the tithing stage need not tithe on his behalf, but once it has reached that stage he must. He stands at the threshing floor and takes what is required, without concern for what the other party already ate, because there is no responsibility for fraudsters. This is Rambam's version. Our printed text reverses the before and after clauses. In chapter 8: if a person has ten barrels of impure tevel ma'aser and sees one spill or become uncovered, he may designate it as terumat ma'aser for the other nine by name, but he may not do so where this would destroy the kohen's property. In chapter 1 of Terumot: a thief, robber, or violent taker can separate terumah and ma'aser from property in his possession and can consecrate it, unless the original owner is actively pursuing recovery; if the owner is pursuing it, their separation and consecration are ineffective. A sikarikon's separation is effective. A son, hired worker, slave, or wife may separate terumah for what they themselves eat, but not for the whole property, because one cannot separate from property not his own. A son may separate from his father's produce and a wife from her dough when they act with authorization. If a sharecropper separated and the owner objected, then if the objection came before separation it is ineffective, while if afterward it remains effective; the sharecropper may remove ma'aser only from his own share. A laborer who separates from the threshing floor without authority has done nothing. If the owner told him merely to gather the threshing floor, his separation is effective, because a threshing floor is not normally gathered before terumah is removed. Guardians may separate terumah and ma'aser from orphans' property and may sell houses, fields, vineyards, animals, servants, and maidservants to feed the orphans and meet their needs. In chapter 2: one may not separate from produce of Eretz Yisrael for produce of Syria or vice versa. An Israelite who purchases a field in Syria is like one who acquires land in the outskirts of Jerusalem and must separate terumah and ma'aser from it. If an Israelite and gentile jointly purchase a field in Syria, Rabbi says their shares are like tevel and ma'aser mixed together; Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel says the Israelite's share is obligated and the gentile's exempt. If an Israelite purchased a field in Syria and later resold it to a gentile, it remains obligated in tithes and Sheviit because it had already become obligated. But sharecroppers, fixed-rent tenants, hereditary tenancy arrangements, and a gentile who mortgaged his field to a Jew remain exempt from tithes and Sheviit even if the Jew executed a formal document upon the field. Orlah and kilayim of the vineyard apply equally to gentiles in Eretz Yisrael, Syria, and outside the Land, except that R. Yehudah says a gentile has no neta revai obligation in Syria, while the Sages say he does. One may not separate terumah from grain that has not yet reached one-third growth. How is this determined? If seed taken from it germinates when planted, it has reached one-third; if it does not germinate, it has not. In chapter 3: why did they say a mute should not separate? Because he cannot recite the blessing. Why should a blind person not separate? Because he cannot select the superior portion from the inferior. Why should an intoxicated person not separate? Because he lacks settled judgment, even though his transactions may be legally valid. Why should one with a seminal emission not separate? Because he cannot recite the blessing. Why should a naked person not separate? Because he cannot recite the blessing. He may, however, cover himself with straw, stubble, or another material and recite it. One who is about to separate terumah, ma'aser rishon, or ma'aser ani recites the blessing from the time he separates them. If after physically setting them aside he intends later to call them by name, they do not become sacred until he does so. If he does not intend a later declaration, they acquire sanctity when separated. If one separates terumah from a basket and later finds fruit along the sides of the basket, those fruits are included because his intention was to separate for the entire basket. One who separates from a threshing floor should intend also for what is among the straw, stubble, sides, and remaining on the floor. One who separates from a winepress should intend for what remains in the grape seeds and skins; from an oil pit, for what remains in the press residue. If he failed to have such intent, there is a standing stipulation of beit din that his separation covers all of it. If he brought produce into his house, even though it is scattered, he may separate from one pile for all. If there are two storehouses in one upper room, he separates from each separately. Sacks of grain, cakes of pressed figs, and containers of dried figs can be tithed one for another when all are within one hakafah. In chapter 4: the printed wording concerning separating wheat for bread and figs for dried figs is corrupt and must be reversed, as explained above in seif 63. One may not separate from moist produce for dry or dry for moist; if he did, it is effective. One may separate clear wine for cloudy wine but not cloudy for clear. R. Yehudah permits even cloudy for clear provided the separated portion is superior. R. Yishmael son of R. Yose says in his father's name that one may separate wine for vinegar but not vinegar for wine except proportionally. If one designated a barrel believing it wine and it was found to be vinegar, if it was already vinegar at the time of separation the terumah is ineffective; if it soured afterward, it is terumah; if uncertain, it is doubtful terumah and he separates again. These are Rabbi's words, for Rabbi regards wine and vinegar as two species. The Sages say they are one species: if he intended to separate wine for wine and what he had was vinegar, it is ineffective; if what he had was wine, he separates and afterward separates again for the vinegar. If one regularly checked a barrel from which he separated and it was later found vinegar, for the preceding three days it is certainly treated according to its prior state, and earlier periods are doubtful. In chapter 5: impure terumah is taken at one-sixtieth; among the cases taken at one-sixtieth is terumah that became impure through accident or inadvertence. In chapter 10: how much terumat ma'aser of demai must be present to require carrying it to a kohen? One-eighth of one-eighth. This applies to demai, but with definite tevel even the smallest amount may not be discarded. This distinction is where the terumah is impure; if pure, even the smallest amount must be preserved. So too with terumah from other fruits: if there is this measure, it is given to a kohen; if less, it is cast into the fire and burned. If a kohen's cattle stand near an Israelite's cattle, or a kohen's garments are being woven near an Israelite's garments, the Israelite may burn terumah oil for the kohen's benefit. He may go up to an attic or down into a pit to perform the kohen's needs, but not his own. If they are partners in a shop, it is permitted. Likewise, if a kohen is reclining in an Israelite's house, the Israelite may light a lamp for him with terumah oil; if the kohen later rises and leaves, he need not extinguish it immediately but may let it go out by itself. In chapter 2 of Ma'asrot: one who has a heap of onions, dried figs, or carobs on his roof may select and eat, select and place on the table, and select and throw before his animal. One may continue feeding loose bundles of grain until they are made into formal bundles. In chapter 3 of Ma'aser Sheni: if a seller says, "I sold you tevel," and the purchaser says, "You sold me produce already corrected," the seller is compelled to correct what he sold. Similar laws apply if the royal authority seized his threshing floor. Ma'aser sheni is not redeemed in Jerusalem nowadays, not separated there, not desacralized there, not sold there, and not removed from Jerusalem; if removed, it is left to decay. One who says, "The ma'aser sheni in this object is desacralized onto this issar," without fixing a location within the object, according to R. Shimon has designated it, while the Sages require him to say its northern or southern side. In chapter 6 of Berachot: one walking to separate terumot and ma'asrot says, "Blessed... Who sanctified us... to separate terumah and ma'asrot." When does he bless? From the time of separation. In chapter 3 of Demai: a chaver sitting at an am ha'aretz's drinking party or meal, even if observed taking food or drink directly, does not thereby lose his presumption concerning tithes, because perhaps he tithed mentally; the same applies to the son of a chaver. <b>(Likkut) And likewise the tenant.</b> Gittin 43b. Tosafot, Bava Metzia 101a, explain that the case is Syria, as above. The Tosefta states this explicitly.

<b>Even if they divided.</b> The Yerushalmi in chapter 6 of Demai teaches: if an Israelite and gentile purchase a field in Syria, Rabbi says it is as though tevel and ma'aser are mixed together, while Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel says the Israelite's share is obligated and the gentile's exempt. If they divided only after piling the grain, even Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel agrees with Rabbi, because each stalk had been jointly owned. In Eretz Yisrael we rule like Rabbi that there is no bereirah, which is the basis of Mechaber's "even" and "needless to say." The Yerushalmi speaks of Syria because in Eretz Yisrael we rule that gentile acquisition does not remove sanctity. Rambam writes the rule even in Eretz Yisrael where the gentile completed processing: the gentile share is fully exempt, while the Israelite share is rabbinically obligated. Although bereirah is normally accepted in rabbinic law, here the produce had already become obligated before final processing.

Ritva in Kiddushin writes that this case is indeed analogous and that the earlier question is effectively resolved even though not resolved in its original passage. See Tosafot, Kiddushin 23b, Ran in Nedarim, and Ritva there, who brings proof from the present law. Mishneh LaMelech writes similarly. Kesef Mishneh, however, says that since the second question in Nedarim concerning the right of choice was resolved, the first question is automatically resolved as well. This is very difficult and Mishneh LaMelech already objects. Ran's discussion in Nedarim shows that the Gemara knew the right of choice belongs to the person separating and nevertheless still asked the first question.
