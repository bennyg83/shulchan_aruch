<b>One who separates terumah without authorization.</b> Rambam's wording here raises great difficulty. Bava Metzia cites a baraita: one who entered another's field without permission, picked produce, and separated terumah, if the owner objects because of theft, the terumah is invalid; if not, it is valid. How do we know? If the owner says, "Go take from the better produce," and better produce is in fact present, the terumah is valid. The Gemara challenges Abaye from this because he holds that consent arising only afterward is not effective. Rava answers for Abaye that the case is where the owner had appointed him an agent. The Gemara adds that this is also logical, because terumah requires "your knowledge." Since we rule like Abaye, it would seem terumah should not take effect unless he had been appointed an agent. Yet Rambam rules here even where the person entered the field initially in order to take produce for himself, which is plainly not agency. Beit Yosef asks this and answers that Rambam did not follow the Gemara's agency explanation, because later passages show that with terumah the phrase "go take from the better produce" can reveal retroactive satisfaction even where the person originally took without permission. I find this very difficult. The later case of Mari bar Isak proves nothing because there the issue was only whether Mar Zutra could eat once the owner expressed approval; there was no need to establish retroactive authorization. Kiddushin likewise cites Rava, who himself holds that later consent can work and therefore does not require agency. How can that overturn an explicit explanation of the baraita according to Abaye, whose ruling we follow? Tosafot in Kiddushin indeed explain that even there the case involves an agent. Rambam, however, reads the passages literally without agency. More difficult still: Rambam says even one who entered another's field to take produce for himself can later have his terumah validated when the owner says, "Go take from the better produce." Yet the Gemara concludes that such an expression does not validate actual theft; in non-mitzvah contexts it may merely be said out of embarrassment. Why then is this terumah valid when the initial picking itself was theft? Rashi avoids this by explaining that "concern for theft" means concern about the unauthorized act of separating, not actual theft of the produce.

It appears that Rambam understands the conclusion differently. Since Rava and Mar Zutra distinguish terumah from other matters because terumah is a mitzvah, when the owner now says "go take from the better," we infer that with respect to this mitzvah he was pleased from the beginning that anyone who would separate from the better produce should do so. This counts as "your knowledge," as though he had originally appointed him. Once that is true for Rava, we can say the same conclusion is accepted even according to Abaye. Rava initially answered Abaye by agency because he was uncertain whether Abaye would accept the special mitzvah distinction, but the final analysis permits us to say that Abaye also accepts it. This resolves Rambam's omission of explicit agency. As for the actual theft problem, the case can be understood as one where everything he picked for himself was subsequently used as terumah and he retained nothing. The mitzvah at the end reveals that the initial taking too is treated as having been for terumah rather than theft. This also allows Tur and Semag to be read literally, without requiring prior appointment as agent.

<b>Many laws of charity.</b> It appears that ma'aser separated from monetary profit should follow the law of ma'aser ani from grain and fruit. My father-in-law wrote that our monetary ma'aser is obligated neither biblically nor rabbinically. I am surprised, because at the beginning of siman 249 it is presented as a full obligation by the poskim and by Beit Yosef citing the Yerushalmi. Tur's laws of ma'aser ani therefore provide guidance here as well. When ma'aser ani is distributed in the field, the owner has no discretionary benefit; the poor may come and take it even against his will, and even from a poor owner it can be removed. When it is distributed in the house, the owner has the right to choose which poor person receives it. Chullin explicitly says that household ma'aser ani gives the owner such discretion, while the obligation itself can still be enforced even against a poor owner. This is not contradicted by Tosafot's rule that where the owner has discretionary benefit, a poor person who seizes the gift may be made to return it. We compel the owner to give ma'aser ani, but he retains the right to decide which poor person receives it. If one poor person takes it without his consent, he may recover it and give it to another poor person. This also supports the custom of compelling a groom, when he receives his dowry, to separate ma'aser. That ma'aser may be distributed to needy relatives of the bride and groom, who take precedence over other poor people. Some say that ma'aser is taken only from the dowry given by the bride's family but not from money the groom's father gives his son, because the father already separated ma'aser. This is mistaken. The obligation is on the recipient to give from what Hashem grants him. It does not attach to the money as though the money itself had already been permanently exempted. Therefore it makes no difference whether the gift came from his father or father-in-law.

The other laws of ma'aser ani likewise apply by analogy. A father and son, two brothers, or partners, if one is poor, the other may give him his ma'aser ani. One may not use it to pay a debt or repay an ordinary favor, but one may use it for an act of kindness, while informing the recipient that it comes from ma'aser ani, such as participation in a mourner's recovery meal. One may not use it for captive redemption or other charity obligations already imposed upon him, because that would be paying his own debt from ma'aser funds. But if he is not otherwise obligated to redeem that captive, certainly he may direct his ma'aser there, for there is no greater charity. The same general principles appear applicable to ma'aser separated from monetary profit, and Beit Yosef also cites Mordechai comparing ma'aser ani to designated charity funds.
