<b>If his intention is to bake, and so forth.</b> R. Shimshon proves from Berachot that bread made for kutach is generally exempt from challah when its form shows it is not intended as ordinary bread. The Yerushalmi nevertheless says such dough can be obligated lest he later change his mind and bake from it a small loaf. It therefore appears that the Yerushalmi speaks where he actually took a little from the dough to bake over the fire, creating concern that he may later bake a challah-sized loaf. This also explains the distinction between thick and thin forms: with thick pieces people may change their minds, while with thin forms such a change is less common.

<b>And he baked even, and so forth.</b> At first glance this implies that he does not separate challah until after baking the small portion. Beit Yosef writes that if he merely intended to bake a small amount but did not actually bake it, the whole dough does not become obligated. Even actual baking creates liability only because of a decree, so one should not extend the decree further. Mordechai records that R. Shimshon ultimately held that dough intended for boiling is exempt unless a small cake is actually baked from it, in which case one should separate challah from the baked piece together with the remaining dough. Rosh, however, reports that Maharam, when dough was kneaded in his house for boiling, would instruct that a little be baked and thereby the whole dough became obligated before any piece was removed. My father-in-law therefore challenges Beit Yosef and says Rosh disagrees with Mordechai, since according to Rosh mere intent to bake should suffice. I do not think there is any dispute. If at the time of kneading he already intends both to boil the dough and to bake a small portion, the entire dough is obligated immediately because the concern of changing his mind exists from the outset. That is Rosh and Tur's case. Mordechai and Beit Yosef discuss where at the time of kneading the intent was to boil the entire dough and only afterward he decided to remove a small part for baking. In that case, because the rest is still intended for boiling and the obligation is only a decree lest he later change his mind about more of it, actual baking of the small part is required before the whole becomes obligated. This reconciles the sources. <br><b>However,</b> one may still ask why Maharam needed any such device, because ordinary dough intended for boiling could itself be obligated by the concern that he might later change his mind, as in the case of thick kutach bread. The answer appears to be that dough intended for boiling was ordinarily prepared with spices or egg liquid in a form unlike ordinary baking dough. Therefore, without separating and actually baking a piece, there was no ordinary concern that he would change his mind and bake the whole. This is similar to the Yerushalmi's special case.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>The entire dough becomes obligated through it.</b> Maharshal infers from Rosh's wording that Maharam would recite a blessing over this challah, because otherwise why intentionally bake a small piece merely to create the obligation? He finds this difficult because the obligation arises only from a decree lest one change his mind, so perhaps no blessing should be recited. He suggests Maharam's purpose was only to create an obligation to separate without a blessing, perhaps to avoid intentionally separating challah where there was no obligation, which would create needless destruction because nowadays the challah is burned. In my opinion this is incorrect. Berachot explicitly says that thick bread made for kutach is obligated because of concern that one may confuse or later redirect it to ordinary bread, and an unqualified rabbinic obligation requires the normal blessing. Shabbos 23 likewise teaches that even a rabbinic doubt can require a blessing in settings such as the second day of Yom Tov, apart from exceptional cases like demai. Similarly, dough jointly owned by a Jew and gentile in siman 330 is obligated by rabbinic decree and plainly requires a blessing. Whenever Chazal state simply that an item is obligated and do not distinguish, the standard blessing applies. This is why Tur does not say that R. Shimshon omitted a blessing over the challah itself. Mahari Weil explicitly records two doughs intended for frying, where he instructed that a little be baked and then challah separated with a blessing. My father-in-law likewise concludes that a blessing is required and brings proof from Rashba.
