**** ENGLISH ****One onah. Thus we conclude explicitly at the end of chapter HaIshah. Beit Yosef brought in the name of Or Zarua who ruled: if she is accustomed to see by day, he separates from her the entire day and the night before it; and Beit Yosef wrote on this that one need not be concerned with this, but rather as the Tur and poskim wrote, that the prohibition is only on the day alone, as Shulchan Arukh wrote here — for thus we rule explicitly, the halakhah is like Rabbi Yehuda in the Gemara who holds thus. And Maharshal corrected this ruling of Or Zarua to say: although the halakhah is like Rabbi Yosi, nevertheless it is fitting to be stringent on this. And it is astonishing to be stringent on what is explicit in the Talmud leniently, especially in veset derabbanan.

Further, Beit Yosef brought in the name of Hagahot Maimoniyot who wrote in the name of Aviassaf that this onah is a day or night in the days of Nisan and Tishrei when day and night are equal, but in Tevet and Tammuz we require half a day and half a night — astonishing, end of his words. And Beit Yosef wrote on this: it appears his reason is because it seemed to him that the measure of onah is twelve hours of the hours that are twenty-four in a day and night; nevertheless it appears one need not be concerned with this, since the poskim did not mention it, and also the language of the Gemara implies it is particular only about day or night and they did not mention hours in chapter HaIshah except because of the dispute of Rabbi Yosi there, end of his words. And Maharshal wrote on this that it escaped the author of Hagahot Maimoniyot and Beit Yosef that which is stated in the first chapter of Tinoket explicitly regarding niddah too, that onah is specifically twelve hours — meaning a day or night in the days of Nisan and Tishrei, and half a day and half a night in Tevet and Tammuz — and thus we rule, end of his words.

And it is difficult for me: if you say the matter depends on hours, automatically the prohibition of the onah is in that period when she is accustomed to see, whether by day or by night, and you will not find here to say twelve hours, except if she saw in the days of Nisan and Tishrei in the middle of the day or middle of the night; but if she sees at the end of the day in Nisan and Tishrei, how will you calculate twelve hours? If you calculate them from the hour she is accustomed to see onwards for twelve hours, you will find she is forbidden also on the night after it and permitted before that hour on that same day — and this is certainly not so, for behold she is forbidden the entire day as Rabbi Yehuda in the Gemara and in the poskim. And if you calculate them retroactively twelve hours, how will you do when she sees at the beginning of the day — for according to this she would be permitted after that hour on that same day, and Heaven forbid to say so. And likewise if you say to calculate six hours retroactively and six forward, she would not be forbidden the entire day if she sees at the beginning of the day — rather the matter must depend on day and night, that the Sages gave a measure for the prohibition here: either a day or a night shall be as it is, whether short or long, whether before the hour she is accustomed to see or after — they equated their measures and call this the measure of onah. For in most places we consider night as twelve hours in total, and likewise day — and this is not the matter of the derasha in chapter Tinoket (Niddah 65a), which depends only on hours, for there it refers to a girl who saw in her father's house and then married, that she is permitted to have relations after first intercourse and we attribute the blood to her virginity — a day or night in Nisan and Tishrei, and half a night and half a day in Tevet and Tammuz. One answer there: there it is impossible to attribute to the night itself as here, for there the permission was in fixing the time to attribute to virgin blood after first intercourse and it does not depend on day or night; and if so, if he had first intercourse at the end of the night he would not give her but a short time, and for one who had first intercourse at the beginning of the night she would be permitted afterward a long time — therefore they said there that twelve hours the matter depends, that every such virgin is permitted twelve hours after her first intercourse. Unlike here in seeing niddah blood, which is attributed to time — meaning either at night or by day according to her veset — the Sages gave a measure to prohibit that he separate from her that entire day or that entire night when she is accustomed to see, both before and after, and the measure was equal in all of them; and before that night or day, or after it, is permitted. And I am astonished at Beit Yosef that he did not challenge Aviassaf's reasoning thus; and it appears that Aviassaf himself possibly did not hold so — rather they always calculated for prohibition six hours before the hour she is accustomed to see and six after; it follows that if she is accustomed to see at the beginning of the day, she is likewise permitted at the end of the day after six hours. But we have no reason to say so, for the halakhah is fixed in our hands that she is forbidden the entire day even if it is a long day. And this is clear and simple.

And Maharshal wrote that the Tur and Rosh had a different version in the Gemara; and in my humble opinion the matters appear according to their plain meaning: regarding removal of blood due to fear, there is reason to be concerned that perhaps at the hour she needs her husband the fear departed from her heart, and since it is her veset hour it is possible she will become impure then, for her regular time comes; and they did not say in her case that fright removes blood unless she was in fright the entire veset hour and it did not cease from her — and this is a clear matter: if she was in fear at the beginning of her veset hour and departed from the fear within that veset time, then she is like other women whose nature returned to its original place — therefore she may not have relations until after the entire veset hour passed. Unlike a pregnant woman who is already cleared of blood and there is no concern to forbid her even at veset time — it follows there is no resemblance of pregnant woman to one in hiding except after the fright ended, meaning after the veset measure which is certainly removal of blood; and this is each according to her way — in this case after the fright passed, and in this case even immediately. And therefore the plain meaning in the Gemara regarding a pregnant woman ab initio from one in hiding: for the entire veset time afterward she does not need a bedikah — and this is correct and clear. And I am astonished at the commentators who did not notice this matter, for it is a correct reasoning and fitting to establish the halakhah thus.
