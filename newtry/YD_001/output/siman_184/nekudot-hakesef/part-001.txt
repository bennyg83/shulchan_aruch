<b>(Siman 184, Turei Zahav, subsection 2) It is surprising that he rules stringently, etc. </b>In Siftei Kohen, subsection 7, I wrote that this is also the opinion of Ramban and that it is demonstrated by the Talmud, and I was brief there. In my book I explained the matter, for this is demonstrated by the Talmud's statement (Niddah 63b) concerning Rava's ruling in accordance with Rabbi Yehudah: if she regularly sees at the beginning or in the middle of the day, she must separate for the entire day, and if at the end of the night, for the entire night. The Talmud asks: Did Rava indeed say this? Rabbi Yoshiyah said, "There is a warning to the Children of Israel that they separate from their wives near the time of their veset." How long is this period? Rava said: one onah. Does this not mean an additional onah? Rashi explains that if her veset is during the day, she is forbidden for that entire day and the entire preceding night. The Talmud answers: No, it means that same onah. This is difficult, for why would one initially think that it means an additional onah? Rava said one onah, and if it meant both, the Talmud should have said two onot. Rather, the Talmud necessarily maintains that if she has no fixed hour during the day, the entire day is considered the time of her veset. She must therefore separate for one onah adjoining her veset, namely, the entire preceding night, which is indeed an additional onah. Since Rava stated without qualification that one must separate for one onah adjoining her veset, he presumably addressed every case, whether or not she has a fixed hour during the day. When she has no fixed hour, he must be referring to an additional onah. The question, "Does this not mean that onah, namely, an additional onah, even when she has a fixed hour during the day?" is therefore answered: "No, that same onah," meaning that if she has a fixed hour, she need separate only during that onah. Examine this carefully. Although this proof can be rebutted, this nevertheless appears to me to be the primary ruling. One should not ask why Or Zaru'a did not specify that she has no fixed hour, for one may answer that his unqualified language is self-explanatory: by writing, "If she regularly sees during the day," he implies that the entire day is her time. Alternatively, he may hold that nowadays women do not have sufficiently regular veset patterns to establish particular hours during the day. Similarly, the Agudah wrote at the end of the chapter HaIshah, after citing the Talmudic discussion concerning Rabbi Yehudah: "It was taught: 'You shall warn them,' etc. Near her veset means one onah, and after that onah passes she is permitted. Nowadays, when women do not have fully regular veset patterns, one should distance oneself somewhat before and after the onah of the veset." <b>He further </b>expounded there at length in questioning Avi'asaf: How are these twelve hours to be calculated, since this is not comparable to blood of virginity, etc.? This presents no difficulty. Ma'adanei Yom Tov already noted this, yet nevertheless concluded that Avi'asaf's words are primary. We therefore certainly calculate seasonal hours, as I wrote there in Siftei Kohen. This means that if her veset is during the daytime at the winter solstice, when the day is only nine hours, then when she must be concerned for it the second time, separating only during the day is insufficient. She must separate from an hour and a half before daybreak until an hour and a half into the night. If her veset is at night, then when she must be concerned for it the second time, she need not separate from the beginning of the night. Rather, she separates from an hour and a half into the night until an hour and a half before sunrise, a total of twelve hours. This is because at the winter solstice the night borrows from the end of one day and the beginning of the next. The hour and a half that now begins as night would still have been day at the spring equinox, and the hour and a half at the end of the night would likewise have been day then. The corresponding calculation applies at the summer solstice. This was also the Derishah's first explanation. His final explanation, which he adopted as primary, has no basis at all. We can now understand why the Talmud and the authorities state that if her veset is during the day, he separates from her for the entire day, and likewise at night. They address only a day and night that are day and night by law, meaning twelve hours. At the summer and winter solstices, however, the permitted portion of the day or night is not by law part of that day or night, since the day borrows from the night or the night from the day. All this is clear.
