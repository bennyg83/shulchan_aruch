<b>וכן המרכיב כו'. </b>לשון הרמב"ם והשמיט ירק בירק משום דמיבעיא לן בחולין ס' הרכיב שני דשאים כו' ומשמע ליה אף בא"י והא דתנן במתני' פ"ק אין מרכיבין כו' ס"ל איסורא בעלמא מדרבנן [מכאן למד דאילן בירק וירק באילן לוקה]. ועתוס' שם ד"ה הרכיב כו' וכ"ז כתב הר"ש במתני' שם. והרא"ש בה"ק כתב כתירוץ הראשון דבח"ל קמיבעיא ליה אבל נראה כתי' האחרון דלבני נח קמיבעיא ליה וכ"ה בירושלמי פ"ק דכלאים תני בשם ר"א מותר הוא עובד כוכבים ללבוש ולזרוע כלאים אבל לא להרביע בהמתו כלאים ולא להרכיב אילנו כלאים למה מפני שכתוב בהן למיניהן והרי דשאין כתיב בהן למיניהן אין כתיב בציווי אלא בהוצאה וכן דעת הטור שסתם וכתב בין ירק בירק ע"ש:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And likewise, one who grafts, etc.</b> This is Rambam's language. He omitted grafting one vegetable onto another because the Gemara in Chullin 60a raises the question of one who grafted two species of grasses, etc. Rambam understood that the question applies even in Eretz Yisrael. As for the Mishnah in the first chapter, which states, “One may not graft,” etc., he understood this as a rabbinic prohibition alone. [From here he derived that one who grafts a tree onto a vegetable, or a vegetable onto a tree, incurs lashes.] See Tosafos there, s.v. “One who grafted,” etc. The Rash explains all this in his commentary to the Mishnah there. The Rosh, in Hilchos Kilayim, follows the first answer, that the question concerns Chutz LaAretz. It appears, however, that the final answer is preferable, namely, that the question concerns the descendants of Noach. This is also stated in the Yerushalmi, in the first chapter of Kilayim: It was taught in the name of Rabbi Elazar that a gentile may wear and sow kilayim, but may not crossbreed his animals or graft his trees with unlike species. Why? Because the expression “according to their kinds” is written regarding them. Yet “according to their kinds” is also written regarding grasses. There, however, it is not stated as a command, but only in the account of their emergence. This is also the view of the Tur, who writes without qualification that the prohibition applies even to grafting one vegetable onto another. See there.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>It is permitted, etc.; and likewise, etc.</b> The Tosefta in the first chapter of Kilayim states: One may sow ordinary seed together with the seeds of trees. One who sows grape seeds together with wheat incurs forty lashes. The Yerushalmi in the first chapter of Peah likewise teaches: One may sow ordinary seeds and tree seeds together, but one who sows grape seeds with wheat incurs forty lashes. Rabbi Zeira said: Scripture states, “You shall not sow your vineyard with kilayim,” meaning that the principal species of your vineyard may not be sown together with kilayim. Rabbi Yudan of Cappadocia asked Rabbi Yosi: Elsewhere we say that tree seeds are called “seeds,” yet here you say that tree seeds are not called “seeds.” He answered that there Scripture limits the term because people do not ordinarily call them seeds, whereas here Scripture includes them through the phrase, “every seed that is sown,” meaning with regard to susceptibility to tumah. The Gemara in Bechoros 54a states similarly: Just as wine and oil, etc.; wine and grain, etc. As explained above, wheat and grape seeds are liable as kilayei hakerem, in accordance with Rabbi Yonasan and not Rabbi Yoshiyah. This is also the meaning of the Yerushalmi cited above, “the principal species of your vineyard,” which follows Rabbi Yonasan. The Yerushalmi in the eighth chapter of Kilayim states according to Rabbi Yonasan: Scripture says, “You shall not sow your field with kilayim.” For what purpose, then, does it say, “You shall not sow your vineyard with kilayim,” etc.? It is as Rabbi Zeira said: “You shall not sow your vineyard with kilayim,” meaning that the principal species of the vineyard may not be sown together with kilayim. Rabbi Yudan of Cappadocia asked Rabbi Yosi, etc., as above. The expression “the principal species of your vineyard” does not follow Rabbi Yoshiyah, who explains the verse as referring to two kinds of grain sown with the vineyard. Rather, it means the principal species of the vineyard itself, since all other trees may be planted together with seeds, as explained above. This was the basis of the Yerushalmi's question. See the Behag. The Beis Yosef also wrote this, but retracted in Bedek HaBayis. That retraction is mistaken, for the Yerushalmi states the opposite, namely, that such a combination is kilayim. See there. The Rash cites the Yerushalmi in his commentary to the second Mishnah of the first chapter, and again at the end of the seventh Mishnah, where the Yerushalmi asks whether Rabbi Yosi's position is inconsistent. There is no difficulty, however, because any two distinct species may be termed kilayim even when their combination is permitted. Thus, “its essence is kilayim” means merely that it consists of two species. The question at the seventh Mishnah means as follows: Rabbi Yosi permits grafting a vegetable onto a tree because they do not unite with one another, yet there he rules that one removes it, etc., because they do unite there, etc. The Tur writes: It is permitted to mix tree seeds with field seeds and sow them together, for the Torah prohibited in Chutz LaAretz only grafting, etc. The Beis Yosef understood from his words that this would be forbidden in Eretz Yisrael. Heaven forbid that such an error be attributed to the Tur. The Gemara in Bechoros cited above must refer to Eretz Yisrael, since it discusses wine and oil as kilayim, which applies specifically in Eretz Yisrael, as stated in Kiddushin there and as explained in siman 296. Rather, the Tur's text contains a scribal error and should read, “and the Torah did not prohibit,” etc. This begins a separate statement. This is also the view of the Rosh.

<b>ואפי' לזה כו'. </b>כ"מ בירושלמי פ"ה דגרסינן שם בע"ה שקיים ירקות שדה בכרם אסורין בין לו בין לאחר פועל שקיים ירקות שדה בכרם אסור לו ומותר לאחר וקשיא אם אסור לו יהא אסור לכל אדם ואם מותר לכל אדם יהא מותר לו אלא כר"ש דאמר אין אדם מקדיש דבר שאינו שלו אע"ג דר"ש אמר א"א מקדיש דבר שאינו שלו מודה הוא הכא שאסור לו ומשמע דוקא בכרם וכן ממה שהקשה ואם מותר לכ"א כו' ואמר מודה הכא דר"ל לעצמו מודה לרבנן דמקדש:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And even to that person, etc.</b> This appears from the Yerushalmi in the fifth chapter, which states: If a landowner maintained field vegetables in a vineyard, they are forbidden both to him and to others. If a worker maintained field vegetables in a vineyard, they are forbidden to him but permitted to others. The Yerushalmi asks: If they are forbidden to him, they should be forbidden to everyone; and if they are permitted to everyone, they should also be permitted to him. It answers that the ruling follows Rabbi Shimon, who holds that a person cannot consecrate property that is not his. Although Rabbi Shimon says that a person cannot consecrate property that is not his, he agrees here that it is forbidden to the worker himself. This implies that the special rule applies specifically to a vineyard. The same is evident from the question, “If it is permitted to everyone,” etc., and from the answer that Rabbi Shimon agrees here, meaning that with respect to the worker himself he concedes to the Sages that the produce becomes forbidden.
