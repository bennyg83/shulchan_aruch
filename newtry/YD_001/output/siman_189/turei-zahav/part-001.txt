<b>חוששת לזמן הידוע. </b>פירוש כל עונות הוסתות כמ"ש סי' קפ"ד סעיף ב' וא"ל הא כתב בסוף הסעיף דאפי' בשביל פעם אחת חוששת דכאן קא משמע לן דאין צריך לחוש לסתם עונה שהוא שלשים יום אלא זמן הידוע לחוד כיון שיש לה וסת קבוע:

<b>מווסת הקבוע לה. </b>פירוש שהיתה רגילה לראות ביום ך' ושינת' לראות ביום כ"א וכ"ב וכ"ג אף ראיית כ"א מן המנין כיון שגם היא היתה בדרך דילוג שוה כמו האחרים שאחריה ובאמת אין בזה שום חדוש ובגמ' לא אמרו זה אלא דרך חדוש על קושיא לשמואל ומכ"מ י"ל דקמ"ל רבותא דל"ת דאין הראשונה מן המנין אלא כשראיית י"ט היא ראיה חדשה שלא הורגלה תחילה בזה ובדרישה ר"ל שהדילוג הראשון אין צריך להיות שוה עם שאר דהיינו אפי' הית' רגילה לראות בט"ו ואח"כ ראתה כ' כ"א כ"ב ואינו נכון כלל דהא בעינן שיהא בהפלגה עם האחרים משמע דבשוה בעינן כמשמעות הגמרא בזה וכן משמעות הטור והשלחן ערוך:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>From her established veses. </b>This means that she had been accustomed to see on the 20th day, and then changed and saw on the 21st, 22nd, and 23rd. The sighting on the 21st is also included in the count, since it too followed the same equal progression as those that came after it. In truth, there is no novelty in this. The Gemara stated it as a novel answer to a difficulty according to Shmuel. Nevertheless, one may say that it teaches a significant point, lest one think that the first sighting is counted only when the sighting on the 19th is a new sighting to which she had not previously been accustomed. The Derishah wishes to say that the first progression need not be equal to the others, meaning that even if she was accustomed to see on the 15th and afterward saw on the 20th, 21st, and 22nd, it is sufficient. This is entirely incorrect, for we require that its interval correspond to the others, which implies equal intervals, as is evident from the Gemara and from the wording of the Tur and Shulchan Aruch.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And one should be concerned for their words, etc. </b>This is difficult, for even according to the first opinion there is one stringency. Namely, if she is subject to concern for two vestos, neither is uprooted unless the second becomes established, as stated in seif 13 at the beginning of the gloss. According to the later opinion here, since the progressive veses is established through three occurrences, the concern for her other veses is automatically uprooted and she no longer need be concerned for it. According to the first opinion, the progressive veses is not yet established, and she must still be concerned for the other veses as well. Therefore, my father-in-law of blessed memory wrote that regarding this matter we rule according to the first opinion, meaning that we follow both opinions stringently. This is correct and will be explained in seif 13. <i data-commentator="Nekudot HaKesef" data-label="♯" data-order="2"></i>I still require clarification regarding the Tur, who wrote that the Rosh ruled according to the first opinion, meaning like Shmuel in the Gemara and unlike Rav, whose view is the later opinion here. Yet in the chapter Shor Shenagach Arba'ah VaChamishah, the Rosh wrote on folio 8 that we rule like Rav in this matter. This requires further study.

<b>ראתה ג"פ בג' חדשים כו'. </b>הוי מצי למינקט בקיצור כגון ט"ו בסיון ט"ז בתמוז ואח"כ ט"ו באב ט"ז אלול קבע לה וסת אח"כ בחודש א' בחמשה עשר בו ובשני ט"ז בו וכן לעולם אלא דקמ"ל רבותא אפי' אם הדילוג הוא בג' ימים דהיינו ט"ו ט"ו י"ז אזלינן בתרי' וחוששת בחודש השלישי ביום י"ז אע"פ שיש בינו לבין יום ט"ו ב' ימים וממילא נדע אם הדילוג הוא ביותר משלשה ימי' דהיינו שראתה ד' פעמי' בד' חדשים שלש פעמים בדילוג דג"כ אזלינן בתרי' אלא נקט שלש פעמים דבזה הוה שיעור שהוחזקה בו וממילא הוא הדין ביותר מזה. כן נ"ל פשוט:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>She saw three times in three months, etc. </b>He could have stated the case briefly, for example: she saw on the 15th of Sivan, the 16th of Tammuz, then the 15th of Av and the 16th of Elul. She has established a veses to see in one month on the 15th and in the next month on the 16th, and so on forever. Rather, he teaches a greater novelty: even if the progression is separated by three days, meaning 15, 15, and 17, we follow the pattern, and in the third month she must be concerned for the 17th, even though two days separate it from the 15th. From this we automatically know that the same applies when the progression is greater than three days, namely when she saw four times in four months with three progressive changes, for there too we follow the pattern. He mentioned three occurrences because this is the minimum by which the pattern becomes established, and the same obviously applies to more than this. This appears obvious to me.

<b>וסת הסירוג ראיה ראשונה כו'. </b>פי' שסירגה ראש חדש דהיינו שראתה בר"ח ניסן ובר"ח סיון ובר"ח אב קבעה לה בר"ח לסירוג שאח"כ אינה חוששת לר"ח אלול אלא לר"ח תשרי וכן לעולם וכאן הוה ר"ח ניסן מן המנין אפילו לדעה קמייתא דסעיף (ד')[ז'] בהפלגה בדילוג שצריכה לד' ראיות דהתם דחשבינן לימות החודש כמה יש ימים בין ראיה הראשונה לשניה שע"י זה יהיה ניכר וסת ההפלגה נמצא הראשונה ושניה שייכי' להדדי דוקא לקבוע וסתה ע"כ אין הראשונה מן המנין כי אין לה לבד שייכות להפלגה אבל כאן הוי קביעו' לר"ח והוי ג"כ ר"ח הראשון מן המנין כי בו לבד יש ג"כ שייכות הוסת מצד שהוא ר"ח ומה לי שיש בין ראיה ראשונה של ר"ח לשניה ושל ר"ח אחד ביניהם או שנים סוף סוף הוסת דוקא בר"ח הוא:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>For a veses of alternating months, the first sighting, etc. </b>This means that she skipped one Rosh Chodesh: she saw on Rosh Chodesh Nisan, on Rosh Chodesh Sivan, and on Rosh Chodesh Av. She thereby established a veses for alternate Roshei Chodashim. Accordingly, she need not be concerned for Rosh Chodesh Elul, but only for Rosh Chodesh Tishrei, and so on forever. Here, Rosh Chodesh Nisan is included in the count even according to the first opinion in seif 7 regarding a progressive interval veses, which requires four sightings. There, we count the days of the month between the first and second sightings, and thereby the interval veses becomes recognizable. Thus, specifically the first and second sightings are connected in order to establish the veses, and the first sighting is therefore not counted independently, since by itself it has no connection to an interval. Here, however, the establishment is tied to Rosh Chodesh, and therefore the first Rosh Chodesh is included in the count, since it independently has relevance to the veses by virtue of being Rosh Chodesh. What difference does it make whether between the first Rosh Chodesh sighting and the second there was one Rosh Chodesh or two? Ultimately, the veses is specifically for Rosh Chodesh.

<b>Whether she saw on the 1st of Iyar or did not see, etc. </b>In the Tur, citing the Ramban, the text reads here: and if she did not see, etc. The Beit Yosef wrote that this implies that if she did see on Rosh Chodesh Iyar, she need not be concerned for the 9th of Iyar. The reason is that if she returned and saw on Rosh Chodesh Iyar, we would say that the first sighting on Rosh Chodesh Nisan was the primary one, while the second sighting on the 20th of Nisan was only an additional flow of blood. She would therefore not be concerned either for the 9th of Iyar or for the 20th. But now that she did not see on Rosh Chodesh Iyar, it is revealed that the sighting on the 20th of Nisan was primary, and she must be concerned for both the 9th and the 20th of Iyar. The Darkei Moshe disagreed and wrote that from the words shortly afterward, “she must be concerned for both until one becomes established,” we infer that before one veses has been established three times she must be concerned for both. Furthermore, the text shortly afterward states that if she saw on the 9th, etc., which implies that although she has already seen twice at 20-day intervals, she must nevertheless be concerned for the days of the month and for the 20th of Iyar. The same applies here. Although she saw on the 1st of Iyar, she must still be concerned for the 9th because of the sighting on the 20th of Nisan. As for the wording “if she did not see on the 1st of Iyar,” it appears to me that it was stated as a greater novelty: although the veses of Rosh Chodesh Nisan was uprooted, she must nevertheless be concerned for the second veses so long as it has not been uprooted even once. End quote. This appears to me to be the primary view. The Rama therefore correctly amended the text here to read, “whether she saw or did not see, etc.” We do not find anywhere that a sighting at a changed time uproots a preexisting concern so long as the changed pattern itself has not been established according to its law, whether by three or four occurrences. Moreover, suppose she also saw on Rosh Chodesh Iyar, on the 9th of Iyar, and on the 28th of Iyar. Would she not thereby establish a 20-day interval veses beginning with the sighting on the 20th of Nisan? Certainly she would, for there are three equal intervals, and the additional sighting on Rosh Chodesh Iyar is irrelevant. How, then, could we permit her, merely because she saw on Rosh Chodesh Iyar, not to be concerned at all for the 9th when this could still develop into an established veses? The Perishah wrote that although it is possible to establish a 20-day interval veses in this manner, we are not initially concerned for it before seeing three equal intervals. This is difficult, for whenever such a pattern is possible we certainly are concerned lest it develop, as we find throughout this siman, except for a progressive veses, for which there is no concern until it becomes established, as stated in seif 11. A further clear proof appears at the end of this siman in the Tur. If she saw three times on Rosh Chodesh, and on the third occasion she also saw on the 25th of the month and on Rosh Chodesh, she must also be concerned for the earlier day, the 25th, lest she be establishing another veses. This is a kal vachomer. There, three Rosh Chodesh sightings are clearly before us, and Rosh Chodesh is also the ordinary onah for a woman, yet she must still be concerned for the 25th. All the more so here, where only two sightings are before us, one on the 20th of Nisan and one on Rosh Chodesh Iyar. One of them must be primary and the other incidental, but how do you know that Rosh Chodesh is primary and that she need not be concerned for the 9th? Perhaps the sighting on the 20th of Nisan is primary and she must be concerned for the 9th of Iyar. The blood of Rosh Chodesh Iyar is not redder than the blood of the 20th of Nisan. It therefore appears primary, in accordance with the Rama, that even if she saw on Rosh Chodesh Iyar she must be concerned for the 9th and the 20th of Iyar. However, the Rama's explanation that the Tur's wording “if she did not see” was stated only as a greater novelty does not settle well with me, for if so, afterward too, where it says whether she saw or did not see on the 9th, it should have stated that same novelty. My father-in-law of blessed memory went very far in disagreeing even with the Rama and said that it is an outright error to require concern for the 9th when she saw on Rosh Chodesh Iyar, because the sighting on Rosh Chodesh Iyar interrupted the interval. If she were to be concerned for a 20-day interval, she should count it from the sighting on Rosh Chodesh Iyar, as the Tur states shortly afterward: if she was accustomed to see on the 20th and changed to the 30th, both are forbidden, and when the 20th day from the sighting on the 30th arrives she is forbidden. This shows that she is not concerned for a 20-day interval from the sighting preceding the 30th, but rather for a possible sighting 20 days after the 30th-day sighting, in accordance with Rav Huna son of Rav Yehoshua at the end of the chapter Benos Kusim. End quote. I say that honor is the inheritance of the wise, but these words are like an inadvertent error before a ruler. On the contrary, that passage proves the Rama's view. There, we count only from the sighting on the 30th because there was no actual sighting earlier on the 20th; she had only been expected to see then, but she delayed until the 30th. Rav Huna son of Rav Yehoshua therefore holds that at the end of Benos Kusim we do not count from the time she was expected to see but from when she actually saw, in contrast to Rav Pappa, who holds that we count from when she was expected to see. This is clear in the sugya, and it is also evident from the wording of Rashi and Toras HaBayis HaAruch. They wrote that if we were to count from when she was expected to see, we would be concerned that the veses might come earlier after a shorter interval than her present sighting, and this is unreasonable: she changed by delaying, yet we would be concerned that she might now advance. End quote. This implies that if she actually saw also on the day when she was accustomed to see, we see that she indeed advanced, and she must certainly be concerned for the 20th day from that sighting and also for the 20th day from the sighting on the 30th. Accordingly, here too she must be concerned for a 20-day interval both from the 20th of Nisan and from Rosh Chodesh Iyar. It therefore appears obvious that if she actually saw also on the 20th and added another sighting on the 30th, then according to all opinions we count from the sighting on the 20th and require concern for a 20-day interval from it as well. The intervening sighting on the 30th does not benefit her by creating a leniency. <i data-commentator="Nekudot HaKesef" data-label="♯" data-order="4"></i>Thus the matter is clear: Moshe is true and his Torah is true, and the Rama's words here are accepted halacha among the Jewish people. Whether she saw on Rosh Chodesh Iyar or did not see, all the stated concerns remain.

<b>And the same method applies to intervals and progressions. </b>This means that the progression is not based on dates of the month, such as 15, 16, and 17, but on increasing intervals. If the 20th day arrives and she does not see, she no longer need be concerned for that interval. If she sees a third sighting on the following day, namely 21 days after the second, then when 21 days after the third arrive she must be concerned. If she does not see that day, she no longer need be concerned for that interval. If on the following day, 22 days after the third sighting, she sees a fourth sighting, then when the 22nd day after the fourth arrives she must be concerned. If she does not see, she no longer need be concerned for that interval. If on the following day, 23 days after the fourth sighting, she sees a fifth sighting, she has established a progressive interval veses. From then onward, she need be concerned only for the progression and need not be concerned for the 23rd day after the fifth sighting. One should not ask why above four sightings suffice, while here five are required. Above, the progression is based on dates of the month, 15, 16, and 17, so the date of the first sighting is independently recognizable. Here, however, we begin from her sighting after 20 days, and one cannot know where those 20 days begin without an earlier sighting. Therefore, the initial sighting before the 20-day interval and the sighting on the 20th day are considered together as one unit, since neither is meaningful without the other. According to the second opinion above, which follows Rav and holds that 15, 16, and 17 suffice, only four sightings are required here for the reason just stated. This is what the Rama later wrote: “There are those who say that for a monthly progression the first sighting is included in the count,” meaning the later opinion in seif 7. According to that view, four sightings suffice here, as explained. See what I will write shortly concerning the Rama's statement, “There are those who say that she must be concerned for the progression,” and what appears to me to be the Ramban's view in this ruling.

<b>There are those who say that she must be concerned for the progression, etc. </b>This is difficult, for these are the Ramban's words in the Tur, and the Tur already wrote above in the Ramban's name that we rule like Shmuel and require four sightings for a progressive veses. The Beit Yosef answered that here the case is one in which she already had a veses before she began seeing progressively, and in that case even Shmuel agrees that three sightings suffice, as stated in seif 7. The Ramban should therefore have explained that the case is of a woman who had been accustomed to see every 14 days, and then changed and saw on the 15th and her flow continued, etc. The Derishah cites the Maharshal, and my father-in-law of blessed memory likewise ruled, that the case concerns the law if she later sees a fourth time on the 18th of the month. According to this, the Tur's concluding words are settled: she is concerned for the dates on which she saw three times as for an established veses, and for the other dates as for a progressive veses, implying that the progressive veses is not yet established. End quote. <i data-commentator="Nekudot HaKesef" data-label="♯" data-order="5"></i>I am astonished by their words. How can he write that she is concerned for the other dates as for a progressive veses? Since the progression has not been established according to its law, there is no concern for it when the date on which she would be expected to see according to the progression arrives, until she has already progressed three times according to its law, meaning four sightings, as stated in seif 11. And if the Tur's case is that she has already seen a fourth time on the 18th, then the progressive veses is as fully established as the veses of the 17th. Why, then, does he call only the veses of the 17th established? It appears to me that although the Ramban was quoted as ruling like Shmuel, the Ramban himself nevertheless rules stringently in practice like Rav, that three sightings suffice, as I wrote at the end of seif 7. A proof is that in the chapter HaIshah SheOsah Tzeracheha, the Rosh wrote that the Rambam ruled like Shmuel and that the Ramban also found that view reasonable. He did not write that the Ramban ruled like Shmuel, as he did regarding the Rambam, but only that it appeared reasonable to him. In actual practice, however, the Ramban is stringent that three sightings already create a prohibition. This is also evident from the wording of Toras HaBayis HaAruch. It first records the view of Rabbeinu Chananel and the Raavad like Rav, and then writes that the Rambam ruled like Shmuel and that the Ramban wrote that this seems reasonable. It then continues: “Nevertheless, one should be concerned for the words of Rabbeinu Chananel,” etc. End quote. The word “nevertheless” implies that the Ramban himself wrote this, meaning that although Shmuel's view appeared reasonable to him, one should in practice be stringent like Rav. Were “nevertheless” the Rashba's own language, he should have written simply that one should be stringent like Rabbeinu Chananel. Rather, it is necessarily as I have explained, that the Ramban himself wrote that one should be stringent like Rav in practice. One should not ask from what the Tur wrote earlier in the Ramban's name, concerning a sighting on the 23rd, etc., which implies that four sightings are required like Shmuel, as I mentioned shortly above. There, the question is one of leniency, namely that from then onward she need be concerned only for the progression. If we were to say that the progressive veses becomes established through three sightings, a leniency would result, for she would no longer be concerned for the 23rd day, as explained there. He therefore required four sightings there, because only then may we follow the pattern leniently. Here, however, if we say that the progressive veses becomes established through three sightings, the result is a stringency, since she must thereafter be concerned for that progression. In this respect we should be stringent like Rav. I already discussed this in seif 7. As for the Tur's statement that regarding the 17th she is concerned as for an established veses, it means as for an ordinary veses of a woman that occurs on an equal calendar date and is not progressive, as the Beit Yosef explained. According to the Maharshal's approach, the Tur should not have said “as for an established veses,” using the comparative kaf. This appears correct to me.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And if, after she changed, etc. </b>These are the Ramban's words in the Tur. <i data-commentator="Nekudot HaKesef" data-label="♯" data-order="6"></i>At first glance, they contradict the Gemara. At the end of the chapter HaIshah SheOsah Tzeracheha it states: if she was accustomed to see on the 20th and changed to the 30th, both dates are forbidden. If the 20th arrived and she did not see, she is permitted to have relations until the 30th. If the 30th arrived and she saw, then the next 20th arrived and she did not see, the next 30th arrived and she did not see, and the next 20th arrived and she saw, the 30th is permitted, for it was uprooted through a single occurrence since it had not been established three times, while the 20th is forbidden. Rashi explains that she had been accustomed to the 20th and uprooted it only twice, for the final omission of the 20th following the second 30th on which she did not see was not an uprooting, since she did not change to the 30th then and did not see until the 20th of the month, which was her veses. The Rosh likewise wrote that if a woman skips her veses even three times, but does not see in the interim at another fixed time and then returns to see at the time of her original veses, the veses is not uprooted until she establishes another veses. End quote. This makes the Ramban's words difficult. Why did he write that she changed twice to the 30th and then saw on the 20th, implying that she had not yet uprooted the 20th three times, while in the Gemara just cited the law applies even if she uprooted the 20th three times, as Rashi explicitly explained? It appears that the Gemara refers to calendar dates of the month. Her original sighting was on the 20th of the month, and the change to the 30th was likewise based on calendar dates. Therefore, although three times passed without her seeing on the 20th of the month, when on the fourth occasion she sees on the 20th it is evident that her flow returned at its proper time, since the original veses is identifiable and defined by the date of the month. The Ramban here, however, applies the law to an interval veses, meaning that she originally saw every 20 days and changed to every 30 days, as is explicit from the phrase “when the 30th day after the sighting on the 30th arrives,” etc. If he had said that three 30-day intervals passed, that on the third she did not see on the 30th, and that afterward she saw on the 20th, this would not demonstrate that she returned to her original 20-day interval. Before the present sighting on the 20th she had not seen since the middle 30th-day sighting, so 50 days separate the two sightings, and the original interval is not recognizable. Therefore, he would have needed to say that she now saw two consecutive sightings 20 days apart, for only then would the original interval be recognizable. Since the Ramban was not discussing two new sightings, he stated his law where she saw on the 20th day after the second 30th-day sighting, for there the 20-day interval is clearly recognizable. From this sighting on the 20th she must again be concerned when another 20 days arrive. The same would indeed apply if, after the third 30th-day interval, she saw twice with 20 days between them; the original veses would return to its place. There is an additional novelty here: the 30th day becomes permitted even though it has not yet been fully uprooted, because the first veses has now returned to its place. Toras HaBayis HaAruch, folio 174, explains the Gemara above also as discussing an interval veses. Its phrase “the 20th day arrived and she saw” means 20 days after the 30th-day sighting. See there. It appears to me that there is a scribal error in his wording, which reads: “and she is concerned for the 30th day; the 30th day arrived and she did not see; the 20th day arrived and she saw; the 30th day is permitted,” etc. It should be amended to read: “the 30th day arrived and she saw.” It appears that this was also the Ramban's text of the Gemara. According to this, if she failed to see on the 20th three times, the veses is indeed uprooted even if she then did not see on the following 30th and later saw on the 20th after those 30 days. In the Rosh, however, the text is as in our Gemara. This requires further study concerning the Beit Yosef and Shulchan Aruch, who did not note this.

<b>The same applies if she ceased, etc. </b>In the Tur the wording is not “the same applies,” but “and if she ceased,” etc. The Beit Yosef explained that this too refers to the preceding case, meaning that three onos passed, each consisting of the 20 days on which she was accustomed to see, and she did not see. Thus, when the Tur says “after 20 days had passed,” it means after three periods of 20 days. She is then considered one whose blood has ceased and need not thereafter be concerned. This implies that if three onos of 20 days have not yet passed, the 20th day within them remains forbidden. Although she did not see on the second 20th day, she is forbidden on the third 20th day. The Beit Yosef was therefore compelled to explain that “if she ceased” applies specifically where three onos passed without a sighting. The Tur's later statement that she returned to see means that she saw on the day of the veses, as written here in the Shulchan Aruch. The Beit Yosef similarly cited above in this siman, s.v. “the Raavad wrote,” that if she returned and saw on that very day, even after many interruptions, etc. This is difficult for me. Since the case necessarily concerns intervals of days, as explained above, how can she be described as returning to see “on the day of the veses”? Her veses depends on a fixed number of days between one sighting and the next, yet here she did not see for a long time, so there is no previous sighting from which to calculate an interval. See the end of this comment for my resolution. The Maharshal, however, explained the Tur as follows: the reason is not that she is considered one whose blood has ceased, and the case need not involve three onos. Even if she failed to see only once and did not change to another sighting at all, she is no longer concerned for that day. The reason is that there is no 20-day interval, since she did not see. Were we to require concern for the next 20th day, it would actually be a 40-day interval from the last sighting. We cannot say that the day on which she was expected to see but did not see was merely an accident and is treated as though she did see, for ultimately she did not see. This differs from a case in which she changed to another day, such as the 30th. There she must be concerned for the 20th day following the 30th-day sighting, because that is the interval of her established veses. But where she did not see at all, no interval can be calculated. This is why above, when she changed from 20 to 30, the Tur states that when the 20th day after the 30th-day sighting arrives she is forbidden, rather than being concerned for the 10th day after the 30th, which would be the calendar date corresponding to the original 20th day. We thus see that it is more logical to attach the concern to an actual sighting than to a date on which she had merely been expected to see, in accordance with Rav Huna son of Rav Yehoshua at the end of Benos Kusim. All the more so where there was no sighting at all, we do not base a calculation on something that never occurred. Therefore, she need not even be concerned for the ordinary onah of 30 days from the last sighting, for that concern applies specifically to a woman who has no fixed veses. This woman has a fixed veses, but failed to see at its time, and no present indication of impurity exists. The Tur's statement “if she returned to see, she must be concerned,” applies even if she ceased for three onos. Whenever she stops and later sees, she returns to her original veses and must be concerned for it counting from this new sighting. If she had ceased for three onos, she was considered one whose blood had ceased and is concerned only for the 20th day from this new sighting, for we assume that she has returned to her original veses. But if she had not ceased for three onos and then saw, she must also be concerned for the interval between her previous sighting and the present sighting, as the Tur wrote above that both dates are forbidden. She must be concerned for the 20th and 30th, and likewise for 40 and 50. If three onos passed, however, she need not be concerned for this new long interval, because she was considered one whose blood had ceased. This is unlike a monthly veses. If Rosh Chodesh passed and she did not see, she must still be concerned for the next Rosh Chodesh until three Roshei Chodashim pass without a sighting, for a monthly veses must be uprooted three times. If not, we attribute the missed Rosh Chodesh to an incidental occurrence and she remains concerned for the next one. An interval veses is different. As soon as the 20th day arrives and she does not see, there is no veses for that day, since the interval depends on one sighting following another, and she does not know after how many days the next sighting will occur. Although one does not respond to a lion, and my father-in-law of blessed memory also cited and praised the Maharshal's view, it nevertheless appears astonishing to me. <i data-commentator="Nekudot HaKesef" data-label="♯" data-order="7"></i>First, the Tur wrote that she returned to see, whether she had ceased to see or had changed to unequal sightings. This necessarily refers to ceasing for three onos, as the Maharshal himself also understood. Accordingly, the Tur's earlier words “if she ceased” must likewise concern the same circumstance, for the later statement refers back to it. Second, why did the Tur say “she ceased and did not see after the 20th day passed”? There are two unnecessary expressions. Why say “she ceased,” which repeats the same idea? And why say “after the 20th day passed” rather than simply “if she did not see on the 20th day, she need not be concerned”? It must mean three onos. She failed to see on the 20th day and then ceased for another three onos after that 20th day passed, seeing nothing at all. Third, according to the Maharshal there would be a distinction between interval vestos and monthly vestos in this matter. Yet the Tur later writes, “the uprooting of an interval veses is like the uprooting of a monthly veses,” implying that they are entirely equal, except that one is tied to its interval and one to its month. My father-in-law wished to insert the Maharshal's distinction into the phrase “one is concerned for its day,” interpreting it to mean only if she sees on that day, while for a monthly veses she is concerned for the month even if she does not see. This interpretation requires no refutation, for it is not implied by the wording at all. Furthermore, at the beginning of the siman the Tur explicitly equates interval vestos and date vestos regarding the rule that they are not uprooted in fewer than three occurrences. Just as a date veses is not uprooted by one failure to see, the same applies to an interval veses, for the Tur grouped them together. A further proof appears from Toras HaBayis HaAruch, folio 174: “How so? She saw, and after 20 days she saw again. When 20 days arrive she must be concerned for that day. If she saw again, she must also be concerned for the next 20 days. If she saw then, it is an established veses. If she did not see, it is uprooted and she need no longer be concerned for it.” End quote. This shows that only where the veses was not yet established, because she failed to see on the third occurrence, is she no longer concerned for the next 20th day. If it had already become established and she later failed to see on a 20th day, she certainly remains concerned when the next 20th day arrives. A further clear proof can be drawn from the very passage that the Maharshal cited in support of his position. At the end of Benos Kusim we rule like Rav Huna son of Rav Yehoshua that the 20-day interval is counted from the day she changed, meaning from the 30th-day sighting, and not from the 20th day on which she had been expected to see. Toras HaBayis HaAruch, folio 174, explains: “If she were to count 20 days from the 20th day on which she had been expected to see, she would be concerned lest the flow arrive early, after a shorter interval than her present sighting. This is unreasonable: she changed by delaying, and we would be concerned that she might now advance.” End quote. This shows that there was indeed a basis to count the interval from the day she had been expected to see, and we reject it only because she actually changed by delaying to a 30-day sighting. It follows that had she not seen at all, we would count the 20-day interval from the day on which she was accustomed to see, contrary to the Maharshal, who holds that it is impossible to calculate an interval from a time at which she was merely expected to see. The distinction that he made between interval vestos and monthly vestos when one sighting is missed is entirely unclear to me. An actual sighting does not cause the following interval sighting in such a manner that if this one is absent the next is also absent. What source is there for that? Since this woman has not become one whose blood has ceased, we may say that her failure to see once at the interval was incidental, that she still has excess blood, and that her flow will return at its normal interval, just as with a monthly veses. One cannot bring proof from the Beit Yosef at the end of the siman regarding a woman whose pregnancy and nursing period passed. There he wrote that otherwise it would be impossible to say she is concerned for the interval she had been accustomed to, since there is now no sighting from which to calculate an interval. That case is different, for during pregnancy and nursing she was entirely removed from all her vestos and is now like a new woman. She must therefore establish the interval anew. Here, however, she remains within her ordinary cycle, and it has not been uprooted three times. An interval may therefore be calculated from the day on which she was expected to see, as though she had seen. Thus, it appears to me that the Beit Yosef's explanation is primary, and that the Tur's case specifically concerns cessation for three onos. As for the Tur's later words “she returned to see,” it appears that each case follows its own law. Where she changed her sighting, it means that she returned to see after the original interval, 20 days after the sighting on the 34th day, and thus it is recognizable as the original veses. Where she ceased entirely, whenever she sees again she need only be concerned for the 20th day thereafter. This accords with the Ramban at the end of this siman, who wrote that once the days of pregnancy and nursing have passed and she sees once, she returns to her veses even if she did not see at the time of her veses. End quote. All the more so here should we say so, for both rulings are the Ramban's. This is also how the Shulchan Aruch rules at the end of this siman. It is therefore astonishing that here the Shulchan Aruch wrote that even after cessation she must return and see on the day of the veses, for this is unnecessary according to his own ruling at the end of the siman. Moreover, since this case concerns intervals of days, an interval cannot exist until there have been two sightings. It therefore appears that the Shulchan Aruch's phrase “on the day of the veses” at the end of this seif is imprecise. What I have written appears correct from every side, with God's help.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And even if she returned to see at those, etc. </b>The general rule is that once she ceased for three onos, the original veses is entirely gone, and she is as though she had never seen before. She must begin again with three sightings, as at the outset, in order to become established as a woman who sees blood. Even if she ceases a second time and then returns, failing to see for another three onos, we do not say that she now returns once more to her earlier status and again requires three new sightings after the second cessation. We do not go that far. Rather, the first sighting after the first cessation begins the process of establishing her. If she later sees two more times, whenever they occur, she becomes established as a woman who sees. Consequently, there are two possible patterns among the three sightings after the first cessation. If they occur in a manner capable of establishing a veses, namely on the same date three times, or through four sightings creating three equal intervals including the first sighting after the cessation, she establishes a veses like any other woman. She can also establish an interval veses through only two further intervals after the first post-cessation sighting. Thus, if after that first sighting she sees twice more at 90-day intervals, we combine the final sighting before the cessation as the starting point of the first interval, yielding four sightings and three equal intervals. In my view, even if she had no established veses before the cessation and saw only once before it, once she afterward sees three times at 90-day intervals she establishes a 90-day interval veses. This is obvious. If the sightings after the cessation cannot create an established veses because they are not equally aligned, the three sightings nevertheless establish her as a woman who sees blood from that time onward, and her law is that of a woman with no fixed veses, as explained in siman 187. This is unlike the Rashba's statement, cited by the Beit Yosef s.v. “and what he wrote, even if she returned,” that a minor and an elderly woman need not be concerned at all for a non-established veses, which the Rama brings in seif 28. That applies only before she has seen three times, when she is not yet established as a woman who sees blood. The woman described here has seen three times and is certainly established as a woman who sees blood, so her law is that of an adult woman without a fixed veses. The Tur and Shulchan Aruch write here that she need not be concerned until she returns and establishes it three times, etc. The word “establishes it” might imply that the sightings must specifically occur in a pattern that creates a fixed veses, and that if they do not, she need not be concerned at all. This is not so. The phrase refers back to the case where she originally had a fixed veses. It means that she need not be concerned for that original veses unless she reestablishes it through three aligned sightings. Otherwise she becomes merely like a woman with no fixed veses. Toras HaBayis HaAruch does not say “until she establishes it,” but rather “until she sees three times,” as I have explained. Toras HaBayis HaAruch, cited by the Beit Yosef under the heading “the language of the Rashba,” states: “He called the first sighting the first sighting she saw after three onos had passed, for now three equal intervals emerge among them,” etc. End quote. The Beit Yosef was troubled, for there appear to be only three sightings and two intervals. He therefore forced an explanation that the “first sighting” means the sighting she sees after the initial sighting following the three onos. He strengthened this interpretation from the later statement of Rav Gidal: the first sighting suffices at its time, and the second renders her tamei retroactively for 24 hours. <i data-commentator="Nekudot HaKesef" data-label="♯" data-order="11"></i>According to the Rashba, who holds that Rav Gidal's ruling follows halacha, the “first” would appear to mean the first sighting at the end of the onah, yet he says that at the second sighting she becomes tamei retroactively. According to Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel, however, the veses is not established until a third sighting at the end of an onah. The Beit Yosef therefore concluded that “first” means the first sighting after the one about which the question was asked, namely after she again saw at the onos. The “second” would then actually be the third sighting after the sighting at the end of the onah. Together with the sighting before the three onos, there are four sightings and three intervals. End quote. I stood astonished for a long moment at our master the Beit Yosef. According to his approach, even after the second cessation she is treated like after the first cessation, and does not become established as a woman who sees blood until three new sightings after the second cessation. Thus his “second” is actually the third. If so, Rav Gidal's first and second statements would be identical, and why did he say in the first that both the first and second sightings suffice at their own time, but in the second that the second sighting renders her tamei retroactively? This is astonishing. Furthermore, the Rashba explicitly wrote, as cited by the Beit Yosef: “Any girl who has not reached the age of seeing is presumed to have no blood, and the first sighting is merely incidental and does not join until she becomes established through other sightings. Therefore, the second sighting is itself only one sighting and cannot establish the first. But when she sees a third time, the first is established together with the two later sightings, and those two reveal that the first was not incidental.” End quote. The “first” plainly refers to the first sighting after the first cessation. Were it to refer to a later sighting, why should that one become established through subsequent sightings any more than the sighting immediately after the cessation? Surely that sighting too should become established through those that follow it. If so, why did Rav Gidal say in his first statement that the second sighting suffices only at its own time? According to the Beit Yosef's explanation, that is already the third sighting after the post-cessation sighting and should render her tamei retroactively because she has seen three times after the cessation. <br><b>Rather, </b>the matter is clear as I wrote above. After the second cessation she is not treated like after the first cessation in the sense of requiring two entirely new sightings to join with the first after the second cessation. One additional sighting suffices to establish the first sighting after the initial cessation, and together with another third sighting she has three sightings. These may occur at any time. If they are aligned at 90-day intervals, she establishes a fixed veses. Even if they are not aligned, whether the second post-cessation sighting did not occur after 90 days, which is Rav Gidal's first case, or the second did occur after 90 days but the third occurred at the ordinary onah rather than after 90 days, which is Rav Gidal's second case, she is nevertheless established as a woman who sees blood and becomes tamei retroactively from the third sighting onward. This is why Rav Gidal said in his second statement that the second sighting renders her tamei retroactively, since it is the third sighting counting from the first sighting after the initial cessation. The Rashba's words, “and he called it the first sighting,” are now also properly understood. Rav Gidal taught that she does not return to an established status regarding blood until she again sees three sightings. The Rashba was troubled that Rav Gidal called it the “first” sighting, which would appear to refer to the sighting before the cessation. In that case only two further sightings would seem necessary. He therefore explained that “first” refers to the sighting after the cessation, and then she indeed becomes established through three sightings. The Rashba concludes that now three equal intervals emerge. According to our explanation that “first” refers to the sighting after the cessation, the Baraisa's statement of three 90-day intervals is settled. Had “first” referred to the sighting before the cessation, there would be only three sightings and two 90-day intervals. According to our explanation there are three equal intervals because we now combine the sighting before the cessation for calculating the interval, although we do not combine it for establishing her as a woman who sees blood. Everything is resolved, with God's help. <br><b>I was led </b>by Hashem on the path of truth, for after writing this I saw in Bedek HaBayis on Toras HaBayis HaAruch, folio 170, that he likewise wrote that the “first sighting” is the first sighting after the first cessation, and that after the second cessation the first sighting following it also helps, so that if she then sees a third sighting at a shorter interval, all the more so does it help to render her tamei retroactively, since even a more distant third sighting helps. He wrote that the Rashba challenged the approach I have set forth, and that the Rashba in Mishmeres HaBayis complained that Bedek HaBayis attributed that approach to him, when in truth the Rashba himself had written it in proper order. Thus, I have, thank God, arrived at the Rashba's own view, as he himself testified that he explained it in this manner. In truth, this is the straightforward explanation I stated above. The Beit Yosef, followed by my father-in-law of blessed memory, did not fulfill the obligation of careful analysis in this matter.
