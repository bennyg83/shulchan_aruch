<b>(Taz s.k. 18) He wishes</b> to prove that even though she saw on Rosh Chodesh Iyar, she must still be concerned for the ninth of Iyar because of a twenty-day interval. His words are not convincing. His statement that we do not find that a sighting at a changed time uproots the concern that previously rested upon her is not valid. Here, once she saw on Rosh Chodesh, she changed her interval to twelve days, and since she had not established a fixed veset of intervals, the previous interval is uprooted by a single change. Regarding his further statement, “And moreover, if she were to see, etc.,” I say that this is indeed so. In such a case she would not establish a veset, because there are not three equal intervals. Since she saw on Rosh Chodesh, she uprooted the twenty-day veset that had previously been pending. The Perishah also holds this view; see there, examine his words carefully, and you will find it so. His further statement that there is a clear proof from the Tur at the end of this siman, in the case where she saw twice on Rosh Chodesh, is also imprecise. There the discussion concerns a veset of the month, and in that case the Bach and Perishah also agree, as stated in se'ifim 30 and 31. His objection to the Bach was correctly directed, but the Bach's ruling is nevertheless true, while the Taz's attempted proof to the contrary is no proof at all. There she had a fixed date on the twentieth. Therefore, if she had seen on the twentieth, we would not say that she changed her interval merely because she saw ten days later. She would still have had to be concerned ten days after the sighting on the tenth, namely, on the twentieth. This differs from the present case, where no veset of intervals had been established. Since all his proofs are nullified, we return to the law that if she saw on Rosh Chodesh Iyar, she need not be concerned for the ninth of Iyar. This is implied by the plain meaning of Ramban's words. Indeed, the Taz himself wrote that the Rama's phrase “if she did not see” cannot comfortably be explained as merely stating a greater novelty.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>(Taz s.k. 28) At first glance, this is contrary to the Gemara, etc.</b> He concludes that Ramban had a different version of the Gemara than the Rosh. This is difficult, for the Tur cites Ramban's ruling without qualification. Rather, the primary explanation is as I wrote in the Shach, s.k. 40. When the Tur and Ramban write, “If, after changing once or twice to the thirtieth day, she then saw at the end of twenty days, etc.,” they mean that she saw at the end of twenty days after having seen a third time after thirty days. Accordingly, although she did not see three times on the twentieth day, the twentieth-day veset was not uprooted, because she saw only twice on the thirtieth day, while the third sighting occurred after thirty days. Therefore, when she then saw at the end of twenty days following the sighting that occurred after thirty days, the twenty-day veset returned to its place. The Perishah also explains it this way; see there.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>(Taz s.k. 31) He elaborated</b> in refuting Maharshal, but none of his arguments appear correct. The plain language of the Tur is understood by every careful reader in accordance with Maharshal. The Taz writes that just as the phrase “she changed” refers to all three onos, so too the Tur's preceding phrase, “If she interrupted, etc.,” must refer to the same manner because it follows upon that discussion. This is not correct. The phrase “if she interrupted” denotes an interruption, meaning that there was a break in her pattern of sightings, namely, she did not see when twenty days had passed. By contrast, the phrase “if she ceased seeing” denotes a complete cessation. The first clause teaches one novelty, and the latter clause teaches another. The first clause teaches that although she interrupted the pattern, meaning that she did not see even once on the twentieth day, she need no longer be concerned for the fortieth day. The latter clause teaches that even if she ceased seeing entirely for three onos, the original veset is not uprooted if she afterward returned and saw in accordance with the original veset.

His further statement that, according to Maharshal, there would be a distinction in this respect between intervals of days and a veset of the month, although the Tur later writes explicitly that the uprooting of a veset of the month follows the same law as the uprooting of a veset of intervals, presents no difficulty. They are certainly equal regarding uprooting. Whenever she uproots the pattern by interrupting for three onos and does not see on the twentieth day even once, or when she changes to three equal sightings, then just as a veset of intervals is uprooted, so too a veset of the month is uprooted. Nevertheless, when she merely interrupted and did not see on the twentieth day, although she need not be concerned for a twenty-day interval, namely, for a sighting on the fortieth day, the veset of the twentieth day itself has not been uprooted. Therefore, when she sees again, she must immediately be concerned for the twentieth day. By contrast, when she interrupted for three onos, the veset is uprooted. Consequently, even if she sees again, she need not be concerned for the twentieth day unless she once again sees on the twentieth day, at which point the original veset returns. This answers everything the Taz wrote afterward, as I will explain.

His further statement that the Tur writes at the beginning of the siman that a veset of intervals and a veset of days are equal in that they are not uprooted in fewer than three occurrences also presents no difficulty. Certainly, the veset is not uprooted in fewer than three occurrences. Nevertheless, she need not be concerned for a sighting on the fortieth day, because the intervals are not equal. The statement that it is not uprooted in fewer than three occurrences means that she need not be concerned for a veset of the month, as Maharshal wrote. The same applies in that, if she sees again afterward, she must immediately be concerned for the twentieth day, unlike a case in which she ceased seeing three times.

His additional proof from Toras HaBayis HaAroch is no proof. Its language there is: “From what point does she count the twenty days? From the sighting on the thirtieth day, and not from the sighting on the twentieth day, on which she was accustomed to see, because the flow had not been accustomed to arrive in fewer than twenty days. Were she to count twenty days from the twentieth day, it would emerge, etc.” Thus, the essential reason is that the flow was not accustomed to arrive in fewer than twenty days. The same applies in the converse situation, where the flow was not accustomed to arrive after more than twenty days. Therefore, when she did not see on the twentieth day, she need not be concerned for the fortieth day.

The Taz writes that he did not understand the distinction made by Maharshal. In my view, however, it is readily understood. With a veset of the month, although she did not see on this Rosh Chodesh, one may say that it was merely incidental that she did not see on this Rosh Chodesh, but that she will see on another Rosh Chodesh, since she is accustomed to see on Rosh Chodesh and may again see in accordance with her practice. With a veset of intervals, however, one cannot say this. If she sees only after forty days, she has not seen at an interval of twenty days. Thus, all of Maharshal's words are correct.

The Taz then writes that the Tur's language is difficult and that the Shulchan Aruch is therefore astonishing. This is imprecise. Here, because she interrupted and did not see for three onos, the twenty-day veset was automatically uprooted entirely. Even if she later sees, she need not be concerned for the twentieth day, since she interrupted for three onos. This is unlike the case at the end of this siman. There, her veset was not uprooted. During pregnancy and nursing she merely did not have to be concerned for it because she was presumed not to see blood during those periods. Therefore, when the periods of pregnancy and nursing ended, as soon as she saw again she had to be concerned for the intervals to which she had previously been accustomed. That is not so here.

Accordingly, the Taz's statement that the Shulchan Aruch's phrase “on the day of the veset” at the end of the se'if is imprecise is itself incorrect. The phrase is exact and forceful. Since she ceased seeing for three onos, the original veset does not return until she again sees on the day of the original veset. This means that she first sees once, and then sees again twenty days later. At that point the original veset returns in every respect, and she need not be concerned for a veset of the month, for it once again has all the laws of a fixed veset. All this is clear.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>(Taz s.k. 32) And at first glance, etc.</b> The Shach already preceded him in raising this point in s.k. 59.

<b>Gloss.</b> In the glosses it is written that Rashba, the Tur, and the other poskim follow the law of the Gemara that a woman does not establish a veset during her days of niddah or during her days of zivah. For example, if she saw on the first of the month and again on the fifth, which falls within the days of niddah stemming from the first sighting, then even if this occurred three times she does not establish a veset for the fifth of the month, but only for the first of the month. Likewise, if she saw on the first of the month and again on the fifteenth, and the same applies to the eighteenth, which is still within her days of zivah, as the Perishah also wrote, she does not establish a veset for the fifteenth, because each sighting on the fifteenth falls within the days of zivah generated by the sighting on the first.

This applies when the later sighting came from an already open source, as I explained. If, however, it came from a closed source, such as where she saw twice on Rosh Chodesh, and on the third occasion she saw on the twenty-fifth of the month and again on Rosh Chodesh, then although the third Rosh Chodesh sighting occurred during the days of niddah stemming from the sighting on the twenty-fifth, she nevertheless establishes a veset for Rosh Chodesh. This is because the first two sightings came from a closed source, while on the twenty-fifth an additional abundance of blood caused the flow to arrive early. She must also be concerned for the earlier date, lest she be establishing a separate veset. Although above, in se'if 13, the gloss states that once she establishes a veset she need no longer be concerned for a prior unfixed veset, this case differs because the third Rosh Chodesh sighting is not regarded as a full sighting, since it came from an open source. This is the gloss of the Derishah, and the Bach wrote likewise.

The Mechaber and Rama omitted all these laws. It appears that they did so because the Tur and the poskim wrote in the name of Ramban that, since Jewish women nowadays accepted upon themselves the stringency of making no distinction between the days of niddah and the days of zivah, as explained above at the end of siman 183 in the Tur and Beis Yosef, there is likewise no distinction between them regarding the establishment of a veset. Therefore, if she saw every fifteen days, she establishes a veset.

In truth, however, it is clear that Ramban said this only stringently, so that she may establish a veset even during the days of niddah and zivah, but not leniently. He certainly did not intend to dispute the Gemara. It has already been explained in many places above that some laws of an unfixed veset are more stringent than those of a fixed veset. A woman who has no fixed veset must be concerned for the veset of the month and for the interval, whereas one who has a fixed veset need not be concerned for them, as explained above in se'if 13. Rashba in Toras HaBayis HaAroch and the Maggid Mishneh likewise imply that Ramban ruled only stringently and not leniently.

The Perishah in se'if 93 also implies this. He writes: “Accordingly, even nowadays one must rule stringently that a veset may be established during the days of zivah and niddah. A practical difference concerns uprooting, for even if it was uprooted twice, she must still be concerned for the third occasion.” His extra phrase “even nowadays one must rule stringently,” and the fact that he identifies only a stringent practical consequence and not a lenient one, show that we hold there is no distinction only for purposes of stringency. Far be it from us to dispute the Gemara for purposes of leniency. The Mechaber and Rama should therefore have cited these laws. In any event, we learn that whenever this siman states that she has established a veset, if the consequence would be lenient, this applies only when the sightings did not occur during her days of niddah or zivah. This concludes the gloss.

<b>(There, s.k. 39) His distinction</b> in the words of Raavad is not correct. Since we say that its law is like a veset of days alone, why should she not also have to be concerned before the onah? Furthermore, the poskim and the Tur later write that she must separate for the entire onah, just as with a veset of days alone, which implies that it has literally the law of a veset of days alone. Moreover, Raavad there also states: “Before the veset she is permitted, and if she examined herself at the time of the veset and did not see, she is permitted after the veset, etc.” This appears to refer back to the preceding statement, meaning that she is permitted after the veset.

Rather, the meaning is that there Raavad teaches that even if she has a fixed day for those bodily signs, but no fixed veset for seeing blood, such as when she yawned or sneezed repeatedly on successive Roshei Chodesh but did not see, she has nevertheless established a fixed day for those signs. If she then yawned on two successive Roshei Chodesh and saw blood afterward, she must be concerned for a veset of yawning alone. Here, however, the discussion concerns a veset that itself arrives at a known time and becomes fixed. In that case, although the yawning has also become fixed, its law is like that of a veset of days alone.

The words of the Maggid Mishneh at the beginning of chapter 8 likewise imply this, as I explained. The Beis Yosef cites him below, at the beginning of page 137, in the passage beginning, “The Maggid Mishneh wrote,” and comments that his language is imprecise. According to what I have written, however, it is precise.

Nevertheless, Responsa Rashba, siman 838, as cited by the Shulchan Aruch above in siman 187, se'if 11, implies that even for an unfixed veset she must separate for the entire onah, in accordance with the view of the Beis Yosef and as I wrote in the Siftei Kohen, s.k. 78. This is likewise implied by the plain language of the Tur and Shulchan Aruch here, and this appears to be the primary ruling in practice.

<b>(Taz s.k. 43)</b> His explanation of the words of Rashba and Rav Gidal is correct. However, his practical conclusion that a young girl who saw three times must be concerned for an unfixed veset appears entirely incorrect to me. If so, how could Rashba, the Tur, the Mechaber, and Rama all write without qualification that she is not concerned for an unfixed veset at all? Rather, it appears that Rashba's entire analysis was only intended to prove that, regarding impurity, the law of a young girl is equal to that of other women, so that the first sighting joins the count. The practical consequence concerns a fixed veset, for even with a young girl the first sighting joins toward establishing it. When she has no fixed veset, however, she need not be concerned at all that she may see blood. This is implied by the plain language of Rashba, the Tur, and the later authorities in many places. As long as she has not seen, she need not be concerned that she may see, since she has not established a veset. The general rule is that a young girl need not be concerned for an unfixed veset.
