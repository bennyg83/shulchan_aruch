**** ENGLISH ****
<b>A minor whose relative died, and even if he became an adult, and so forth.</b> Maharam and his student Rosh disagree. Maharam holds that once the minor becomes an adult, he must observe seven and thirty days from the time he attains majority. He compares it to one who hears a recent report during a festival and observes seven and thirty afterward. Although festival days can count toward thirty because some thirty-day restrictions such as pressing and haircutting are observed then, during minority no mourning was observed at all. One might distinguish that in the festival case the person is legally obligated and only the day prevents mourning, while a minor is not obligated at all. Maharam rejects that distinction because mitzvot are not subject to permanent rejection. Rosh accepts the distinction and says that because the minor was exempt when mourning should have begun, the obligation falls away permanently. He compares this to a minor who becomes an adult between Pesach Rishon and Pesach Sheni according to the view that the second Pesach is only compensation for the first: one not obligated in the first is not obligated in the second. Likewise, a later recent report is merely compensation for mourning that should have begun at burial. If the person knew of the death but was not legally obligated then, there is nothing to compensate later. Rabbenu Yerucham says Maharam should be followed because he was Rosh's teacher, while Beit Yosef rules like Rosh because the halachah follows the lenient opinion in mourning. Shulchan Aruch therefore exempts the person.

In my youth such a case came before me when the minor became an adult within shivah. I found Rosh's reasoning difficult. Mourning is not simply a compensatory obligation. Each day of the seven has its own status, as seen here in seif 2, where one who neglected part of shivah does not make it up. Why then should the law depend on a concept of tashlumin? I raised this with my father-in-law. He replied that Shulchan Aruch itself appears inconsistent. In Perek Mi Shemeto there is another dispute between Rosh and Maharam regarding Havdalah. R. Yehudah was an onen on Motzaei Shabbos and did not make Havdalah. After burial the next day he still did not make Havdalah, reasoning that since he was exempt when the obligation first arrived, it was permanently deferred, like the case of one unable to bring the first Pesach. Maharam disagreed and held that he must make Havdalah the next day, because one who did not make Havdalah on Motzaei Shabbos may continue making it during the week. Rosh sided with R. Yehudah. Yet Shulchan Aruch above, siman 341, rules that the onen must make Havdalah afterward, like Maharam, while here it exempts the minor, like Rosh. My father-in-law therefore ruled consistently like Maharam in both places and required the now-adult minor to observe seven and thirty.

<br><b>The Gemara states</b> in Perek Nigmar HaDin: with due respect to these great authorities, I believe the Gemara itself clarifies the matter. Sanhedrin 47 discusses those executed by beit din. They were not buried in family graves because full atonement had not yet occurred. The Gemara asks: if burial brings atonement, why do their relatives not mourn? One answer says atonement also requires decomposition of the flesh. Rav Ashi says no: burial itself is enough once the deceased experiences some of the anguish of the grave, but mourning begins immediately upon closure of the grave and atonement comes only afterward. Therefore, since mourning was inapplicable at the moment it normally begins, it remains permanently deferred. Rashi explains: since they were rejected from mourning at the beginning, they remain rejected afterward. Although one who buries during a festival does observe mourning after the festival, that case differs because mourning was not wholly rejected: public consolers still come and some mourning-related conduct remains.

<br><b>And now</b> this is a kal vachomer for our case. In the case of those executed by beit din, there was initially no mourning obligation anywhere because the deceased was not yet fit for mourning until atonement. Even so, once the ordinary starting point passed without mourning, the obligation never begins later. All the more so here, where mourning did exist generally but this particular person was exempt at the critical starting time because of minority. Rav Ashi is the later authority and the halachah follows him. Even the first answer in the Gemara does not necessarily disagree with the principle that once rejected, mourning remains rejected; it may disagree only as to when atonement occurs. Beit Yosef in siman 345 also cites the conclusion according to Rav Ashi that those executed by beit din receive atonement after death and burial but are not mourned because mourning begins at closure of the grave before the atonement takes effect.

Thus the reason for exemption is not Rosh's concept of tashlumin. Rather, Chazal fixed mourning to begin at the moment of greatest bitterness, namely when the grave is closed. One who is legally exempt at that crucial moment never acquires a later mourning obligation. This is why both those executed by beit din and a minor who becomes an adult are exempt. It also explains Rashi's distinction from burial during a festival, where mourning was not entirely absent. Havdalah is different. Its proper time was established from the outset as extending beyond Motzaei Shabbos, whether through the entire following period or until Tuesday night. Havdalah recited later is therefore not compensation for a missed moment but performance within its original time. The same is true of the time for Maariv and Shema extending through the night, and of a minor who becomes an adult during Sukkot and then becomes obligated in sukkah for the remaining days. Mourning, however, is fundamentally tied to its initial moment of bitterness. Therefore, practical halachah follows Shulchan Aruch: the person is exempt from mourning when he becomes an adult, but an onen who was unable to make Havdalah must make it afterward. The rulings are consistent.
