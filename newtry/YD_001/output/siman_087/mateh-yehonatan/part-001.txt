(סימן פ"ז סעיף ג') <b>ובשר חיה ועוף כו' מותר בבישול ובהנאה כו'</b>. דין זה כתב הרמב"ם וצ"ל מהא דפ' כ"ה דף קי"ג גבי פרט לחיה ועוף טמאה ופריך לשמואל הא בעי גדי לרבות את המתה ומשני קסבר שמואל א"ח ע"א וכו' א"כ לדידן דקיי"ל דאין איסור חל על איסור ע"כ מבעיא לן גדי לרבות חלב ומתה כקושית הש"ס וא"כ מנ"ל דגדי פרט לחי' ועוף ובהמה טמאה וא"ל דס"ל להרמב"ם דזה יותר מסתבר לאוקמי פרט לחי' ועוף מלרבות המתה דלא כשמואל א"כ מאי דוחקא להש"ס התם בדף קט"ז דס"ל לר"ע אחע"א הל"ל דזה יותר מסתבר לאוקמי הקרא פרט לחי' ועוף מלרבות המתה א"ו אי לאו דס"ל אחע"א הוי מוקמינן הקרא לרבות המתה וא"כ קשה דאי' שם איתמר המבשל חלב בחלב ר' אמי ור' אסי ח"א לוקה וח"א אינו לוקה על אכילה כ"ע ל"פ דאסור כ"פ אבישול מ"ד לוקה קסבר חד איסורא הוא ומ"ד אינו לוקה קסבר מדאפקינהו רחמנא בלשון בישול להורות דכיון דעל אכילה לא לקי גם אבישול לא לקי א"כ מ"ד אבישול לקי משום דחד איסורא הוא וא"כ אבישול ל"צ קרא לרבות המתה ואי גדי לרבות המתה לאסור באכילה אתא ז"א במשמעות הלשון של רבוי של גדי ומ"ש דמיותרא דקרא דלא תבשל דרשינן על אכילה מ"מ ודאי רבוי דכתב לא אאכילה קאי לחוד דהא לא כתיב אכילה בקרא להדיא א"כ ודאי גדי למעט חיה ועוף הוא דאתיא והא דאצרכוה הגמרא לעיל למימר דס"ל איסור חל על איסור היינו למ"ד כיון דעל אכילה לא לקי אבישול נמי לא לקי א"כ שפיר צריך גדי לרבות המתה היינו לחייבו אבישול לכך הוכרחו דס"ל אחע"א א"כ לק"מ על הרמב"ם דס"ל כמ"ד אבישול לקי א"כ גדי פרט לחי' ועוף אתי ודו"ק:
**** ENGLISH ****
(Siman 87, seif 3.) And the flesh of a wild animal and fowl etc. is permitted in cooking and in benefit etc. This ruling was written by the Rambam, and it must be explained from the passage in chapter Kol HaBasar, daf 113, regarding that the word kid serves as an exclusion for wild animals and impure fowl. And the Talmud questions this according to Shmuel: but we require the word kid to include the carcass! And it answers: Shmuel holds that ein issur chal al issur, etc. If so, according to us, who hold that ein issur chal al issur, we necessarily require the word kid to include both the prohibition of cooking with milk and the carcass, as the Talmud's question implies. And if so, from where do we derive that kid comes to exclude wild animals, fowl, and non-kosher animals? And do not say that the Rambam holds that it is more reasonable to establish the word kid as excluding wild animals and fowl rather than as including the carcass — unlike Shmuel — for if so, what compelled the Talmud there on daf 116, where it holds that Rabbi Akiva maintains issur chal al issur, to say that it is more reasonable to establish the verse as excluding wild animals and fowl rather than as including the carcass? Rather, perforce: were it not that Rabbi Akiva holds issur chal al issur, we would establish the verse as including the carcass. And if so, a difficulty arises, for it states there: it was stated — regarding one who cooks forbidden fat in milk — Rabbi Ami and Rabbi Asi: one says he is liable to lashes and one says he is not liable. Regarding eating, everyone agrees it is forbidden. The dispute is regarding cooking. The one who says he is liable holds that it is one single prohibition. And the one who says he is not liable holds: since the Torah expressed it in the language of cooking, this comes to teach that since one is not liable to lashes for eating it, one is also not liable to lashes for cooking it. If so, according to the one who says one is liable for cooking because it is one single prohibition, we do not need a verse for cooking to include the carcass. And if the word kid comes to include the carcass in order to forbid eating it — that is not consistent with the semantic implication of the inclusive force of the word kid. And as for what is said — that from the superfluous element in the verse you shall not cook we derive the prohibition regarding eating — nevertheless, certainly the inclusion written in the verse does not apply to eating alone, for eating is not stated explicitly in the verse. Therefore, certainly kid comes to exclude wild animals and fowl. And as for the fact that the Talmud earlier required the explanation that Rabbi Akiva holds issur chal al issur — that is according to the one who says: since one is not liable to lashes for eating it, one is also not liable to lashes for cooking it. In that case, we indeed need the word kid to include the carcass, that is, to render one liable for cooking it. Therefore they were compelled to conclude that he holds issur chal al issur. Thus there is no difficulty with the Rambam, who holds like the one who says one is liable for cooking; therefore kid comes to exclude wild animals and fowl. Investigate this carefully.

**** ENGLISH ****
(There, seif 4, in the hagahah) And it appears that according to this, all the more so it is forbidden to cook l'chatchila, etc. See in the Acharonim what they seek to resolve the difficulty of the Darkei Moshe, who left the words of the Rashba requiring further study. And one can say: granted, regarding non-kosher meat with kosher milk, etc. — there is reason to say that since in any case it is forbidden on account of its non-kosher status, it is an uncommon matter that anyone would come to cook it, and regarding an uncommon matter the Rabbis did not issue a decree upon it. Which is not the case with human milk, for by this very reason there is no prohibition whatsoever, and there is reason to be concerned, etc. And note carefully.

(שם סעיף ה') <b>ביצים הנמצאים בעופות כו' אע"פ שהיא מעורה בגידים כו'</b>. עיין בט"ז מה שהקשה על דעת הרשב"ם והמרדכי ומדברי הש"ך סק"י נראה שהוא מפרש שהרשב"ם דכתב שיהיה דווקא כדי קליפה קשה לא שהוא מפרש כן פי' דביצי' גמורות אלא משום דס"ל כראב"י ומספקא מה מעורת בגידין ואין מוכרח דוודאי שפיר י"ל דהרשב"ם מפרש כן דברי הברייתא דביצים גמורות היינו שנגמרה קליפה והא דקאי התם בגמ' כמאן כראב"י דמשמע דראב"י מחמיר טפי דשפיר א"ל דמחמיר טפי ופי' הברייתא כך הוא דקאמר ומצא בה ביצים גמורות מותר לאכלן בכותח היינו אפי' לכתחלה אבל בדווקא אם נגמר הקליפה אבל אם לא נגמר הקליפה הקשה אסור לאוכלן לכתחלה בכותח אבל בדיעבד משמע לת"ק דליכא איסור כלל בין גמורות לשאין גמורות לענין דיעבד א"כ לת"ק לא מטמא בגדים בבית הבליעה וע"ז קאמר הרב"י אם היו מעורות בגידין אסורות אף דיעבד וקא' שפיר כמאן כראב"י דלרבנן לא מחלקינן כלל בין גמורות לאינן גמורות לענין דיעבד א"כ לא היה מטמאה הבגדים אבית הבליעה א"ו כראב"י או י"ל דזה ודאי גם הרשב"ם סבר דת"ק ס"ל כיון שנגמר החלבון והחלמון נמי מותר אלא כיון דאין כל א' בקי לשער איזה נקרא נגמר ונתנו שיעור שיהיה צריך שיהיה קליפה קשה דאז יצא מן הספק וראב"י ס"ל דכל איסור דחלבון וחלמון לא תליא אלא במעורת בגידין וא"כ אף שנגמר החלבון והחלמון אם הוא מעורת בגידין נמי אסור א"כ שפיר קאמר התם כמאן כראב"י דקתני האוכל משלל של ביצים היינו אם הוא עדיין מעורת בגידין עדיין הוא נבילה א"כ ע"כ אתי' כראב"י דאי כרבנן היכי קתני סתמא האוכל משלל של ביצים כו' הא לרבנן פעמים שלל של ביצים לא הוי נבילה היינו אם נגמר החלבון והחלמון א"ו כראב"י אתי' דתלי' הכל במעורות ושפיר קאמר אשלל של ביצים כיון שהמה מעורות הוי כנבלה עצמה ודוק:
**** ENGLISH ****
(There, seif 5) "Eggs found in birds, etc., even though it is attached by sinews, etc." See in the Taz what he raised as a difficulty against the opinion of the Rashbam and the Mordechai. And from the words of the Shach, subsection 10, it appears that he interprets the position of the Rashbam — who wrote that it must specifically be developed to the point of having a hard shell — not as his interpretation of the definition of "fully formed eggs," but rather because he holds like Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov and is uncertain about the meaning of "attached by sinews." But this is not necessarily so, for one can certainly validly say that the Rashbam interprets the words of the baraita such that "fully formed eggs" means that the shell has been completed. And as for the fact that the Gemara there asks "in accordance with whom? Like Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov" — which implies that Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov is more stringent — one can validly say that he is indeed more stringent, and the interpretation of the baraita is as follows: it states, "if one found fully formed eggs in it, they are permitted to be eaten with kutach" — meaning even l'chatchila, but specifically if the shell has been completed; but if the hard shell has not been completed, it is forbidden to eat them l'chatchila with kutach. But b'dieved, it is implied from the view of the Tanna Kamma that there is no prohibition whatsoever — whether the eggs are fully formed or not fully formed — with regard to b'dieved. If so, according to the Tanna Kamma, the egg does not render garments impure at the gullet. And regarding this, the Beit Yosef states: if they were attached by sinews, they are forbidden even b'dieved. And this validly explains the Gemara's question "in accordance with whom? Like Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov," for according to the Rabbis, we do not distinguish at all between fully formed and not fully formed with regard to b'dieved — and if so, the egg would not render the garments impure at the gullet — therefore it must be like Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov. Or one can say that certainly the Rashbam also held that the Tanna Kamma's position is that once the white and the yolk have been completed, it is also permitted, but since not everyone is expert enough to assess which stage is called "completed," they gave the measure that there must be a hard shell, for then the egg has emerged from doubt. And Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov holds that the entire prohibition regarding the white and the yolk depends only on whether the egg is attached by sinews — and therefore, even if the white and yolk have been completed, if it is still attached by sinews, it is also forbidden. Therefore, it is valid that the Gemara there asks "in accordance with whom? Like Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov," for it teaches: one who eats from the cluster of eggs — meaning if they are still attached by sinews — they are still neveilah. Therefore it must be that the baraita follows Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov, for if it were according to the Rabbis, how could the baraita teach without qualification "one who eats from the cluster of eggs, etc." — for according to the Rabbis, sometimes a cluster of eggs is not neveilah — namely, if the white and the yolk have been completed. Therefore it must follow Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov, who holds that everything depends on whether the eggs are attached by sinews, and it is valid to state regarding the cluster of eggs that since they are attached by sinews, they have the status of neveilah itself. And study this carefully.

(שם) <b>פטור ואין לוקין על אכילתן כו'</b>. עיין בס' פ"ח שרוצה להוכיח מדברי תוס' פ' כ"ה ד' קי"ד ד"ה המבשל במי חלב דפטור היינו פטור ומותר לבשל לכתחילה שכ' המבשל במי חלב פטור הא אסורא מיהו איכא דהא כותח מנסיובי דחלבי עבדי לי' ואמרינן לעיל אסור לאכול בשר בכותח משמע דווקא התם אסור לכתחילה אבל שאר דברים דלא מצינו בפירוש דאסור מותר לבשל לכתחלה ודבריו אין להם שחר כלל אדרבה משם מוכח דפטור דהכא פטור אבל אסור דהא י"ל היכי מייתי תוס' ראיה מכותח דאסור לאכול אבישול דלמא דווקא לאכול אסור אבל לבשל אפי' לכתחלה מותר א"ו דס"ל כל מה דאסור לאכול ה"ה לבשל אסור לכתחלה א"כ ה"ה בשאר אסורים דאסרו חז"ל לאכול משום בב"ח ה"ה בישול אסור:
**** ENGLISH ****
(Ibid.) "Exempt, and one does not receive lashes for eating them, etc." See in siman 88, where he wishes to prove from the words of Tosafot, chapter 25, daf 114, s.v. HaMevashel b'Mei Chalav, that "exempt" means exempt and it is permitted to cook l'chatchila. For Tosafot wrote: "One who cooks in milk whey is exempt, but there is indeed a prohibition, for kuttach is made from milk whey, and we said above that it is forbidden to eat meat with kuttach" — implying that only in that case is it forbidden l'chatchila, but other things where we do not find explicitly that they are forbidden, it is permitted to cook them l'chatchila. But his words have no basis whatsoever. On the contrary, from there it is proven that "exempt" here means exempt but forbidden. For one could ask: how does Tosafot bring proof from kuttach, which is forbidden to eat, to the matter of cooking — perhaps it is only forbidden to eat, but to cook is permitted even l'chatchila? It must therefore be that Tosafot holds that anything that is forbidden to eat is likewise forbidden to cook l'chatchila. If so, the same applies to all other prohibitions that our Sages forbade to eat on account of basar b'chalav — cooking is likewise forbidden.

**** ENGLISH ****
(Ibid.:) "And one does not receive lashes for eating it on account of basar b'chalav." This implies that, however, on account of blood one would receive lashes, even though it is dam shevishlo (blood that was cooked). It must therefore be said that he holds to distinguish between congealed and liquid blood: if it is congealed, one is not liable for dam shevishlo; and if it is liquid, one is liable. With this, what appeared at first glance as a contradiction in his words is resolved — for above in the laws of salting he wrote and implied many times that dam shevishlo is only d'rabbanan, whereas here it implies the opposite. Rather, it is certainly as stated above. And the source he cites for the Rambam and the Mechaber to distinguish between congealed and liquid is Tosafot in tractate Zevachim.

{Gloss: What the Gaon, the author, wrote — that this is found in Tosafot of Zevachim — it seems that in Zevachim this statement of Tosafot is not found, and perhaps it is a scribal error, and it should read "in Menachot" (daf 21a, s.v. "kan"), for thus it is found there in Tosafot according to Rashi there. However, the matter requires further study, for according to Rashi there is no practical halachic difference at all in this — since according to Rashi even for congealed blood one is liable, and he holds that Rava disagrees there with Ze'iri. Moreover, Ze'iri's own statement that dam shevishlo is exempt applies only to the inner sin-offerings, but for the outer sin-offerings, and all the more so for chulin, even for congealed blood one is liable. According to Rashi's opinion there, that distinction between congealed and liquid applies only to congealing in the sun, but blood cooked in fire certainly always drains out, as is explained there.

But in truth, to settle the Rambam's words more satisfactorily, it appears better to say as the Beit Yosef wrote here: that the Rambam holds like Rashi that dam shevishlo is truly liable with lashes. And as for what the Beit Yosef wondered regarding the sugya in Menachot mentioned above — perhaps he holds that the sugya of Menachot does not follow the halacha — though he did not clarify from where the Rambam derived that it does not follow the halacha. But in truth, according to what Rashi wrote there, it is evident that Rava disagrees with that statement, and Rava is the later authority. A great proof for this is that the Rambam does not cite at all in his laws the ruling of Ze'iri that one does not transgress for dam shevishlo, and therefore he must hold that it was rejected from the halacha.

And as for what the Tur and the Mechaber copied the language of the Rambam — even though they certainly hold that dam shevishlo is d'rabbanan, as the Tur wrote at the end of siman 76 in the name of the Rashba explicitly [and so too the Mechaber, it is explained in many places that he holds it to be d'rabbanan] — and the Drisha already pointed this out here — I say there is no difficulty: since nowadays there are no lashes, they did not bother to be precise in this; and something similar is found in many places, and it is obvious. End of the gloss of the distinguished Gaon, etc., Moreinu HaRav Shlomo HaKohen, may his light shine, Moreh Tzedek of the holy community of Vilna.}

(ibid.) They further wrote that the vessel in which water is prepared for washing, etc., which is made from the ash upon the stove, etc. The Tzemach Tzedek raised the question: is it not established law that the ash of a stove impairs flavor? If so, what is the concern, since its taste has already been impaired?

One can answer: the Tosafot in the chapter Gid HaNasheh, daf 100b, wrote as follows: According to the interpretation of R' Eliezer, it is well understood why the Torah commanded to kasher all utensils, etc. — for do not the initial waters produced during kashering, etc., carry the absorbed taste and return and render things forbidden, etc. And one might say: when a utensil is not ben yomo, one koshers it, since the Torah only prohibited a pot that is ben yomo. But commentators raise the objection: how would this have helped if one were to kasher those that are not ben yomo? — for then one would have to say that the Torah also prohibited noten taam lifgam; if so, the question still returns to its original place, that the waters give taste and return and render things forbidden.

And one can answer: the Rambam wrote that the reason noten taam lifgam is permitted is because it is used not in the normal manner of deriving benefit from the forbidden substance. But regarding basar b'chalav, the Bach holds that using it not in the normal manner of deriving benefit is also subject to liability, as stated in Pesachim daf 26. If so, a new ruling emerges according to the Rambam's approach: if the taste of meat or milk was absorbed into a pot and it impairs flavor, it is permitted, since it is not considered basar b'chalav at all — for there is no taste of meat here to render the milk forbidden, since the taste of the meat impairs flavor. But this is not so if the pot was already rendered forbidden through cooking basar b'chalav — even if it is not ben yomo and its taste has also been impaired, it is nonetheless forbidden, since it has already been rendered nevelah through basar b'chalav. And even though the taste has now been impaired, even so it is forbidden, for in basar b'chalav even using it not in the normal manner of deriving benefit is also forbidden.

According to this, there is no difficulty with the Rama. For here we are dealing with the prohibition of basar b'chalav: even though it is impaired, it is nevertheless forbidden. And with this, the aforementioned words of the Tosafot are also resolved. For what it says — that when a vessel is not ben yomo one koshers it — is not because of concern for other prohibitions, regarding which it is correctly established that noten taam lifgam is permitted. Rather, what it writes — that the Torah required commanding the kashering of a vessel that is not ben yomo — is because of the concern that perhaps the gentile had been cooking basar b'chalav in it, in which case even noten taam lifgam is also forbidden.

And if so, one cannot raise the objection: but the waters themselves give taste and thus render other things forbidden! This is not a valid objection, for it is established law that regarding a prohibition of mashehu, one does not apply chatichah naaseit nevelah. For if we were to say that in all Torah prohibitions noten taam lifgam is forbidden, then the established law that taam k'ikar d'oraisa applies even to noten taam lifgam would truly be d'oraisa, and one could correctly apply chatichah naaseit nevelah to it. But now that we say that in all Torah prohibitions noten taam lifgam is permitted, therefore the ruling that basar b'chalav is forbidden even when it impairs flavor is only like a prohibition of mashehu, and regarding mashehu one does not apply chatichah naaseit nevelah. Deliberate on this carefully.

That is to say, in such a case the Sages did not extend their stringency to forbid the cheese. Reflect well on this.
