Or a spring of forty se'ah. Beit Yosef challenged what Tur wrote — it is not clear, etc., for regarding impurity too he ruled in siman 201 that we require forty se'ah; and Darkei Moshe resolved that Tur challenges according to the view of Sefer HaMitzvos, who wrote: even though a spring, etc. (end of his words), that according to him it suffices with less than forty se'ah for impurity, but he is stringent in this tevilah over that — our rabbi wondered why the difference. And it is not pleasing to me from Tur's wording, also from the law, for certainly Semak too holds that for a woman it does not suffice in a spring less than forty se'ah, but for an impure vessel it suffices with ten. And it appears to me the explanation of Tur's words is thus: this tevilah of vessels of a non-Jew we derive in the last chapter of Avodah Zarah from what is said "and it shall be pure"; and in truth there is no implication of "and it shall be pure" that it be through water — let us say through something else. Also what they said there that we derive from "only in the waters of a niddah" — there is no resolution in this, for the plain meaning of the verse refers to waters of sin-offering, as Rashi explained in the Chumash; rather we must interpret this "and it shall be pure," which is through water, since it is written regarding impure vessels "in water it shall be brought and it shall be pure" — from this we learn: in what do we find that this "and it shall be pure" regarding vessels of a non-Jew also refers to water? And after it is clarified that "and it shall be pure" refers to water, we interpret "waters of a niddah" as waters in which a niddah immerses, and it teaches us that it does not suffice with less than forty se'ah like a niddah, as stated in the Gemara. And in Tur we read "more than tevilah of impurity," and he means impure vessels; and this is what he says: since we do not know that purification is in water for vessels of a non-Jew except from tevilah of impure vessels, we do not be more stringent than them regarding a spring, except in what the Torah revealed regarding a mikveh, that it should be like a niddah:

And specifically if he moistened his hand in mikveh water, etc. The rabbi left room here to defend himself, for his honor is in its place — he did not analyze this gloss at all, although they follow Mordechai at the end of Avodah Zarah, as we will quote shortly in the name of Darkei Moshe; nevertheless one must understand Mordechai's words and explain them as we will set forth, with God's help. First we will mention the essence of this matter that Tur and Shulchan Aruch mentioned — that rinsing one's hand in water helps — and this is what we teach in the mishnah, chapter 8 of Mikvaot: one who holds a person and vessels and immerses them — they are impure; and if he rinsed his hands in pure water, R. Shimon says he should loosen his hands so water enters them. And Rashi explained: he rinsed his hand before holding — pure, because moisture on his hand connects to mikveh water and there is no chatzitzah here. And likewise Tur brought in siman 197, and likewise at the end of Tur HaAroch. This is according to what we teach in chapter 6 of Mikvaot: one draws and moistens even a hair's breadth — meaning there one wishes to purify invalid water from valid mikveh water, and by moistening them a hair's breadth all invalid water is purified; and this halachah is widespread that moisture of a fingerbreadth to moisten helps to connect to a valid mikveh, as stated in chapter 2 of Gittin (daf 16). This is the intent of the mishnah we mentioned, that one who holds a person or vessel should rinse his hands in water — meaning before holding the vessel he should moisten his hands, even in detached water, and through their connection to the mikveh the water on his hand becomes actual mikveh water through hashakah; therefore the tanna and later poskim used the language of rinsing generally, like all rinsings that are in detached water — not like Rama, who explained Tur's words specifically in mikveh water; rather detached water also helps through hashakah. And what Darkei Moshe wrote in the name of Mordechai at the end of Avodah Zarah, that specifically mikveh water is required — this is Mordechai's language there: he also taught that one should be careful to immerse a glass vessel full of water and place it in a bucket full of water and sink the bucket in a pit, not to set it on its rim lest the weight of the vessel be a chatzitzah, but tilt it on its side and roll it in the bucket in the pit; and one asked: what is different from a person's foot spread on the ground in a mikveh, where there is no chatzitzah, because we say the water precedes the place before he puts his foot on the ground, as we teach in chapter 7 of Mikvaot: they immersed a bed in it even though its legs sink in thick mud — valid, because the water precedes. One might say: only in a complete mikveh where they are attached; but when one puts glass in a bucket, even full of water, it does not help because they are detached; and one should also be careful from what we teach in chapter 8 of Mikvaot: one who holds a person and vessels, etc. — end of Mordechai. From this Rama learned to explain here that the rinsing Tur mentioned is in mikveh water. In truth this is another matter: Mordechai deals with the weight of the vessel that interrupts the mikveh when it lies, and it is not applicable to say the water under the vessel is purified by hashakah from mikveh water unless there were already valid mikveh water under the vessel; then even though the vessel's weight interrupts so one might say those waters were completely cut off from the mikveh, nevertheless they remained connected and have connection on every side to remain in their validity — unlike detached water in a vessel, which you must purify through hashakah and create purity from the outset; for this a good connection is required, and since the vessel has weight it is not called a good connection. But in truth one who immerses a vessel in his hand in a mikveh does not need mikveh water on the hand; since there is no weight of a vessel, hashakah to detached water already on his hand with mikveh water is fine; nevertheless the person immersing must not grip the vessel forcefully, for then it is like the vessel's weight we mentioned — rather moderate contact, as they said regarding a niddah immersing in several places regarding her eyes and lips. This is Mordechai's intent: he also wrote one should be careful from what the mishnah says, one who holds, etc. — he asked: does the mishnah come to inform us? Rather it teaches that one must be careful not to grip too forcefully; for although the first tanna argues with R. Shimon, who holds he must loosen his hands, nevertheless moderate contact is required even for the first tanna, not with force — this is simple. And I saw in Mishmeres HaBayis of Rashba daf 190: we rule like the first tanna that he need not loosen his hands, only that he rinsed his hands first and does not press hard — tevilah is valid. And it appears to me Tur holds that if he grips moderately in his hand it is permitted even l'chatchila, and we do not decree lest he grip with great force and mikveh water connection not help; and this is why he wrote "however if he moistened, etc.," there is no concern — he asked: why "there is no concern" — he should have said permitted or it is fine; rather it teaches there is no concern lest he grip too hard. And Rosh is even more lenient, for he does not hold the vessel's weight interrupts and nullifies the connection of detached water on his hands with mikveh water; for he wrote in Hilchot Mikvaot: because the water precedes and touched the bed's legs before touching mud, and those waters are connected to the mikveh — therefore there is no chatzitzah, as we teach in chapter 8 of Mikvaot; and for this reason one need not lift his legs during immersion because the water precedes — end of Rosh. Behold he equated this law to rinsing hands, where the rinse-water is detached; nevertheless since Mordechai distinguishes between vessel weight or not, one should be stringent; therefore one who wishes to immerse a vessel should moisten his hands even in detached water, hold the vessel with moderate contact, and immerse it. One who wishes to immerse a vessel full of detached water in a bucket should draw water from the mikveh and place it with the vessel in water; then he must turn it on its side, for this is like a person gripping a vessel forcefully and immersing it — the person holding is voluntary and can hold without great force, unlike a full heavy vessel — unless the bucket is sunk in water and he lowers the vessel into it; then we are not concerned for vessel weight, since there is already valid water inside, as we said. And if not. In the Gemara we say: Mar bar Rav Ashi said: Father pledged a silver chair from a non-Jew, immersed it, and drank from it; and we do not know whether because he holds a pledge is like a sale, or because it appears the non-Jew intends to sink it; therefore poskim ruled: if we clearly see he intends to sink it, tevilah is required; and if it were not a doubt, it implies even if certainly he does not intend to sink it, there is doubt whether tevilah is required from pledge-as-sale; only Tur wrote: if one does not know whether he intends to sink it, we are in doubt, etc. — implying if certainly he does not intend to sink it, certainly no tevilah. And Beit Yosef writes: where he does not intend to sink it, we are in doubt, etc. — such is primary; and it appears Tur teaches a leniency that even whenever it is not known for certain that he intends to sink it, no tevilah is required, and all the more so when certainly he does not intend; and that he did not teach the reverse leniency that even when he does not intend to sink one may immerse without blessing — this is only a general stringency. And Maharshal wrote: when he does not intend to sink it, a Jew is forbidden to use it because of deceiving the non-Jew; he brought proof from Maharil's laws of Pesach, who wrote it is not proper to use any non-Jewish vessel before it is secured in Jewish hands unless permitted — therefore Tur did not permit here without intent to sink. I say: if there were prohibition of deceit, certainly intent to sink would not help, as pledge-as-sale — for with a Jew's pledge these things certainly do not help; and if there is prohibition with non-Jews, it would be like a Jew — why did Rav Ashi use a non-Jew's pledge without permission from pledge-as-sale? Also Tur and other poskim did not speak of one who transgresses; perhaps Maharil explained Rav Ashi used it with permission — then the question returns: why did Tur not write where certainly he does not intend to sink, for even when unknown there is prohibition from deceit per this view. Nevertheless it appears clear to my humble opinion there is clear permission to use a non-Jew's pledge without permission — it is no worse than canceling his loan in Choshen Mishpat siman 248, which was permitted [meaning in their days, since gentiles then practiced canceling Jewish loans, and Jews measured them as they measured them]. In Bava Kama (daf 113) Rashi explained this is not actual theft, for there is no desecration of God's name, for the non-Jew does not know explicitly that he lies, etc.; and what we say in chapter HaNezikin, Rav Kahana said "peace unto you, master" — Rashi: when he gives peace to a non-Jew he does not intend to bless him but his master (God). **** ENGLISH ****
He immerses it too without a blessing. Question: Issur VeHeter general rule 58 law 94 wrote: a non-Jew who gives silver to a Jew to repair a vessel for him, and the Jew buys it from him — even before it reaches the non-Jew's hand requires tevilah — end; implying even with blessing. And Rama's reason appears: Issur VeHeter's main reason obligating tevilah here is we do not say in this case "craftsman acquires through enhancement," as written there before — only regarding enhancement we say so, not the vessel itself, for the craftsman may not keep it; therefore the craftsman's name is not upon it; and since Rama saw other poskim hold "craftsman acquires through enhancement" as Ritzba we mentioned, he ruled here immerse without blessing. But what Rama wrote afterward "or he gave part of the metals from his own, no tevilah" — on what does this apply? If relying on "Jew makes for himself," obviously even if the non-Jew gave all metals he said first no tevilah; if on the previous "craftsman makes for non-Jew," it is difficult: since we are concerned perhaps here "craftsman acquires through enhancement," it becomes the non-Jew's vessel — what help that the Jewish craftsman gives part of the silver, since most of the vessel is the non-Jew's? This resembles buying a vessel from a non-Jew with a hole and a Jew repairs with Jewish silver — does that exempt non-Jew obligation? Now we hold the non-Jew's name is upon it — a fortiori from Rama shortly: if part of the silver from which the vessel was made is the non-Jew's, tevilah is required because we follow the stabilizer who is the non-Jew, even though most is the Jew's — all the more so if most is the non-Jew's and minority the Jew's. And further, even a stabilizer name is not here, for he makes a new vessel and is already nullified in the non-Jew's majority, as we mentioned — whence do we uproot tevilah obligation entirely? And even if you explain the non-Jew gives only part, it is still difficult: why above is tevilah required without blessing and here tevilah obligation is uprooted entirely? Therefore it appears there is a scribal error here, and this "or he gave part of the silver" belongs earlier; but if, etc., and it teaches two matters: first, if the non-Jew gave all metals, nevertheless he does not bless; and if he gave only part from his own, nevertheless tevilah is required. And so it appears primary to include this law in "requires tevilah without blessing," and there is no obstacle:
