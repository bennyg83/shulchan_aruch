<b>אם המוכר עשיר. </b>פי' שמוכר לו דבר שעתיד להתייקר כשיעור שקונה עכשיו זה ממנו נמצא לגבי המוכר שהוא עשיר אין לו הנאה שהרי יכול להניח המקח עד שיגיע שעת היוקר אפ"ה אסור דאזלינן בתר לוקח דאי הוה ליה זוזי היה יכול עכשיו לקנות בזול וכיון דלית ליה זוזי שקיל כיוקרא דלקמיה ונראה פשוט דאע"פ שגם אם הלוקח רוצה להניחו עד שעת היוקר דאסור דעכ"פ בשעה שהוא נוטלו ממנו נוטלו באיסור ובסי' קס"ט תחילת סעיף י"ח הבאתי דין זה ע"ש מה שלמדתי ממנו:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>If the seller is wealthy. </b>This means that he sells him an item that is expected to rise in price, at the price for which the buyer now acquires it from him. With respect to the seller, who is wealthy, there is no benefit, for he could leave the merchandise until the time of the higher price. Nevertheless, it is forbidden, for we follow the buyer. Had he possessed money, he could have bought it cheaply now, and since he lacks money, he takes it at the future higher price. It appears obvious that even if the buyer also wishes to leave it until the time of the higher price, it is forbidden, for in any event, at the time he takes it from him he takes it through a forbidden arrangement. In siman 169, at the beginning of seif 18, I cited this law; see there what I derived from it.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Unminted money. </b>This means, as the Beit Yosef wrote in the name of the Ramban, money without an image stamped upon it. It is regarded like produce, and when he possesses it, the transaction is permitted. <i data-commentator="Nekudot HaKesef" data-label="♯" data-order="1"></i>This is unlike Tosafos, who maintain that even stamped money is permitted, provided it is of another type. <br><b>And what he wrote </b>in the gloss, 'It appears to me from the words of the Rosh and Tosafos, etc.' The Levush, my father-in-law of blessed memory, and the Derishah challenged this, for in siman 175, when one fixes a price for produce, he may fix only up to the quantity he possesses. <i data-commentator="Nekudot HaKesef" data-label="♯" data-order="2"></i>I am greatly astonished by what the Rama wrote, 'It appears to me from Tosafos.' Tosafos explain the Gemara's statement that when he possesses it, it is regarded as though he says, 'Lend me until my son arrives.' Just as a se'ah for a se'ah loan is permitted when he says, 'Until my son arrives,' because se'ah for se'ah is only rabbinically forbidden, so too, by way of purchase and sale, even a se'ah for two se'ah is permitted when he says, 'Until my son arrives.' End quote. From this the Rama learned, here and in Darkei Moshe, that since they compare this case to a se'ah for a se'ah loan, and there he need not possess the entire se'ah, the same should apply here. I say that there is no proof. The two cases are clearly not entirely comparable, for possession does not help when one wishes to borrow a se'ah for two se'ah. The allowance was stated only for a se'ah for a se'ah loan, where the concern is that the price may rise; possession removes that concern. An explicit loan of a se'ah for two se'ah is fixed interest, and possession does not help. The Rosh wrote this explicitly in the chapter HaZahav: 'We have not found that a se'ah for two se'ah is permitted even when he possesses it.' End quote. Here, however, possession helps even if he gives him more than one dinar's worth for a dinar, because it is by way of purchase and sale. Tosafos mean only that just as a se'ah for a se'ah loan has an allowance when he possesses some of it, according to the law of that case, so too this purchase and sale has an allowance when he possesses the subject matter, according to the law of this case. Here he must possess everything he sells, as I wrote at the beginning of siman 163 in the name of the Nimukei Yosef. The comparison is only that in both cases possession creates an allowance, but each follows its own law. This appears clear. I have also seen fit to mention a practice whereby a borrower writes that he sells the lender a certain quantity of silver, though it is known that he does not possess that quantity, and the lender relies upon his admission that he does. This appears to me to involve a Torah prohibition. How can a false admission uproot a Torah prohibition? An admission can obligate a person monetarily for something he did not owe, but who can waive Hashem's portion? This is similar to what I wrote in siman 169, seif 11. There is further proof from the Rama there, who rules that when a person is not established as possessing collateral belonging to a non-Jew, his false admission does not help. The same applies here.

<b>אם ישנם ברשות המוכר. </b>פירש בית יוסף בשם ריב"ש הטעם כיון דמן התורה מעות קונות ואם חוזר בו מקבל מי שפרע הוה לענין רבית כאילו קנאם קנין גמור מעתה כיון דאין איסור במכירת פירות אלא מדרבנן אבל כשאין לו אנו חוששין שמא יתייקר ומרויח בהקדמת המעות וגם המוכר יצטרך לקנותם ביוקר עכ"ל:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>If they are in the seller's possession. </b>The Beit Yosef explains in the name of the Rivash that since, according to the Torah, money effects acquisition, and if he retracts he is subject to the curse of 'He Who exacted punishment,' with respect to interest it is regarded as though the buyer fully acquired them immediately. The prohibition against selling produce in this manner is only rabbinic. When the seller does not possess the produce, however, we are concerned that the price may rise and the buyer profits because he advanced the money, while the seller will also need to purchase it at the higher price. End quote.

<b>אבל אם מפרש כו'. </b>בטור כתוב כאן י"א אפילו אם מפרש דמותר ביש לו ונראה טעמא דאע"פ דבריש סי' זה אסור במפרש לכ"ע שאני התם שהלוקח מבקש זמן להמתין לו המעות ובזה שייך המתנה מה שאין כן כאן שהמקח יש ביד מוכר מזומן אלא שממתין עד שיבא בנו אין זה קרוי המתנה והטור חולק דאף זה קרוי המתנה ומ"ש רמ"א כאן ואם לאחר זמן ביו"ד ר"ל אחר שיבא בני ואפ"ה אסור:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>But if he specifies, etc. </b>The Tur cites an opinion that even if he specifies the lower cash price, it is permitted when the seller possesses the item. The reason appears to be that although at the beginning of this siman all agree that specification is forbidden, there the buyer seeks additional time to pay the money, and therefore delayed payment applies. Here, however, the merchandise is ready in the seller's possession, and he merely waits until his son arrives; this is not called delayed payment. The Tur disagrees and holds that even this is called delayed payment. What the Rama writes here, 'and if it is after time,' means after the son arrives, and even so it is forbidden.

<b>Even if he does not possess it, it is permitted. </b>There are astonishing matters here. Rav Nachman taught that if one gives money to a wax seller when 4 cakes sell for a zuz, and the seller says, 'I will give you 5,' it is permitted if he possesses them and forbidden if he does not. The Gemara asks that the prohibition when he does not possess them is obvious, and answers that even credit owed to him by others is insufficient, as the Tur writes here. Tosafos and the Rosh ask from Rav Nachman's own ruling that a deferred-price sale is permitted, meaning that one may sell for more than the present value because payment is delayed, provided he does not expressly state a lower cash price. Why then should it not likewise be permitted to sell cheaply because money is advanced, when he does not expressly state the discount? They answer that since 4 cakes presently sell for a zuz, selling 5 is equivalent to expressly stating a lower cash price. Rav Nachman's deferred-price allowance applies only to an item without a fixed market price or known appraisal. End quote. The Beit Yosef understood from this that when an item's appraisal is unknown, one may advance money to buy it cheaply even if the seller does not possess it, like Rav Nachman's deferred-price sale. Darkei Moshe copied this, and from it arose the gloss permitting the case whenever the discount is not expressly stated. I was astonished for a long time at these two great authorities. How could one permit fixing a future delivery for any item when the seller does not possess it and no market price has been established? How is this different from the Mishnah, 'One may not fix a price for produce until the market price has been established'? Even for an item without a known appraisal, such as a cow or cloak, the price may rise. If he received 1 gold coin to supply a cow at a later date, and then must spend 2 gold coins to supply it, this is visible interest, for our eyes see that he pays 2 for the 1 he received. Rav Nachman's deferred-price sale is entirely different. There the buyer does not visibly pay more money than he received, and since the cow has no published appraisal, it may have been worth the deferred price. Here the seller gives a cow cheaply and receives less than he must later spend to supply it. This is visible interest. All the more so is it astonishing that the Beit Yosef and Rama expressly permit advancing money in order to buy cheaply. Who permitted this? It is full interest, for he receives what he did not give. <i data-commentator="Nekudot HaKesef" data-label="♯" data-order="3"></i>The proper explanation, with Hashem's help, is that there are two categories. The first is visible interest, where all see that the borrower gives more than he received. This is the law that one may not fix a price for produce before the market price is established, and the same applies to every item that may rise in price. The second is interest proven by the circumstances. We do not see a greater amount being paid, but the nature of the transaction proves that the difference was given because money was advanced or payment delayed, such as selling below the known market price or buying above it. Rav Nachman's deferred-price sale concerns only this second category. Since the item has neither a fixed market price nor a known appraisal, the excess is not publicly evident, and the Sages did not prohibit it. Rav Nachman's wax case also concerns circumstantial interest, in a setting where the concern of a later price rise has already been removed, such as when the delivery date arrived and the market then fell to 5 cakes per zuz. Even so, if at the time of the agreement the market was 4 and he promised 5, the transaction was evidently made because of the advance payment and is forbidden when he did not possess the wax. Tosafos therefore compare Rav Nachman's two rulings and distinguish between an item with a known price or appraisal, where the circumstantial interest is evident, and a cow, cloak, or similar item without a known appraisal, where it is not. All this distinction applies only where there is no concern that the item may rise in price. Whenever such a concern exists, the Mishnah's law applies and it is forbidden when the seller does not possess the item. This is the clear meaning of Tosafos and the Rosh. No permission may be derived from them for a forward purchase before the market price is established when the seller lacks the goods.

It appears to me that the Rambam wrote these words to explain Rav Nachman's deferred-price sale, as the Maggid Mishneh states. The Rambam permits merchandise to be given for sale at a price above its present value, meaning at the value it is expected to have later. To distinguish this from Rav Pappa's forbidden deferred-price sale, the Rambam makes a different distinction. Rav Pappa's arrangement is forbidden when a complete and irrevocable sale is made immediately. Here, however, the recipient may return the merchandise whenever he wishes. Until he actually sells it, it remains like a deposit or loaned object in his hands, and when he sells it on his own initiative he then accepts upon himself to pay the amount the owner could have received later. This is permitted, for the owner performs no forbidden act; the recipient acts on his own with the owner's property in his possession. The Beit Yosef expresses this principle below, writing that he took the goods on his own initiative and used them. In a sale arrangement of this kind, all agree that it is permitted. Responsibility must therefore remain with the owner until the recipient sells the merchandise, for otherwise it would become an immediate sale. The Rambam gives two forms. In the first, the owner bears all responsibility until it is sold. In the second, the recipient accepts responsibility immediately, yet it is still not treated as a sale because the owner promises that any amount above the fixed sum will belong to the recipient. The recipient waits until the higher-price season and may receive even more; in exchange for that potential gain he accepts responsibility. Nevertheless, he may return the merchandise whenever he wishes. Thus, there is no sale from the owner to him; rather, he independently sells another person's property that is in his possession and undertakes to pay the future higher value. This is permitted. The two forms of the seif are thereby explained. In the first form, although the text states, 'If he cannot sell it, he returns it,' as part of the rationale rather than as an explicit condition, it is obvious that because the owner bears responsibility and no sale has yet occurred, the recipient may return it whenever he wishes. This appears correct to me.

According to this, if he pays them compensation for their effort on the return journey, it is permitted. This appears obvious to me.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And some permit produce even if he accepts responsibility. </b>So write the commentators in the name of Rabbeinu Tam and the Ramban. The reason is explained in the Nimukei Yosef and Rivash, siman 306. Although there would seem to be grounds to prohibit, since once he receives the money he may spend it, and therefore he accepts responsibility for the return journey with the produce and supplies it at the lower price, it is nevertheless permitted. The benefit is not compensation for a loan. Although he used the money during the outward journey, he is like a moneychanger with whom money was deposited, who repays it at the destination. They make this arrangement because of the two benefits stated above, that the marketplace is opened to them and prices are lowered for them. They are merely the sender's agents, except that a guardian may accept the liability of a borrower; therefore they accept responsibility for the merchandise on the return journey. End quote. One should not challenge this from seif 15, where even compensation for effort does not permit the arrangement unless responsibility rests upon the seller. Although one might also say there that the marketplace is opened to him, as the Tur writes that he says, 'When I sell it, I will repay you immediately,' meaning that he wishes to establish his credit, that case concerns ordinary merchandise, where there is no genuine opening of the marketplace. Furthermore, these two reasons apply specifically to one who purchases produce, because the seller benefits when the buyer purchases merchandise for him. When others see money in the buyer's hands, many sellers approach him, and that benefit compensates him for accepting responsibility. In the earlier case, however, the seller receives no comparable benefit from potential buyers. Anyone who comes to buy has no concern, for he can purchase in many places and need not ingratiate himself with anyone. The Gemara at the beginning of Eizehu Neshech says that people's money itself serves as a broker, meaning that no broker is needed because the donkey drivers bring the grain directly to the owner of the money. Thus, the seller receives no benefit of this kind, and the buyer must therefore accept responsibility.
