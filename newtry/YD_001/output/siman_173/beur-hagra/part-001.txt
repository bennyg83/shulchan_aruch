**** ENGLISH ****
<b>In what case, etc.</b> See Tosafot, 63b, s.v. 'for he said to him,' etc., and 65b, s.v. 'and the halachah,' etc. See also the Yerushalmi cited by Nimukei Yosef. His explanation of the Yerushalmi does not appear correct to me; see the Yerushalmi there: 'He went out, established it in a baraita, and taught,' etc. See also Shitah Mekubetzet in the name of Ramban. <br><b>(Supplement) In what case, etc.</b> This is the deferred-price arrangement of Rav Nahman. Rif omitted it, and Milchamot likewise demonstrated that the halachah does not follow Rav Nahman, discussing the matter at length. He further wrote there that Rav Nahman's arrangement, according to the Geonim, is like that of Rav Pappa: he sells to him in Tishrei at the price current in Nisan and waits until Nisan. The Gemara challenged this, etc., and answered, etc. There, in the Mishnah, he fixed the price for payment at the threshing season at twelve maneh. Here, however, the price is merely that which will prevail in Nisan and is not fixed, for the price may not rise. This is why it is called tarsha, as the Arukh writes that tarash in Arabic means deaf, comparable to the expression in Tanhuma at the end of Behukotai, 'your deaf dance, strike upon it,' etc.; it denotes something not fixed. The Yerushalmi explicitly rules that Rav Nahman's arrangement is prohibited. It states there: Rabbi Hiyya the Elder had flax. Wine merchants came to buy it. He said to them, 'I do not intend to sell it now, but only at Purim.' They said to him, 'Sell it to us at the price for which you expect to sell it at Purim.' He came and asked Rabbi, who told him that it was prohibited. His words are persuasive. (End of supplement.)

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>An item, etc.</b> As Baal HaTerumot writes in the name of Ramban, the Gemara's challenge there from the Mishnah, 'but if payment is at the threshing season,' etc., applies even though he raises the price considerably, because land is expensive in Nisan, as stated in the first chapter of Bava Kamma, 7a. <br><b>(Supplement) An item, etc.</b> This is difficult in my eyes. Since its assessed value is known, it is as though the price were fixed. Even if in most cases it is customary, etc., it is nevertheless near to profit and remote from loss, etc. Hagahot Mordekhai, however, writes differently: 'The ancient custom of delivering cloaks after a long period for a set amount at the high market price is because their price depends upon fortune. If a nobleman comes to the city and desires them, their price will rise beyond their value. Had the nobleman come immediately, their price would have doubled, and there is no payment for waiting,' etc. One must say that in most years noblemen would come to the city, for otherwise it would appear as payment for waiting. It is clear that there, if the nobleman came, the price would double beyond what the buyer had paid. This is what he means by saying that their price depends upon fortune. It is therefore also near to profit, as with all merchandise whose value depends upon fortune. Nevertheless, he requires that this occur in most cases, etc., because although it is legally permitted, it would otherwise appear, etc. This requires study. (End of supplement.)

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And some permit, etc.</b> Rabbeinu Hananel explains it this way, as cited by Nimukei Yosef. This resolves Tosafot's question on 73a, s.v. 'what is the reason': 'It could not have said,' etc. Since here the goods are under the buyer's responsibility, payment for the agent's labor alone does not suffice, unlike Tosafot's understanding of Rabbeinu Hananel, according to which the goods remain under the seller's responsibility. They were therefore forced into a strained interpretation of his words. <br><b>(Supplement) And some permit, etc.</b> This is also indicated by the Yerushalmi, whose concluding clause speaks even where the merchant bears responsibility. It states there: If one transports a package from place to place and another says to him, 'Give it to me, and I will pay you according to the price at which you sell it in such-and-such place,' then if responsibility remains with the giver it is prohibited, but for the buyer it is permitted. 'But donkey drivers who receive produce from householders may provide them with produce at the expensive location according to the price at the inexpensive location.' The word 'but' implies that here it is permitted even while the donkey drivers bear responsibility. The Yerushalmi asks: What is the reason? Rav Huna says: He becomes his agent. They challenged Rav Huna: Is an agent who suffers unavoidable loss liable for the loss? This is rhetorical, for an agent is exempt from unavoidable loss, whereas here he is liable. It answers: Is it not possible that at times an unpaid custodian stipulates to have the status of a borrower? Thus, responsibility rests upon the merchant. Tosafot's question there, s.v. 'the donkey drivers,' nevertheless remains difficult. Therefore Rabbeinu Hananel explains that the householder pays him for his labor and sustenance. Although the Yerushalmi implies that no payment is required at all, because he is the householder's agent, as noted below in siman 177, seif 1, in the gloss, the Yerushalmi does not accept the Babylonian Gemara's rationale that 'they are pleased,' etc., and instead gives other reasons there. According to our Gemara, which says that they are pleased, etc., and thus that the donkey drivers receive a benefit, the Yerushalmi's rationale no longer applies. Nevertheless, we learn from the Yerushalmi that responsibility rests upon the agent, from its use of 'but,' etc., and from its statement, 'he becomes,' etc. Even though he receives payment for his labor, he must still accept responsibility, as in the package case. This is why the Gemara asks, 'Nevertheless,' etc. According to Tosafot's explanation, however, this is simply the law of the package case. What then is novel here, and why does it say 'but'? Furthermore, why does the Gemara ask, 'What is the reason?' Payment was not mentioned there. See Tosafot, s.v. 'what is the reason,' etc. Their explanation is strained, for according to it the main novelty, that no payment is required, is absent from the text and is mentioned neither in the first clause nor in the second. According to Rabbeinu Hananel, however, everything is clear. (End of supplement.)
