The verse stating that the vow of a widow or divorcee shall stand refers to a vow made while she is widowed, and Rabbi Yishmael's baraita addresses that case. This is also evident from Rava's argument there concerning stoning and terumah. See there carefully.

Some explain that a father or husband may annul before a vow takes effect only when its condition itself involves personal affliction or a matter between husband and wife, such as, 'I will derive no benefit from my father or your father if I act according to you,' or, 'I am forbidden to all Jews if I have relations with you.' Ran similarly proves that advance annulment applies only where she cannot avoid the affliction or marital consequence even by observing the condition, or where the condition is something she is likely to violate and difficult to avoid. Where the condition is unrelated, can be kept, and involves neither affliction nor marital matters, he cannot annul beforehand. Although Rabbeinu Yonah may not distinguish a condition she is likely to violate, Ran's leniency may be followed, especially since Rambam and other authorities appear to permit advance annulment in all cases.

There is no contradiction. The Gemara asks whether a husband uproots the vow or merely cuts it off, specifically regarding another who attached himself to it, and concludes that he cuts it off. Regarding a sage, the Mishnah and Gemara plainly establish that he uproots it. Rambam himself rules both laws. Kesef Mishneh explains that when Rambam says a husband uproots the vow, he means that the vow took effect and the husband now removes and nullifies it, unlike merely declaring that he does not want her vow or that there is no vow. The Gemara's question is whether the annulment makes it as though the vow never existed, or whether it removes it only from now on after it took effect. The latter is called cutting it off. Thus, the statement here that a husband does not uproot like a sage means that although he removes it, he does not erase it retroactively as a sage does.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>'Konam, what I produce for you,' etc.</b> Tosafot in Ketubot 59a write that even if the text reads 'what I do not produce,' it is not an intangible matter, citing the Mishnah, 'Konam, I will not place straw before your cattle,' which a husband cannot annul. This requires study. Nedarim teaches that 'Konam, my mouth speaking with you, my hands working with you, my feet walking with you' is intangible unless interpreted as, 'My mouth is forbidden for its speech, my hands for their work, and my feet for their walking.' Rosh and Ran explain that the words are treated as such an expression, and Tur and Shulchan Aruch rule accordingly in siman 213. That interpretation works where she mentions her mouth or hands, because the vow can grammatically refer to the body part and unspecified vows are interpreted stringently. But when she says only, 'Konam, what I produce for you,' she did not mention her hands. Indeed, the Gemara in Ketubot later resolves the case by saying that she declared her hands consecrated to their work, implying that before this resolution we did not treat her words as though she had said her hands were forbidden. Even if the wording is 'Konam, what I produce for you,' without 'I will not,' the same difficulty remains. Tur and Shulchan Aruch also write that if one says, 'My speech, my work, or my walking is forbidden to you,' the vow is ineffective because these are intangible. The case of placing straw presents no difficulty, because an intangible vow still takes effect rabbinically, as explained in the second chapter of Nedarim and above in siman 213. It appears that the present case likewise concerns rabbinic effect. According to the Gemara's conclusion that she said her hands are consecrated to their work, as Rama states, it takes effect by Torah law. This appears correct, though Tosafot at the end of the first chapter of Nedarim requires study.
