<b>והגיע הגט לידה. </b>ב"י פירש דקמשמע לן כיון שהגיע גט לידה אף על פי שעדיין אינה מגורשת כגון שנתנו לה על תנאי או לאחר זמן אינו יכול להפר עד כאן לשונו וקשה דדבר זה כתוב בהדיא בסעיף שאחר זה ונראה לי דקאי אעושה שליח להולכה שמפורש באבן עזר סימן ק"מ שיכול לחזור כל זמן שלא הגיע הגט לידה ואז יכול להפר דעדיין אשתו היא כיון שיכול לבטלו:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And the get reached her hand. </b>The Beit Yosef explains that this teaches that once the get reaches her hand, even if she is not yet divorced, such as when it was given conditionally or to take effect later, he can no longer annul her vows. This is difficult, for the next seif states this explicitly. It appears to me that this refers to one who appointed an agent to deliver the get. Even HaEzer, siman 140, explains that he may retract so long as the get has not reached her hand. During that time he may annul her vows, for she is still his wife since he can revoke the agency.

<b>חייב במזונותיה. </b>בטור כתוב כגון בוגרת ששהתה י"ב חודש ונתקדשה או אלמנה ונתקדשה ושהו אחר הקידושין שלשים יום כו' לפי שהבוגרת אף על פי ששהתה כבר י"ב חודש להכין תכשיטיה מ"מ צריך עוד שלשים יום אחר הקדושין כמבואר באבן עזר סי' נ"ג אלא ששם כתוב מיום התביעה שתבעה להנשא וכאן ג"כ הכוונה כך היא שאחר הקידושין צריכה שלשים מן התביעה ולהכי לא כתב כאן מיום הקידושין אלא אחר הקידושין וסמך על מה שכתב בגוף הדין בא"ע ומ"ש ולא האב מפר לבדו על כרחך לא קאי אבוגרת דהא בבוגרת אין לאביה שום זכות בה אלא קאי אקטנה ונערה שנתארסה ולא הכניס בוגרת אלא לענין שאין הבעל יכול להפר לה:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>He is obligated to support her. </b>The Tur gives the example of a bogeres who waited twelve months and was then betrothed, or a widow who was betrothed and thirty days passed after the kiddushin. Although the bogeres already waited twelve months to prepare her ornaments, she still requires thirty days after kiddushin, as explained in Even HaEzer, siman 53. There it says thirty days from the date she demanded that the marriage take place, and that is also the intent here. Therefore, the Tur did not write “from the day of kiddushin,” but “after the kiddushin,” relying upon the fuller law in Even HaEzer. The statement that the father cannot annul alone cannot refer to a bogeres, for her father has no authority over her. It refers to a minor or na’arah who became betrothed. The bogeres was mentioned only to teach that the husband cannot annul her vows.

<b>קודם שנתארסה. </b>דהכי משמע לשון הפסוק ואם היו תהיה לאיש ונדריה עליה דארוס מיפר בקודמין משום שותפות דאב אבל ניסת אין הבעל מפר נדרים שהיו עליה דאין הבעל מפר בקודמין דכתיב ואם בית אישה נדרה כ"כ הרא"ש ריש פ' נערה:

<b>שניהם ביום אחד. </b>כ"כ הטור בשם רמב"ם וטעמו מדכתיב גבי ארוסה ושמע אישה ביום שמעו והאי ביום שמעו אין לו פירוש דפשיטא שישמע באותו יום שישמע אלא ודאי דקאי אדלעיל מיניה ושמע אביה וגו' על זה אמר שגם הבעל ישמע ביום שמעו דאב דוקא והי"א הוא הרמב"ן סבירא ליה דביום שמעו קאי אדבתריה דכתיב ביום שמעו והחריש לה וגומר דלענין החרישה תלוי בכל היום אבל לענין הקמה תיכף סגי דאם מקיימו לא מועיל תו הפרה דאב כלל וכמ"ש בסעיף ו' כן נראה לי טעמו ולענין הלכה כתב הטור על דברי הרמב"ן והכי מסתבר אבל מדברי הש"ע משמע להחמיר כרמב"ם דספק דאורייתא הוא:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Both on the same day. </b>The Tur writes this in the Rambam’s name. The reason is that concerning a betrothed woman the verse says, “Her husband heard on the day he heard.” The phrase “on the day he heard” cannot merely mean the day on which he happened to hear, for that is obvious. Rather, it refers back to the preceding words, “Her father heard,” and teaches that the husband must hear on the same day as the father. The alternative opinion is the Ramban’s. He holds that “on the day he heard” refers forward to, “and he was silent to her,” meaning that silence depends upon the entire day. Confirmation, however, takes effect immediately, and once one party confirms the vow the other’s annulment can no longer help, as stated in seif 6. This appears to be his reasoning. The Tur writes that the Ramban’s view is more reasonable, but the Shulchan Aruch appears to rule stringently like the Rambam, because this is a Torah doubt.

<b>ונשאל על ההקמה. </b>אף על גב דבסעיף כ"ג מבואר דמועיל שאלה על ההקמה היינו בנשואה שהכל תלוי בבעל לחוד אבל כאן בארוסה שצריך שותפות האב צריך שלא יהא מפסיק שום הקמה ביניהם שכיון שיש שעה אחת שלא תועיל הפרת השני דהיינו אחר ההקמה של הראשון שוב לא תועיל לעולם אף על פי שיהיה נשאל על ההקמה והב"י בשם הרשב"א הקשה מ"ש ממקדש אשה ע"מ שאין עליה נדרים ונמצאו עליה נדרים והלכה לחכם והתיר לה דמקודשת דנעקר הנדר מעיקרא ה"נ נעקרה ההקמה וכאלו לא היתה דמי ודחק לתרץ שמ"ש וקיים א' מהם קאי על האב דוקא והוא תירץ שאינו מספיק וכל הפוסקים לא חילקו בזה וקושיתו תירץ הר"ן דף ס"ז דשאני כאן דכשהפר הראשון נשאר חלק ב' קלוש ולא מהני ביה לאצטרופיה להפרה הקודמת כיון דבטל שעה אחת:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And he sought release from the confirmation. </b>Although seif 23 explains that release from confirmation is effective, that concerns a married woman, whose vow depends solely upon her husband. Here, with a betrothed woman, annulment requires the joint action of father and betrothed man, and no confirmation may interrupt between their annulments. Once there was even one moment when the second annulment could not be effective, namely after the first party confirmed the vow, it can never again become effective, even if that confirmation is later released. The Beit Yosef cites the Rashba’s difficulty from a man who betroths a woman on condition that she has no vows. If vows are found and she goes to a sage who releases them, she is betrothed because the vows are uprooted retroactively. Here too, the confirmation should be uprooted as though it never existed. The Beit Yosef was forced to explain that the statement, “one of them confirmed,” refers specifically to the father, but he himself writes that this is insufficient, and the poskim made no such distinction. The Ran on 67a resolves the question. When the first party annulled, the remaining half became weakened. Once that remaining half was ineffective even for one moment, it can no longer join with the earlier annulment.

<b>עד שתכנס לחופה. </b>זהו דעת הטור אלא שהב"י תמה עליו וכתב שהמסקנא בפוסקים אינו כן אלא מסירה לבעל הוה במקום חופה להפרת נדרים וכדעה ראשונה דהכא ונ"ל שדברי הטור הם ע"פ דברי הרא"ש ז"ל שכ' בפ' נערה שנתפתתה דקי"ל כשמואל דאמר לירושתה מהני המסירה אבל לא לתרומה והטעם דאמרינן דבזה מוחל האב על הירושה כמו שפי' רש"י בגמ' שם דף מ"ח משמע דהפרת נדרים שהוא איסור דומה לתרומה ולא נכנסה לרשותו עד החופה ואע"ג שהתוס' כתבו דלאו דוקא ירושה דה"ה לכל שאר דברים כמו שהביא ב"י מ"מ כיון שהרא"ש כתב לחלק בין ירושה לתרומה לחוד משמע דדוקא ירושה קאמר וכפי' רש"י כן נ"ל לדעת הטור ולפי דעת זאת צ"ל דאפילו הנדרים שנדרה בין מסירה לחופה ג"כ לא יוכל להפר כיון שמרשות האב יצאה בעת ההיא ולרשות בעל לא נכנסה ממילא אם ירצה להפר אחר החופה הוה מיפר בקודמין ואין הבעל מיפר בקודמין אלא דקשה ממה דאיתא בפרק בתרא דנדרים דף פ"ט זה הכלל כל שיצאה מרשותו שעה אחת אין יכול להפר ומפרש בגמרא דאתא לאתויי דאם מסר האב לשלוחי הבעל שאין הבעל מפר בקודמין ופירש הרא"ש דהיינו אם נדרה וגירשה והחזירה שאין הבעל מפר בקודמין עכ"ל משמע מטעם דגירשה בינתיים אין יכול להפר אפי' אחר שהחזירה ונעשה בעלה אבל בלא גרושין בינתיים מפר מה שנדרה אחר המסירה לשלוחיו וע"כ נראה לדעת הטור דסבירא ליה מצד חומרא בעלמא אינו מפר אחר המסירה לשלוחיו עד שתכנס לחופה אבל מ"מ ודאי כאשתו היא מאותה שעה שאחר שתכנס לחופה מפר ביום שמעו אפי' מה שנדרה בין המסירה לכניסה לחופה ואין זה נקרא מפר בקודמין כיון שאף אותה שעה הקדומה היתה כאשתו אלא שלכתחלה לא יפיר לה אז כן נ"ל להכריע מאחר שבאמת כל הפוסקים סבירא להו דבמסירה לשלוחיו מהני להפרת נדריה כמ"ש ב"י וכ"כ בפסקי הרא"ש שחיבר הטור עצמו בפרק נערה שנתפתתה דלכל מילי מהני מסירה לשלוחיו חוץ מתרומה:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Until she enters the chuppah. </b>This is the Tur’s opinion. The Beit Yosef wondered at it and wrote that the conclusion of the poskim is otherwise, namely, that the father’s delivery of her to the husband’s agents is equivalent to chuppah for annulment of vows, as in the first opinion here. It appears to me that the Tur follows the Rosh in the chapter “A maiden who was seduced,” who rules like Shmuel that delivery to the husband’s agents is effective for inheritance but not for eating terumah. The reason is that the father thereby waives his inheritance rights, as Rashi explains on Kesubos 48a. This implies that annulment of vows, which is a matter of prohibition, resembles terumah, and she does not enter the husband’s authority until chuppah. Although Tosafos wrote that inheritance is not exclusive and that delivery is effective for all other matters, as the Beit Yosef cites, the Rosh distinguishes only inheritance from terumah, implying that he meant inheritance specifically, like Rashi. According to this view, even vows she made between delivery and chuppah could not be annulled then, because she had left the father’s authority but had not yet entered the husband’s. If he annulled after chuppah, it would seemingly be an annulment of earlier vows, which a husband cannot do. This is difficult from Nedarim 89a: “This is the rule: whenever she left his authority for even one moment, he cannot annul.” The Gemara says this includes a father who delivered her to the husband’s agents, so that the husband cannot annul earlier vows. The Rosh explains that if she vowed, was divorced, and was taken back, the husband cannot annul earlier vows. This implies that the intervening divorce is the reason. Without divorce, the husband may annul vows made after the father delivered her to his agents. Therefore, according to the Tur, his inability to annul between delivery and chuppah is merely a stringency. She is nevertheless considered his wife from the moment of delivery. After chuppah, on the day he hears, he may annul even vows made between delivery and chuppah. This is not called annulment of earlier vows, because even during that earlier period she was regarded as his wife, though he should not initially annul then. This appears the proper resolution, especially since all the poskim hold that delivery to the husband’s agents is effective for annulment, as the Beit Yosef writes. The Rosh’s rulings, which the Tur himself compiled, likewise state that delivery is effective for all matters except terumah.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>According to the Rambam, if the betrothed man heard, etc. </b>These words are astonishing in my opinion. It is an explicit Talmudic rule that the authority returns to the father. The Rambam himself writes that if both the betrothed man and father heard, and the betrothed man then died, the father may annul because the husband’s authority has returned to him. One cannot say this applies only if the father had already heard, but if he first heard after the husband’s death he cannot annul. Nedarim 70a derives the father’s renewed authority from the verse, “And if she shall be,” comparing the period before a second betrothal to the period before the first. Just as before the first betrothal the father annuls alone, so after the betrothed man’s death, before a second betrothal, the father annuls alone. This includes even a father who first heard after the death. The Tur states explicitly that not only may the father annul a vow made after the betrothed man’s death, but even one made during his lifetime, implying even if the father first heard afterward. The Beit Yosef’s attribution to the Rambam arose from the text he copied. Had he copied the Rambam’s exact words, he would not have written this. The Rambam says: If the betrothed man heard and annulled, then died, and afterward the father heard, or if the father heard and annulled and the husband died before hearing, the father cannot annul those vows alone once they were seen by the first betrothed man. Rather, if she became betrothed again that same day, he annuls together with the new betrothed man, as explained. The Beit Yosef understood that without a later betrothed man the father has no remedy. In my opinion, the Rambam means only that where there is a later betrothed man on that same day, he stands in place of the first, and the father cannot annul without joining him, just as he could not annul without the first. If there is no second betrothed man, the father certainly annuls alone, for the first husband’s authority returned to him, as the Rambam already ruled. There is therefore no dispute between the Rambam and Tur. So it appears correct to me.

<b>She does not return to the father’s authority. </b>The Tur’s text reads, “except regarding vows she will make after the husband’s death,” which is extremely difficult. If she actually entered chuppah, it is obvious that she left the father’s authority entirely, as the Mishnah states that a widow from marriage must uphold her vows. Even if, as the Beit Yosef first attempted, the Tur refers only to the second case, where the father delivered her to the husband’s agents, following the Tur’s view that this is not fully equivalent to chuppah, the difficulty remains. The Tur first mentions both actual chuppah and delivery, so how could his conclusion refer only to one? Moreover, Kesubos 49a expounds, “The vow of a widow or divorced woman, everything she prohibited upon herself shall stand.” Why is this verse necessary, since she is outside both the father’s and husband’s authority? It refers to a father who delivered her to the husband’s agents and she became widowed or divorced on the road. Is she then called in her father’s house or her husband’s house? The verse teaches that once she left the father’s authority for one moment, he can never annul. Rashi explains that the Torah previously described a vow in the father’s house and in the husband’s house. The verse teaches that a woman delivered to the husband’s agents is no longer called in her father’s house but in her husband’s house. The husband’s authority does not return to the father at the husband’s death. Tosafos writes that according to Rashi the verse seems needed merely to determine whose authority she is under on the road, but this is already learned elsewhere. Rather, the verse is needed to teach that she does not return to her father after widowhood or divorce. Thus, even after death or divorce she never returns to the father’s authority. Tosafos plainly refers even to vows she makes after the death or divorce, for they use the language “return to the father.” Rashi’s phrase, “the husband’s authority does not return to the father,” concerns vows made during the husband’s lifetime, but Tosafos teaches the additional point concerning later vows. Rashi does not dispute this in law. He too says she is called in her husband’s house, treating her like a fully married woman whose husband’s authority does not transfer to her father. The verse itself says, “the vow of a widow,” implying a vow made while she is a widow. Further proof comes from Nedarim 89a: A married woman vowed, was divorced that day, and her husband took her back that day; he cannot annul. “This is the rule: whenever she entered her own authority for even one moment, he cannot annul.” The Gemara says this includes delivery by the father to the husband’s agents, where the husband cannot annul earlier vows. The Rosh explains that if she vowed, was divorced, and then taken back, there is no annulment because she left the father’s authority and the husband cannot annul earlier vows. The Mishnah thus equates delivery to actual marriage. In both, once she left the father’s authority, the father has no later authority at all. The Beit Yosef therefore correctly concludes that even for vows made after the husband’s death she does not return to the father. The Tur’s words clearly contain a scribal error. The word “even” was replaced by “except,” and the correct reading is, “even regarding vows she will make after the husband’s death.” This follows Tosafos exactly, and the Tur regularly rules like Tosafos. My revered father-in-law relied on Rashi to argue that only vows made on the road before the husband’s death do not return to the father, and he disagreed with the Shulchan Aruch. In my view, Rashi also agrees with the Shulchan Aruch, as proven, and my revered father-in-law overlooked Tosafos. We therefore have only the Shulchan Aruch’s ruling, that she has entirely left the father’s authority. The Tur himself equates delivery to actual chuppah, writing, “If he brought her in and died, or even if he did not actually bring her in but the father delivered her,” etc. My revered father-in-law explains “brought her in” merely as bringing her into his authority, not chuppah. This is incorrect. Everywhere in this siman, and in the Gemara’s phrase, “he brought her in on condition that she had no vows,” “brought her in” means chuppah. If the Tur meant merely entry into his authority, why would he then write, “or even if he did not actually bring her in but the father delivered her”? Delivery itself is entry into his authority. The correct law is therefore as the Shulchan Aruch ruled.

If she became betrothed again on that same day, even to one hundred successive men, her father and the final betrothed man may annul the vows she made before the first betrothed man, who died before hearing. It appears that “on that same day” refers to everything preceding it, meaning that the first betrothed man also died on the day the father heard. Otherwise, even if the first remained alive, he could not annul, according to the Rambam’s ruling at the beginning of the siman and seif 4, that both must hear on the same day. One should not ask why the latter betrothed man’s partnership is needed, since according to the Ramban cited nearby the father alone should suffice. This case is different. Since a later betrothed man now exists, the father must join with him. Only where no betrothed man exists on the father’s day of hearing may the father annul alone. It appears that the Rambam agrees with the Ramban, and the Tur cited the Rambam only to question his ruling, not to dispute the Ramban. It is therefore extremely difficult that the Shulchan Aruch here states in the Rambam’s name that even if the first betrothed man died after the father’s day of hearing, the father and second betrothed man may annul. Why should the second be stronger than the first? The Derishah answers that immediately upon the first man’s death, before the second betrothal, the husband’s portion returned to the father. Although the father could not annul it alone, the day of the father’s hearing now became a day of hearing for the husband’s portion as well. If she becomes betrothed again that day, the father and second man share the same day of hearing. This differs from the first man’s lifetime, when the father had no authority over his portion. I do not understand even the beginning of this reasoning. How can the first man transmit to the father a right he never possessed because he did not hear on the father’s day? How can the successor be stronger than the one through whom he comes? At most we can say that the second stands in the place of the first, as the Ran explains, but not that he is stronger. The Shulchan Aruch’s intent requires further study.

His difficulty is from Shmuel on 71a, who explains the Mishnah, “the final betrothed man annuls together with the father,” to include even vows presented to the first betrothed man, meaning that he had heard them. The Beit Yosef answers that the Rambam agrees and that his condition, “since the first did not hear,” refers to the case in seif 19 involving divorce, where we are uncertain whether divorce is confirmation. This does not fully resolve the matter, for regarding death too the Rambam explicitly writes “provided the first did not hear,” as I copied in seif 16. My revered father-in-law explains that the Rambam distinguishes. If the father first annulled his portion, thereby weakening the first betrothed man’s portion, the first must not have heard. If the father did not annul first, then even if the first man heard, the father and latter man may annul. This does not appear correct. First, what source would the Rambam have for such a distinction? Furthermore, Scripture disproves it. Shmuel derives from “and if she shall be” that the latter stands in the place of the first, and from the additional word “upon her” that even vows presented to the first may be annulled by the father and latter man. If a case where the father had already annulled were not included in “upon her,” it would likewise not be included in “and if she shall be,” upon which “upon her” depends. Then the latter would not stand in the first’s place at all. It appears simple that the Rambam does not rule like Shmuel in this respect. The Gemara asks whether divorce is confirmation or silence. It does not resolve the question from the Mishnah that the latter betrothed man annuls together with the father. If divorce were confirmation, how could the latter annul what the first confirmed? The Gemara dismisses this by establishing the Mishnah where the first did not hear, and the question remains unresolved. If Shmuel’s interpretation that the Mishnah includes vows heard by the first were accepted as halacha, the question would be resolved and that dismissal would fall away. Thus, the Rambam, who rules that the question remains unresolved, does not rule like Shmuel. The Ran cites this proof for the Rashba, who holds that Shmuel resolves the question, whereas the Ramban holds it remains unresolved. Accordingly, the Rambam likewise does not follow Shmuel. It appears that Shmuel’s statement is not halacha regarding divorce, where the first must not have heard. Regarding death, however, we may rule like him even if the first heard. This may be the Tur’s view, though it requires study. One should not challenge the Rambam from his ruling that if the first betrothed man heard and annulled, then died, and afterward the father heard and she became betrothed that same day, the father and latter man may annul. There, the first heard and completed his own role by annulling. The vow is therefore not called one merely “presented” to the first, for he removed himself and did what he could. Ideally, the father alone should annul after the first’s death, because the husband’s authority returned to him. Since a latter betrothed man now exists, the father must join with him. This differs from a vow the first heard and silently left pending until death. His failure to annul resembles explicit confirmation, unless Shmuel’s derivation from “upon her” teaches otherwise, and the Rambam does not accept that derivation for the reason explained. Therefore, practical halacha should follow the Rambam stringently, that the father and latter betrothed man may annul only if the first had not heard.

<b>To appoint an agent. </b>The verse says, “Her husband shall confirm it and her husband shall annul it,” not his agent. Although ordinarily a person’s agent is like himself, Scripture revealed otherwise here. The Tur concludes, “even concerning vows she already made.” This appears obvious. Could the agent be stronger than the husband, who cannot annul before she vows? The answer is that the Baraisa states: If one says, “Any vows my wife will make before I return from a certain place, annul them for her,” and the agent annuls them, perhaps they should be annulled. Scripture therefore says, “Her husband shall confirm it and her husband shall annul it,” according to R’ Yoshiyah. R’ Yonasan answers that throughout the Torah a person’s agent is like himself. This might imply that R’ Yoshiyah forbids only when the agent performs both acts, hearing and annulment, but not when he performs only one. <b>He must intend the particular woman. </b>This is derived from the word “her,” specifically her. <br><b>Regarding what is written, </b>“for so they told him,” the Gemara on 60a asks whether “her” must be exact. Regarding tearing one’s garment, Scripture says, “for Shaul and for Yonasan his son,” yet a Baraisa teaches that if they told him his father died, he tore, and it was later discovered that his son died, he fulfilled the obligation. The Gemara answers that one case was unspecified and one explicit. According to Rashi and Tosafos, unspecified means that he did not intend either his son or father, and likewise with annulment he intended no particular woman, but whoever had vowed. Explicit means that he annulled specifically for his daughter, thinking it was she. The Ran and Rosh explain that even in the unspecified case he thought it was his daughter, but did not state orally that he acted for his daughter. The Tur’s language here follows Rashi and Tosafos. In the following case he writes merely that he was told, “A woman in your house vowed,” and he annulled, implying he did not think it was his daughter. Likewise, in siman 340 regarding tearing, he equates one who was told his father died and tore with one who was told merely that someone died, thought it was his father, and discovered it was his son. If the discovery came after the time of immediate speech, he did not fulfill the obligation. At first glance, this is difficult, for the Tur departs from the Rosh. It appears that after the Gemara first distinguishes between unspecified and explicit, Rav Ashi gives another answer, distinguishing within and after the time of immediate speech. The Ran says Rav Ashi does not dispute the first answer, while others say he does, as the Beit Yosef cites from the Rif and Rosh in siman 340. The Tur therefore ruled that according to those who say Rav Ashi disputes, his dispute applies only if “unspecified” is interpreted like the Rosh, where he still thought it was his daughter. Rav Ashi would reject that. If “unspecified” is interpreted like Rashi and Tosafos, meaning he intended whoever had vowed, that distinction remains reasonable and Rav Ashi certainly does not dispute it. Thus, the Tur does not actually disagree with the Rosh; the Rosh follows his view that Rav Ashi rejects the first distinction. The Rama here, however, appears to follow the Rosh, making the law depend specifically upon explicit oral identification, rather than the Tur. He may have ruled this way because siman 340, seif 24, likewise makes it depend upon speech. As practical halacha, one should distinguish. Annulment is Torah law, so we should be stringent that it is ineffective even without oral identification if he was mistaken and thought it was his daughter. It is effective only where he annulled without specifying any person, for whoever had vowed. Tearing is rabbinic, so one may rely on the lenient ruling stated there.

The Ran writes that according to the Gemara’s version with “for you” twice, one must explain that he expressly said, “It is confirmed for you and annulled for you simultaneously,” using inclusive language to combine them. The Tur says only that he said the words simultaneously. Unless “for you” appears once, there is no language combining them. The Gemara leaves the question unresolved. The Rosh explains that perhaps we follow the first phrase, or perhaps both take effect together and we cannot determine whether it was confirmed or annulled. The Gemara resolves from Rabbah’s principle: anything that cannot take effect successively cannot take effect simultaneously. One who betroths two sisters at once betroths neither, since he could not betroth them one after another. So too, since annulment cannot follow confirmation, neither takes effect simultaneously. This implies that there is neither annulment nor confirmation, and he may still choose either. We do not rule stringently that it is confirmed, for the comparison to Rabbah’s case nullifies the words entirely. The Perishah likewise writes that he may do as he wishes. This appears the Tur’s view. The Rambam, however, rules that it is confirmed. He may hold this case stronger than simultaneous kiddushin of sisters, because he first said “confirmed” and then “annulled,” while “for you” applies to both. The confirmation therefore certainly does not disappear. The question is only whether the later “annulled” can cancel it, and the Gemara’s principle proves that it cannot. In the sisters’ case, “betrothed” is plural and applies equally to both. Here “for you” is not plural so as to place both statements fully together. The comparison is only for the principle that whatever cannot take effect successively cannot take effect simultaneously. This resolves the Beit Yosef’s difficulty with the Rambam.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>“It is confirmed for you today.” </b>The Gemara asks whether this is equivalent to saying, “It shall be annulled for you tomorrow,” or whether he did not say that. If one concludes that he did not, it is confirmation. The Gemara then asks about, “It shall be annulled for you tomorrow.” Perhaps he cannot annul tomorrow because he confirmed it today, or perhaps, since he did not explicitly say “confirmed today and annulled tomorrow,” his words mean that the annulment should begin today. If one concludes that confirmation today makes the vow existent tomorrow and tomorrow is no longer a time for annulment, the Gemara asks about, “It is confirmed for you for one hour.” Perhaps this is like saying, “It shall be annulled after one hour,” or perhaps he did not say that. If one concludes that he did not, it is confirmation. It then asks where he explicitly says, “It shall be annulled for you after one hour.” Perhaps confirmation for one hour is confirmation forever, or perhaps, since the entire day is a time both for confirmation and annulment, the later annulment is effective. The question remains unresolved, and we rule stringently that all these statements constitute confirmation. The Tur omits the third question, “confirmed for one hour,” because the fourth case explicitly says, “and annulled after one hour,” and still does not help, all the more so if he did not say the second phrase. My revered father-in-law wrote that the third case should be inserted in the Tur, but that is not precise. The Rambam counts the third question because he distinguishes the first three from the fourth. In the first three he rules that it is confirmation, not merely because of doubt, but because the Gemara’s successive “if you say” discussions treat them as resolved. The final question remains truly unresolved, so he writes it as a doubt. He therefore needed to mention the third case to teach that it is not doubtful like the fourth. This is straightforward.

נקטי' כלומר שאמרה בענין שתשמישה נאסר על כל העולם וכן כתבו הרא"ש והר"ן בפירוש בזה שאסרה הנאת תשמיש כל העולם עליה:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>She forbade upon herself relations with every man. </b>The Tur writes: If she said, “My relations are a konam upon the entire world,” etc. The Beit Yosef objects that it should have said the reverse, “The relations of the entire world are a konam upon me,” because “my relations are a konam” is ineffective while she is obligated to her husband. He also objects to the Tur’s conclusion that after divorce she becomes forbidden even to him, since matters between husband and wife remain permitted after divorce until she marries another. The Maharshal answers the second question: That rule applies only where the vow concerns the husband alone. Here, she forbade the entire world and he is merely included among them. His annulment therefore removes his portion only while she is married to him. Upon divorce he becomes like every other man. The Perishah and my revered father-in-law write that this also answers the first question, because the inclusive nature of the vow allows it to take effect despite her obligation. <i data-commentator="Nekudot HaKesef" data-label="♯" data-order="4"></i>I say they were not precise. Inclusion does not help against his existing right. Nedarim 89a asks how a woman can consecrate something not yet in existence. Rav Huna son of Rav Yehoshua answers that she says, “My hands shall be consecrated to their Maker,” because her hands exist. The Gemara then asks how they can be consecrated when they are obligated to her husband, and answers that she says, “when I am divorced.” Her statement concerning her hands is fully inclusive, preventing everyone from benefiting from her handiwork, yet the Gemara still asks from the husband’s lien. Even according to the Beit Yosef, who explains “my hands shall be consecrated” as meaning that what she produces for him shall be prohibited like hekdesh, the Rambam’s words prove the point. The Beit Yosef cites him: If she says, “My hands shall be consecrated to their Maker,” or vows that he shall not benefit from her handiwork, he is not forbidden because her handiwork is pledged to him. Thus, even this inclusive formulation does not defeat his lien. Further proof is Nedarim 81a: If she says, “I will not have marital relations,” he should annul. The Gemara asks that if she means, “the benefit of my relations is forbidden to you,” she is obligated to him. She used an inclusive expression prohibiting her relations to the entire world, yet the vow is ineffective regarding him. Had inclusion defeated the lien, the Gemara should have answered so. It is therefore clear that inclusive language does not nullify his right. The Maharshal intentionally offered his answer only to the Beit Yosef’s second question, not the first. The Shulchan Aruch properly avoided copying the Tur’s exact wording. It is obvious that the Tur also did not mean that she necessarily said, “My relations are a konam.” He used that only as shorthand, meaning that she spoke in a manner that prohibited the benefit of all men’s relations upon herself. The Rosh and Ran explicitly describe it that way.
