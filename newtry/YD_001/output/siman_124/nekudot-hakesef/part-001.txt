<b>(סימן קכ"ד בט"ז ס"ק ב') וכתב רש"ל דהקראים דינם כגר תושב ומותר לשתות עמו יין של ישראל. </b>כן צ"ל ובספרי הארכתי בדין הקראים וכאן אכתוב בקצרה דעת מהרש"ל והבית חדש דמותר ומשמע מדבריהם דעדיפי מגר תושב וכ"כ באורח חיים על שם תשובת הרמב"ם ומביאו בבדק הבית אך בתשובת מבי"ט חלק ב' סי' ל"ח הועתקה תשו' רבי שמשון וז"ל ואלו הקראים המחללין את המועדות כאלו מחללין שבתות וכאלו כופרים בכל התורה כולה ויינם יין נסך כעובדי כוכבים עכ"ל משמע לכאורה דאוסרים נמי יין במגען כעובדי כוכבים וכן משמע לכאור' בתשובת רשב"א שהביא ב"י בסימן קי"ט וכן דעת הב"י בבדק הבית סימן קכ"ח דמגען אסור ויינם אפי' נשבעו שלא נגעו בהן אינן נאמנים ע"ש וכן בתשובת רבי בצלאל סי' ג' האריך מאד בדיני הקראים וכתב שהן ממש כעובדי כוכבי' גמורי' ומשמע מדבריו שם שאוסרין ג"כ יין במגעם וכתב שם דהרמב"ם בתשובה מיירי דוקא בקראים שבזמנו משא"כ בקראים שבזמן הזה שקלקלו מעשיהם ע"ש מיהו נראה דאין לאסור יינם אלא בשתיה אבל בהנאה לכ"ע שרי דכיון דלא פלחי לעבודת כוכבים לא גריעי מגר תושב:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>(Siman 124, Taz, seif katan 2) The Maharshal wrote that the Karaites have the status of a ger toshav, and it is permitted to drink Jewish wine with one of them.</b> This is the correct reading. In my works I discussed the law of the Karaites at length, but here I will briefly state the matter. The Maharshal and the Bach permit it, and their words imply that Karaites are superior even to a ger toshav. The same is written in Orach Chaim in the name of a responsum of the Rambam, which is cited in Bedek HaBayit. However, Responsa Mabit, part II, siman 38, records a responsum of Rabbi Shimshon stating that Karaites who desecrate the festivals are regarded as desecrating Shabbos and denying the entire Torah, and that their wine is yayin nesech like the wine of idolaters. This seemingly implies that wine is also forbidden through their touch, as with idolaters. The same appears from the responsum of the Rashba cited by Beit Yosef in siman 119, and this is also Beit Yosef's view in Bedek HaBayit, siman 128: their touch forbids the wine, and they are not believed even if they swear that they did not touch it; see there. Responsa Rabbi Betzalel, siman 3, likewise discusses the laws of the Karaites at great length and writes that they are treated entirely like full idolaters. His words imply that wine is also forbidden through their touch. He explains that the Rambam's responsum addressed only the Karaites of his own time, unlike the Karaites of the present era, whose conduct had deteriorated; see there. Nevertheless, it appears that their wine should be forbidden only for drinking, while benefit from it is permitted according to all opinions. Since they do not worship idolatry, they are no worse than a ger toshav.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>(Gloss, ibid., seif katan 15) And many disagree, etc.</b> He writes that, in his humble opinion, the matter should be treated leniently, and he brings proof from the Rashba. Yet according to our text in Beit Yosef, the Rashba says nothing about lifting; he writes only of carrying the vessel or shaking it, without mentioning lifting. It may therefore be said that, in a case of actual lifting, Rama's ruling is correct. Even according to the Taz's version of Torat HaBayit, the phrase 'and shakes it' may refer to one who carries it, not to one who lifts it. Where he both lifts and shakes it, the Rashba may agree with Rama's ruling. Carrying a vessel on one's shoulder is not considered lifting for this purpose; it is regarded as though the vessel remained resting on the ground. This is evident from the Rambam's ruling, which is derived from the Gemara's statement that a wineskin is permitted whether full or partially empty; see Beit Yosef. From there the Rambam learned that shaking is forbidden even without lifting. If carrying it on one's shoulder were considered lifting, then the non-Jews' carrying of the wineskin in that case would itself be lifting. How, then, could the Rambam derive that shaking without lifting is forbidden? Perhaps that case was different because the non-Jew carried it and thereby lifted it. Rather, the explanation is necessarily as stated, and this is clear.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>(Ibid., seif katan 16) This is exceedingly astonishing, etc.</b> All the later authorities already preceded him in raising this point, and I too mentioned it in siman 125, seif katan 8. <b>(Ibid.) This is astonishing.</b> In my humble opinion, the Rambam wrote this ruling in accordance with the position of his teacher, the Rif. The Rambam found it difficult why the Rif did not rule in accordance with Rav Ashi. He therefore understood that the Rif explains the Mishnah's phrase 'and they permitted it' as permitting benefit, even when the non-Jew acted in anger. The Rif maintains that the reason for the permission is the non-Jew's preoccupation, not the fact that the act was one of throwing. Since the non-Jew touches the wine, what difference is there whether he does so by throwing or by direct contact? If he does not act in anger and there is no preoccupation, the wine is forbidden even for benefit. Why, then, did the Gemara challenge Rav Ashi only from a case in which the act was not done in anger, rather than from the case done in anger? This is no difficulty, because Rav Ashi could have answered that the Mishnah means that they permitted the wine even for drinking. The Gemara therefore had to challenge him from the case not done in anger. This appears to me correct. I later found that Kesef Mishneh explains that the Rambam follows the Rif, his teacher, and that the halachah does not follow Rav Ashi, although he does not fully explain the baraita. Perhaps he understands it as I have written; examine carefully.

Since the wine was flowing and the non-Jew stopped it with his hand, he certainly touched with his hand both the wine beneath his hand and the wine between the boards. Still, in a case of financial loss in the present era, there may be grounds for permission based on the reason Rama gives in seif 24.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>(Ibid.) There is room for great astonishment, etc.; if so, why did the Tur write, etc.</b> This is no difficulty. The Tur stated the matter according to the approach of his father and teacher, the Rosh. At the end of chapter Rabbi Yishmael, the Rosh cites Rabbeinu Tam, and Beit Yosef cites his words at the beginning of siman 125. Rabbeinu Tam explicitly maintains that a non-Jew's unintentional contact through another object is permitted even for drinking, as stated in the Rosh and throughout the poskim. Yet the Rosh repeatedly states there that intentional contact through another object is forbidden even for benefit. In the incident of the non-Jew who climbed a palm tree and, upon descending, touched the wine with the end of a palm branch, the Rosh discusses Rashi's view that unintentional contact through another object forbids the wine for drinking. He writes that Rabbeinu Tam challenges this from the later passage concerning one who took a barrel and threw it into a pit in anger. The Gemara permits it specifically when done in anger, but not otherwise, and answers that the barrel continued moving through the non-Jew's force until it fell into the pit. When it was not done in anger, we are concerned that he touched and poured a libation; when done in anger, we are not concerned. Although he touches the wine through the barrel, this is unintentional contact through another object and is permitted for drinking. Alternatively, when not done in anger it is intentional contact through another object and is forbidden for benefit. The Sages permitted measuring with a rod only because he intended to measure, but if he inserted the rod without that purpose the wine is forbidden. Rabbeinu Tam therefore adopts the version that does not mention the end of the palm branch, but states that the non-Jew touched the wine itself, making it unintentional bodily contact. Contact through another object without intent, however, is permitted even for drinking. Thus, although Rabbeinu Tam permits unintentional contact through another object even for drinking, he forbids intentional contact through another object even for benefit. This demonstrates that intentional contact is more severe than force exerted by the non-Jew. The same implication arises from the other poskim, and the Ran himself also indicates that some maintain that intentional contact through another object is more severe than the non-Jew's force; see there.
