<b>And therefore one should peel it.</b> Explanation: it is a full obligation—not as the Shulchan Aruch implies, that it is only hiddur mitzvah; and so Rabbenu Yerucham: one should be stringent by Torah law. The rishonim asked: why is this different from a knife of Kutim, for we hold a plain vessel of Kutim is not ben yomo—and why different from a radish of chiltit in the chapter "One may not stand," and sharp things in siman 96 forbidden with a Kutim knife because its sharpness spoils it and makes it praiseworthy—implying that without its sharpness it would be permitted? The Rosh and Tosafot answered: here it speaks where the knife is known to be ben yomo; and the Rashba wrote in the name of Rabeinu Yona: here it is forbidden because of smeared prohibition visible on the knife, and whatever is visible is not spoiled even in many days; but with a radish of chiltit there is no concern of visible prohibition, for there it speaks of buying from a shopkeeper, and a plain shopkeeper has clean vessels, and there is only concern of absorbed prohibition—and in this, if it is not ben yomo, it helps. And in Perishah he challenged the Rosh, who is lenient here regarding fat on the knife, from siman 96 regarding radish cut with a knife, etc.; and he answered: here it speaks of a plain vessel of Kutim—as we say regarding it, the plain is not ben yomo, so too we say its plain is well wiped; but there it speaks of an Israelite vessel—as we say its plain was ben yomo, so too its plain is not wiped. And he was not precise—for in siman 122 seif 4 he ruled explicitly that even with an Israelite vessel we say their plain is not ben yomo. And one must explain in the Tur, which brought the dispute of the Rosh and Rashba, in what they argue and why the Rosh was not also concerned for visible prohibition on the knife and why he relied on leniency in a Torah doubt—and he said: he holds that even with visible prohibition there is reason for spoilage, in which all agree, as proved in many places—why different from a piece of prohibition? And I found in Mishmeret HaBayit, who wrote: this answer—that the reason is grease visible on the knife—I hesitate about somewhat; for a recognizable thing, smeared slight grease on the knife's sides, is spoiled somewhat even temporarily, and all the more when it is not ben yomo; and they do not bring proof from a piece of meat and abundant fat standing alone for smearing only; and furthermore through iron it receives taste and is spoiled, and experience testifies to this, end of his words; and it appears Tosafot and the Rosh also hold thus here, therefore they needed another answer. And one must understand the Rashba's and Rosh's views here: the Rashba holds beit hashchitah is called boiling on one side—that the knife can receive from beit hashchitah grease, blood, and anything, but it is not fully boiling regarding expelling the knife's absorption into beit hashchitah; receiving into the knife is easier than expelling from it; therefore if he slaughtered with a Kutim knife there is no concern it will expel into the simanim what the knife absorbed—for beit hashchitah has no power to boil so much; rather concern is from what is visible on the knife from prohibition already cut; in this, sticking in the ground helps; therefore the Rashba called this "somewhat boiling." And in Derishah and Perishah he understood from "somewhat boiling" that therefore the knife absorbs in beit hashchitah somewhat—and from this entered pilpul; and he did not have Tur Orach Chaim before him, who wrote explicitly as I mentioned that it does not absorb at all from knife to simanim; and what the Tur wrote in siman 105—that for the Rashba heat in beit hashchitah expels—means one side we mentioned, from prohibition to knife, but not from knife to simanim; and in Perishah he did not notice this distinction. And it appears clear that also the Rosh, who argues with the Rashba and establishes it in ben yomo, also holds beit hashchitah does not absorb from the knife's absorption, but absorbs from what is visible on the knife—all the more when ben yomo, as he wrote; therefore sharpening and sticking help the knife, as in the primary source Tosafot brought in the first chapter of Chullin (daf 8): you might say: if it absorbs the absorption, what does sticking help for what is absorbed? Rather, as I explained, both hold the primary concern is from what is visible on the knife; but for the Rosh, even in this it must be ben yomo, and for the Rashba ben yomo is not required in this—as explained clearly after investigation; and with this what they ask is resolved: why did the Tur bring the Rashba's plain view that sharpening and sticking help, since for the Rosh they do not help—and according to what I wrote, it is fine.

<b>Permitted to slaughter with it another time.</b> It implies without any rinsing or wiping; and so Maharshal concludes; and it is wondrous from what is in Chullin (daf 8): there is an opinion regarding a treifah knife that hag'alah in hot water is required, and it asks on it: why is treifah different, that it absorbs prohibition—for when he slaughtered kosher it also absorbed eiver min hachai, and the knife should require hag'alah even when slaughtering kosher? And it answers: when does the knife absorb? While the animal lives; and when does it live? At the completion of shechitah—at that moment there is no eiver min hachai—learn that the heter is only because the absorption is only at the end; but if the prohibition is not from absorption but only from what is visible on the knife, it certainly forbids even before completion of shechitah—for this does not need heating; and according to the one who says beit hashchitah is cold, there is prohibition from what is visible on the knife; if so, when slaughtering kosher it should be forbidden to slaughter afterward, since visible prohibition of eiver min hachai is on the knife. And it appears we only ask if the concern is taste absorbed in the knife that will return and absorb in the simanim—we also find in kosher absorption of forbidden absorbed matter from eiver min hachai; but if the concern is only what remains visible, this does not apply to eiver min hachai—for there is no substance of eiver min hachai, only mixed in blood; and since blood does not absorb because simanim are busy expelling their blood, therefore no prohibition even from eiver min hachai; rather they were concerned about treifah animal grease that adheres (in the matter) to the knife, which is something that adheres and joins even at completion of shechitah—in this they were certainly concerned and required kashering the knife, as above. And I saw in Hagahah in the Agudah first chapter of Chullin: also when slaughtering kosher, wiping the knife is required because of eiver min hachai; and therefore it does not ask for the one who says regarding a treifah knife in cold: there is eiver min hachai prohibition, as it asks for the one who says in hot—therefore, end of his words; and Orach Chaim brought and strengthened his words to say: even according to the conclusion that it absorbs only at completion of shechitah—meaning it does not absorb so much that hag'alah is required; but in cold nevertheless it absorbs somewhat before completion of shechitah; and therefore he ruled: even when slaughtering a bird, wiping with something hard is required. And to me it appears as I wrote: otherwise sticking in the ground would be required, like one who absorbs treifot frequently—and we do not find sticking except for a knife that frequently has treifah; and furthermore, how did the Torah command any shechitah—shall we say that at the beginning of shechitah it absorbs somewhat from eiver min hachai prohibition and forbids at completion? Rather it is clear this has no basis and no one is concerned for it at all.
