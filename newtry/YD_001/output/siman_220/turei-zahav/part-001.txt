<b>באמצע השבוע. </b>לאו דוקא כמו שכתב אחר כך או קודם לו ולא אתי לאפוקי אלא עומד ביום השבת דאז אינו אסור אלא עד מוצאי השבת כמו בסעיף ו' בכ"ט באלול כן כתב הר"ן: <br><b>ומ"ש </b>שבוע זה בטור וברמב"ם ובר"ן וברא"ש כתוב שבת זה אלא שבעל הש"ע ס"ל דגם בלשון שבוע נכלל יום השבת דהא לשון שבוע הוא לשון שבעה דהיינו כל שבעת ימי השבוע עם השבת ומ"מ כל שעומד באמצע השבוע לא אסר על עצמו אלא עד כלות הז' ימי השבת. ונלע"ד <i data-commentator="Nekudot HaKesef" data-label="♯" data-order="1"></i>דאם אמר בלשון אשכנז דיא וואך שאין יום השבת בכלל האיסור שלשון זה הוה כלשון ימות החול בלשון הקודש שהרי כשרוצים לומר שבת וחול אומרים בל"א שבת אונ' ווא"ך ממילא נתמעט יום השבת לגמרי משא"כ בלשון שבוע זה דבלשון שבוע נכלל גם שבת דהוא תשלום השבוע אבל בלשון חול הוא עד השבת דלשון ווא"ך הוא הפך דשבת אלא דנ"ל אם אומר לשון חול שלא אוכל בשר אין ה' או ו' וואכי"ן אז הכונה שלו דרך מספר ואז ודאי גם השבתות בכלל דכיון דאמרו דרך מספר הוה כאילו אמר ה' או ו"פ ז' ממילא שבת בכלל אבל זה שאומר דיא ווא"ך אין כוונתו למספר הימים אלא כל זמן שימים הם ווא"ך ולא שבת וכמדומה שכן המנהג מצוי בזה ומכ"ש אם עומד בשבת ואומר שיאסר עליו דיא ווא"ך איזה מין דודאי לא נתכוין על אותו יום אלא על השבוע הבא כן נ"ל להלכה בזה אחר כלל שיש לנו דבנדרים הולכין אחר לשון בני אדם:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>In the middle of the week. </b>This is not precise, as he writes afterward, “or before it.” It comes only to exclude one who is standing on Shabbos, for then he is forbidden only until Motzei Shabbos, as in seif 6 regarding one standing on 29 Elul. So wrote the Ran. <br><b>Regarding what is written, </b>“this week,” in the Tur, Rambam, Ran, and Rosh the text reads, “this Shabbos.” The author of the Shulchan Aruch holds that the day of Shabbos is also included in the term “week,” for the word “week” denotes seven, meaning all seven days of the week together with Shabbos. Nevertheless, whenever he is standing in the middle of the week, he forbade himself only until the completion of the seven days of that week. It appears to me <i data-commentator="Nekudot HaKesef" data-label="♯" data-order="1"></i>that if he says in German, “die Woche,” the day of Shabbos is not included in the prohibition. That expression is equivalent to “the weekdays” in the Holy Tongue, for when people wish to say Shabbos and weekday they say in German, “Shabbos und Woche.” Shabbos is therefore entirely excluded. This differs from the expression “this week,” because the word “week” includes Shabbos as the completion of the week. The expression “weekday,” however, means until Shabbos, for “Woche” is the opposite of Shabbos. Nevertheless, it appears to me that if he uses weekday language and says that he will not eat meat for five or six weeks, his intention is numerical, and the Shabbosos are certainly included. Since he stated it numerically, it is as though he said five or six times seven, and Shabbos is included automatically. When, however, he says, “die Woche,” his intention is not the number of days, but rather the entire period during which the days are weekdays and not Shabbos. I believe this is the common custom in such usage. All the more so, if he is standing on Shabbos and says that a certain species shall be forbidden to him “die Woche,” he certainly did not intend that day but the coming week. So it appears to me as practical halacha, following the principle that with vows we follow ordinary speech.

<b>עד ר"ח אדר ראשון. </b>דהלכה כרבי יהודה דסבירא ליה סתם אדר הוא אדר ראשון ומכל מקום בעד סוף אדר הוא אסור עד סוף אדר השני דשני אדרים כחד חשבינן להו כ"כ הרא"ש ופשוט שאם אמר עד חצי אדר דהיינו עד חצי אדר ראשון ולא אמרינן עד ר"ח אדר ב' קאמר דאין שם חצי נופל על ר"ח השני דדוקא לענין סוף אדר חשבינן להו כחד לענין שנתכוין עד כלות החודש הנזכר בשם אדר יהיה מי שיהיה וכן כתב מו"ח ז"ל:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Until Rosh Chodesh of the first Adar. </b>The halacha follows R’ Yehuda, who holds that an unspecified “Adar” means the first Adar. Nevertheless, if he says, “until the end of Adar,” he is forbidden until the end of the second Adar, because the two Adars are considered as one. So wrote the Rosh. It is obvious that if he says, “until the middle of Adar,” this means until the middle of the first Adar. We do not say that he meant until Rosh Chodesh of the second Adar, because the term “middle” does not apply to the second Rosh Chodesh. Only regarding “the end of Adar” do we consider the two Adars as one, because he intended the completion of whichever month bears the name Adar. So too wrote my revered father-in-law, of blessed memory.

<b>עד הגשם. </b>משמע זמן גשם ומשמע זמן רביעה אחרונה דהיינו ר"ח כסליו שאז כלו כבר ב' רביעיות הראשונה והאמצעית אבל אם ירד בהם גשם די ברביעה ראשונה שהיא י"ז חשוון זה דעת הרמב"ם ולא כהטור שכתב עד רביעה ראשונה אפילו לא ירד גשם. ורביעה פי' גשם על שרובץ על הארץ:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Until the rain. </b>This implies the season of rain, and it implies the time of the final rainfall, namely Rosh Chodesh Kislev, by which time the first and middle rainfalls have already passed. If rain fell during those periods, however, the first rainfall, on 17 Cheshvan, suffices. This is the Rambam’s opinion, unlike the Tur, who wrote that it means until the first rainfall even if no rain fell. “Rainfall” means rain because it lies upon the earth.

<b>עד הגשמים. </b>משמע ירידת הגשמים דוקא ומשמע לשון רבים ומיעוט רבים שנים דהיינו ששני גשמים ירדו רביעה ראשונה ושניה. ומבואר במשנה דגם אם אומר עד שיהיו הגשמים אין אסור רק עד שיגיעו התחלת הגשמים והטעם לפי שאין זמן קבוע והוה כקציר דסעיף י"ב והיינו לפי שאין זמן להתחלת הגשמים שוה כ"כ ר"ן והרא"ש כתב לפי שאין זמן קבוע למשך ירידתן והטור לא הוצרך לכתוב דבר זה שסמך על מה שכתב בקציר בסעיף י"ב וילמוד זה משם וגם אם אמר עד הגשם שדינו עד זמן הגשם מ"מ לא מקרי זמנו קבוע כיון שמשך ירידתם אין להם קביעות כמה יהיו על כן בכל לשון אסור עד שיגיע ההתחלה:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Until the rains. </b>This implies specifically the actual falling of rain. It is also plural, and the minimum plural is two, meaning that two rains must fall, the first and second rainfalls. The Mishnah makes clear that even if he says, “until the rains will come,” he is forbidden only until the rains begin. The reason is that the time is not fixed, and it is like the harvest in seif 12. According to the Ran, this is because the beginning of the rains does not occur at an equal time. The Rosh wrote that it is because the duration of the rainfall is not fixed. The Tur did not need to state this, for he relied upon what he wrote concerning the harvest in seif 12, from which this can be learned. Even if he says, “until the rain,” whose law is that he is forbidden until the rainy season, it is nevertheless not called a fixed time, since the duration of the rain has no fixed measure. Therefore, with every form of expression he is forbidden only until the beginning arrives.

<b>אסור בי"ט האחרון. </b>מצאתי כתוב בשם מהר"ר ליב מפראג מכאן ראיה לו' בשמיני עצרת את יום חג שמיני עצרת הזה דהא מקרי חג עכ"ל ויש לדחות ראיה זו דבנדרים שאני דהולכין אחר לשון בני אדם ובל' בני אדם מקרי חג ולא בשל תורה אבל נראה להביא ראייה ברורה מפרק אלו נאמרין (סוטה דף מ"א) ואי כתב רחמנא בחג הסכות הוה אמינא אפילו יום טוב האחרון כתב רחמנא בבוא כל ישראל הא קמן דאפילו בלשון תורה מקרי חג דהא אצטריך למעוטי ולכתוב קרא יתירה וזה ראיה ברורה שאין עליה תשובה:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>He is forbidden on the final Yom Tov. </b>I found it written in the name of Maharar Leib of Prague that this proves one should say on Shemini Atzeres, “this day of the festival of Shemini Atzeres,” since it is called a festival, end quote. This proof may be rejected, because vows are different, for we follow ordinary speech, and in ordinary speech it is called a festival, though perhaps not in the language of the Torah. It appears, however, that one can bring a clear proof from the chapter “These are recited,” Sotah 41a: “Had the Torah written, ‘on the festival of Sukkos,’ I might have thought even the final Yom Tov; therefore the Torah writes, ‘when all Israel comes.’” We see that even in Torah language it is called a festival, for an additional verse was required to exclude it. This is a clear proof against which there is no answer.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Lest she go, etc. </b>The Prishah copied in the name of the Maharshal: This is said specifically regarding vows, which are lenient, where we say that people are not careful with a condition. Regarding gittin, however, the Rosh ruled at the beginning of the chapter “One who divorces” that whenever a condition is within her power to fulfill, she may marry immediately, and we are not concerned that she may violate the condition, end quote. See Even HaEzer, siman 143. It appears to me that the cases are not comparable at all. There, if she violates the condition she will also become forbidden to her husband from that point onward, and she is therefore frightened to transgress. Here, however, there is no prohibition from that point onward, meaning after Pesach, but only regarding what occurred before Pesach. Because of that, fear will not rise upon her, for what has passed has passed. <br><b>The Tur </b>cited here in the name of the Rambam: “Even though Pesach has passed,” etc., and wrote concerning it, “I do not understand,” etc. In my humble opinion, the Rambam’s words can be explained comfortably. The one making the vow used the additional expression, “until the festival,” even though after Pesach there is no prohibition at all, as the Tur asks. Why, then, did he mention “until the festival”? There are two possible answers. First, he intended that she not benefit until Pesach, lest she later transgress, which is indeed the true law. Alternatively, what he said, “until the festival,” was a retraction. Initially he intended to forbid benefit only until Pesach, and therefore said, “The benefit you receive from me is a konam until Pesach if you go to your father’s house.” At first he did not intend to add anything further. He then reconsidered and said, “until the festival,” meaning that everything should extend until the festival, both the benefit and the going. We find this concept of retraction also in siman 222. The Rambam therefore wrote that if she benefited and went before Pesach, she receives lashes, for in that case the prohibition is certain. After Pesach has passed, however, there remains a doubtful prohibition, for perhaps there was a retraction here. Unspecified vows are interpreted stringently, but lashes are not administered in a case of doubt. So it appears correct to me. <br><b>Furthermore, </b>it appears to me that the Rambam’s reasoning may be explained through what the Rambam wrote, as cited in the Shulchan Aruch, siman 237, at the end of seif 1: Although the others did not accept the oath, if they fulfilled his words they are praiseworthy for not causing him to become accustomed to making an oath in vain. So too here, since in any event he intended in his oath that it last until the festival, even though there is no prohibition of benefit if she goes, one should nevertheless be careful to fulfill as much of the oath as possible. The first explanation, however, appears primary.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>She receives lashes. </b>The Ran explains that this refers to the woman. Although she did not vow anything, she nevertheless violated “he shall not profane,” meaning that the matter of a vow shall not be profaned. Since a person can impose a prohibition upon another, the other person is included in the prohibition of “he shall not profane.” This is unlike the Rambam, who explains that he receives lashes if he provides benefit to her, but she does not.
