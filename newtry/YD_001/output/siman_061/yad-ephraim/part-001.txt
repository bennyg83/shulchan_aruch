<b>חייב ליתן לכהן.</b> ואין מברכין על נתינה זו כ"כ הפר"ח בשם תשובת הרשב"א והרמב"ן בהשגות לספר המצות וכ"כ בכה"ג והשיג על אא"ז דמש"א שכתב שמברך להפריש מתנות וע"ש שהביא מדבר אברהם בשם תשובת ראב"י אב"ד וכ"כ בתמים דעים סי' קע"ט ובמח"ב הביא מהרוקח סימן שס"ו שדעתו כהדמש"א ומ"מ כתב דיחידאה הוא ועיין בפרי תואר שכתב בשם תשובת הרשב"א סי' של"ח שאין הכהן מברך שהחיינו:
**** ENGLISH ****
Obligated to give to the kohen. One does not recite a blessing on this giving, as Rashba and Ramban in Hagahot on Sefer HaMitzvot and Knesset HaGedolah — he challenged Avraham Azulai who wrote one blesses when separating gifts; see there what he brought from Avraham in responsum Ra'avyah; so too Temim De'im 179; and Peri To'ar in Rashba responsa 338 that the kohen does not recite shehecheyanu.

<b>הלחיים.</b> הוא לחי התחתון הימיני והשמאלי ודלא כתרגום יונתן בן עוזיאל שהוא הלחי התחתון והעליון וליתא ובשו"ת הל' קטנות ח"ב סי' ק"ע הניח הדבר בספק וכתב שלא ראה מעשה במקומו (בירושלים) כי הכל פודין במה שנותנין פרוטות לכהן וכתב במח"ב שבזמנו בארץ ישראל מקיימין המצוה שבמתנות עצמם ונותנים הלחי התחתון והלשון:
**** ENGLISH ****
The cheeks. It is the lower right and left cheek — not as Targum Yonatan ben Uziel (upper and lower) — rejected; in responsum Halakhot Ketanot part 8 siman 170 he left it in doubt and wrote he never saw the practice in Jerusalem where all redeem with coins to the kohen; Machaneh Ephraim wrote in his time in Eretz Yisrael they fulfill the mitzvah with the gifts themselves — lower cheek and tongue.

<b>They give them to a female kohen.</b> Yam Shel Shlomo writes that even where a male kohen is available, the gifts may be given to a female kohen. If there is an ignorant male kohen and she is the wife of a talmid chacham, it is preferable to give them to the scholar's wife, because the wife of a talmid chacham is treated like the talmid chacham himself. If there is a male kohen who is a talmid chacham, they are equal; the same applies in the parallel case of an ignorant kohen. Machazik Berachah cites Rambam and Semag that a chalalah does not eat these gifts. Knesset HaGedolah asks that a male chalal likewise does not eat them. Pri Chadash in Derech Mitzvotecha explains that the case concerns a female kohen who became a chalalah through a prohibited relationship. Such disqualification can apply to a woman through prohibited intercourse, whereas a male kohen is not rendered a chalal merely by such an act. But a male born from a prohibited priestly union who is himself a chalal certainly does not eat the gifts. Kiryat Sefer of Mabit writes similarly. Mishneh LaMelech, Hilchot Bikkurim chapter 9, writes that a chalalah not only does not receive the gifts; even if they were already given to her they are taken back, because once she has relations with one who disqualifies her, her priestly sanctity departs.

<br><b>Pri Chadash writes</b> in the responsa appended to Mayim Chayyim, siman 4, that one who transfers one of the twenty-four priestly gifts to a minor kohen fulfills the mitzvah of giving. He bases this on the majority of poskim who hold that where another person confers the acquisition, a minor can acquire by Torah law. He brings proof from Yevamot, where terumah is not distributed to a deaf-mute, mentally incompetent person, or minor at the threshing floor, but is sent to their homes. Tosafot explain that once a minor knows how to perform Birkat Kohanim, terumah may be distributed to him. This requires study according to the authorities who hold that even where another person confers the acquisition, the minor acquires only rabbinically. Even if one says that a rabbinic acquisition has Torah effect through hefker beit din hefker, as discussed in Even HaEzer siman 28 regarding kiddushin with ma'aser sheni, perhaps that helps only to make the item legally "yours." It does not necessarily show that the giver has fulfilled the Torah mitzvah of giving, because the minor did not acquire by force of the donor's gift but rather by rabbinic property law.

Machazik Berachah already raises a related difficulty. The same Yevamot passage says that gifts are sent to the homes of deaf-mutes and mentally incompetent persons. Tosafot and the other commentaries ask only how to prevent them from making the terumah impure and answer that they have guardians. They do not ask the more basic question: how is the mitzvah of giving fulfilled when these recipients lack legal capacity to acquire? This is indeed difficult. Moreover, according to Maharam cited by Mordechai at the beginning of Bava Metzia, since the owner's discretionary benefit in priestly gifts is not legally money, there is no ordinary category of "another mind conferring ownership," and the minor should not acquire at all. If so, even giving to a minor is difficult, because how can the giver fulfill the mitzvah of giving?

One cannot distinguish terumah from the other priestly gifts. Chullin 134 concludes that regarding terumah as well, "giving" is written, and Rashi explains that it must be given to a kohen. Rambam, Hilchot Terumot chapter 12, halachah 17, writes that Chazal instituted that one must take care of abandoned terumah and bring it into the city, receiving reimbursement of transport costs from the kohen, because simply leaving it for animals would constitute a desecration of Hashem's Name. This is surprising, because the Gemara's conclusion sounds as though the obligation of giving terumah is Torah law. Bechorot 23 similarly says that challah may be given to a minor kohen, and challah too is described in Scripture as "giving." Although that passage concerns rabbinic challah, if Torah-level challah could not be given to a minor because he lacks acquisition capacity, one would expect Chazal initially to require giving the challah to an adult who could then feed it to the minor. The simple implication is that even Torah challah could be given directly to a minor if not for its impurity.

It therefore appears that with priestly gifts, although Scripture uses the language of giving, once the owner actually places the gift into the kohen's possession and does not withhold it, the giver has fulfilled his mitzvah even if the recipient's technical acquisition capacity is incomplete. At that moment the object is at least in the kohen's control and he can eat it. The mitzvah of giving does not require that his civil title be so complete that anyone who seized it from him would necessarily be forced to return it. We find an analogous concept with makirei kehunah: although the owner could retract, so long as he does not retract, the kohen's expectancy is treated as his from the time of slaughter for purposes such as a firstborn inheriting a double share. Likewise, Gittin 20 says that a get written on an item prohibited in benefit is valid even though the verse says "and he shall give." Tosafot explain that delivery of the get is called giving, just as one grain can exempt an entire pile as terumah even though it is worth less than a perutah. Where the object is worth less than a perutah, ordinary monetary acquisition concepts are greatly weakened, yet it is still called giving.

<br><b>Now,</b> with poor-person gifts such as leket, shikhechah, and peah, it is obvious that a minor may collect even if he lacks ordinary acquisition capacity. Bava Metzia 12 says that if a minor has no independent acquisition, then where his father is wealthy his collecting is treated as collecting for his father and is prohibited. This implies that if he has no father, he may collect for himself. The institution of three collection periods, including midday for young children who sleep in the morning, likewise assumes minors may gather these gifts. The reason is that Scripture does not say "give" regarding those gifts but "leave them." The owner need only leave them available; he does not have a personal mitzvah to place them into a recipient's legal ownership. Terumah and other priestly gifts, however, do use the language of giving, so one might still have thought they require transfer to someone legally capable of acquisition. Yet, as explained, the law that even less than a perutah can count as giving indicates that full monetary acquisition is not the criterion.

<br><b>However,</b> the primary explanation in my opinion is stronger. Once produce has reached the stage obligating terumah, the kohanim already possess a divinely granted share within the pile. Tosafot Sukkah 35 say that an etrog of tevel is like jointly owned property because the kohanim's portion is mixed into it. The same applies to the priestly gifts from an animal: immediately upon slaughter, Hashem grants the gifts to the kohanim. The owner need not create their ownership; he only possesses the right of determining which kohen will receive them. Thus all kohanim already have a divinely created proprietary interest. Since Scripture uses the word kohen without excluding a minor, a minor is included. Although the proof Pri Chadash brings from Sanhedrin is not decisive because that verse has its own wording distinction, Kiddushin 53 shows that where Scripture says "a man among his brothers," the word "man" is what excludes a minor from a share in sacred offerings. Without such an exclusion, a minor is included in "kohen." Yoma likewise says regarding Aharon's bull that the Torah renders it ownerless for the benefit of his fellow kohanim, making them partners. Bava Kamma says of priestly gifts that kohanim receive from the table of Heaven, and regarding the property of a convert who dies without heirs it says Hashem acquired it and gave it to the kohen. Kiddushin 58 similarly treats the priestly portion of tevel as already belonging to the kohanim where the owner's discretionary benefit is not money.

Accordingly, the minor's share is not acquired through the owner's act at all. Hashem Himself grants the kohanim their share, and that divine grant applies equally to minor and adult. Once so, the owner's giving simply identifies which kohen's preexisting communal share is being delivered. This also suggests a distinction between ordinary priestly gifts and the five sela of pidyon haben. With pidyon haben, perhaps the kohen must actually acquire the money upon receipt, so giving it to a minor who lacks independent acquisition may not effect redemption. On the other hand, since pidyon haben is a Torah debt secured by the father's property, and the minor kohen has a share in the class of creditors created by the Torah, perhaps even there there is no ordinary need for donor-created acquisition. All the more so, Maharam's position that discretionary benefit is not money does not undermine a minor's acquisition of priestly gifts. On the contrary, if the owner does not own the priestly portion because Hashem already granted it to the kohanim, then the divine grant itself is the strongest possible "other mind conferring ownership," and the minor's title is complete.

This also provides another answer to Tosafot's question in Sanhedrin at the beginning of Perek Ben Sorer about how a minor convert could ever possess property for the law of robbery of a convert, given that a minor ordinarily has no acquisition and inheritance from a Jewish father is unavailable. Tosafot answer that he may acquire through another person, or inherit from his father before conversion, or receive wages. According to what I have written, another possibility is that he received ma'aser ani. Scripture says, "and you shall give to the convert, the orphan, and the widow." A minor included among the poor can acquire that divinely allocated share by Torah law, and one who steals it from him would owe restitution.

<br><b>Now,</b> the incident of Rabban Gamliel and the elders, where he transferred tithes to R. Yehoshua and R. Akiva by renting them the place where the produce stood, is often explained through the principle of "another mind conferring ownership." Maharam in Mordechai says that although Rabban Gamliel possessed only discretionary benefit in the gifts, he also transferred the courtyards to them. The distinction is difficult, because even what he transferred as produce was not fully his. It appears instead that Rav Papa holds the recipients needed no new acquisition of the produce itself from Rabban Gamliel; Hashem had already granted them the priestly and levitical share. What was required was fulfillment of the Torah's "giving," meaning entry into their domain. Because they were not physically standing by the rented place, the question was whether it counted as their domain. Rav Papa answers that where another person's mind is intentionally bringing the item into the recipient's domain, that intent can make the courtyard effectively guarded for him even though he is not standing beside it. "Another mind conferring ownership" there is shorthand for the owner's intent to remove it from his own control and place it into theirs. The actual substantive entitlement to the gift came from the Torah.

Poor gifts must again be distinguished. Leket, shikhechah, and peah require no act of giving because "you shall leave them" is written; they are abandoned to the poor, who acquire through collection. Ma'aser ani, however, is described as a gift. There too the owner has only discretionary benefit, while the underlying poor-person share is granted by the Torah when the produce is obligated. The owner's act determines which eligible poor person receives the divinely allocated share. Thus, if a minor has no father whose hand legally absorbs his acquisitions, one may give him ma'aser ani even if one assumes he lacks ordinary independent acquisition, because the Torah already included him among the poor. But if the minor has a wealthy father and his hand is legally treated as his father's hand, the Torah never granted him a poor person's share in the first place. The same is true even of an adult child supported by a wealthy father where his acquisitions legally belong to his father. One cannot fix that by the owner's later desire to "give" the ma'aser specifically to the child, because the produce does not belong to the owner beyond discretionary benefit, and that discretion extends only to eligible poor recipients. Even a condition that the father have no rights in the gift would not help if, at the moment the Torah allocated the poor people's share, this recipient was not legally classified as an independent poor person. Similarly, with money set aside as ma'aser ani, once the poor have acquired the communal claim, the owner cannot later redirect their portion to someone who was not legally eligible when the right arose.

<br><b>I found</b> in Responsa Rashba, at the end of siman 18, that no blessing is recited over priestly gifts because the kohanim receive from the table of Heaven. Regarding the property of a convert who died without heirs, the Gemara says Hashem acquired it and gave it to the kohen. R. Yosef ben Plat answered Raavad similarly, as cited also in Tamim De'im. This supports what I have written: one giving priestly gifts does not give from his own property; the gifts belong to Hashem's allocation, as Scripture says, "I have given you the charge of My terumot." Therefore, a minor included among the kohanim acquires through Hashem's grant.

I later saw Ketzot HaChoshen, siman 243, who also discusses Pri Chadash and raises the same questions from Maharam in Mordechai and from the rule that gifts are sent even to mentally incompetent recipients. He writes along some of the same lines but adds that priestly gifts resemble wages in exchange for the Temple service, and Tosafot hold that a minor can acquire wages. Therefore, even kohanim who cannot perform the service can acquire the gifts. This is difficult. Wages are owed by the particular employer to the worker, so the worker takes what is already due him. Here the owner owes the minor nothing personally; Hashem transfers the owner's property to the kohanim. Why should it matter whether Hashem's reason was compensation for service or some other purpose? Indeed, if it were literally wages for service, minors and other kohanim disqualified from service should be excluded. The fact that they receive the gifts proves the gifts are not ordinary wages. Scripture calls them a gift. The phrase "in exchange for your service" praises the kohanim because Hashem chose them for His service and therefore granted them these gifts, not because each gift is contractual compensation. One could perhaps say that because the kohanim received no territorial share in Eretz Yisrael and live from Hashem's offerings, the gifts function as a broad communal entitlement, but this too is ultimately a divine grant rather than wages. The straightforward explanation remains that Hashem granted the gifts to all kohanim, including minors, and giving to a minor therefore fulfills the mitzvah.

My relative, the author of Netivot HaMishpat, writes that because the owner can renounce his discretionary benefit, he may give the gifts even to a minor. With due respect, he overlooked Chullin 134, which shows that merely abandoning one's discretionary benefit does not fulfill the mitzvah of giving; one must actually bring terumah and give it. His proof from the possibility of betrothing a woman with the discretionary benefit in terumah is also not decisive. If discretionary benefit is legally money, a man can betroth a woman with the monetary value of her ability to obtain a perutah from a kohen in exchange for choosing him as recipient. But the mitzvah of giving terumah must still be fulfilled by delivering it to a kohen. If the terumah ultimately reaches a kohen capable of acquisition, whether through the woman or another person, the mitzvah can be fulfilled. That does not prove that merely handing it to someone who cannot acquire constitutes the Torah's required giving.

<br><b>Regarding what was written,</b> Ketzot further cites Pri Chadash that giving priestly gifts against the kohen's will still counts as giving, and argues that the same should therefore be true of giving to a deaf-mute, mentally incompetent person, or minor. He compares this to divorce by Torah law, where a mentally incompetent woman can sometimes be divorced without her consent because a get does not require the woman's consent. In my opinion this comparison is not correct. Even where Scripture says merely "and he shall give," the item must enter the recipient's legal domain in a manner that can constitute acquisition. Certainly one who obligates himself to give another person a particular object does not fulfill the obligation by placing it where the other person acquires nothing. With a get too, if the verse said only "and he shall give to the woman," it would still need to enter her domain. The additional word "into her hand" teaches the special requirements of a hand-like domain, such as being near her courtyard or guarded for her. Gittin itself explains that "and he shall give" might imply any sort of delivery, while "into her hand" teaches the additional quality of the domain. Thus giving against the recipient's will works only because the giver has done everything required to transfer it into a legally effective domain; the recipient's refusal is not a defect in the giver's act. But placing property into the hands of someone wholly incapable of acquisition would ordinarily fail to satisfy a Torah requirement of giving.

Still, the earlier proof from one grain of terumah is relevant. One grain exempts the pile even though it is worth less than a perutah. Perhaps one can distinguish: less than a perutah is still capable of legal acquisition in some contexts; its lack of enforceable monetary significance reflects ordinary waiver. Bava Metzia teaches that once a court is already engaged in adjudicating a perutah claim, it completes judgment even for less, and Noachides are prohibited from taking even less than a perutah because they do not have the same presumption of waiver. Therefore, "giving" can apply to less than a perutah. That does not itself prove that giving to someone who has absolutely no legal capacity to acquire works.

For this reason I return to the central argument: since the Torah did not exclude minors from priestly gifts, they themselves acquire a real Torah entitlement in those gifts. It cannot be that a child receives a divinely assigned share yet any passerby can lawfully seize it from his hands. Even if one were to say a later taker does not acquire and must return it to the original owner because the owner's discretionary benefit was directed to the child, the arrangement would still be unnatural and contrary to the ways of pleasantness. The better conclusion is that the Torah itself gives the minor kohen full ownership of the priestly gift when it is allocated to him.

The same issue deserves thought regarding poor children collecting leket, shikhechah, and peah. Bava Metzia seems to imply that if a minor has no independent acquisition, whatever he gathers could be taken from him. Yet Chazal established collection times specifically to accommodate young children. It is difficult to imagine that their labor was intended to be fruitless because others could immediately seize what they collected. This point itself requires further study, but it reinforces the broader conclusion that the Torah's allocation to protected recipient classes cannot be reduced entirely to ordinary private-law acquisition categories.
