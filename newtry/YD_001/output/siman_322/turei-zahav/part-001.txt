<b>There are three laws of challah, and so forth.</b> Two major approaches are involved. Tosafot and Rosh explain the Mishnah in Challah as follows. From Eretz Yisrael to Keziv, only one challah is separated. From Keziv to the river and Amanah, two challot are separated, one burned and one given to a kohen. The burned challah has a fixed measure while the kohen's does not. Beyond the river and Amanah, two challot are again separated, one burned and one eaten, but there the burned one has no fixed measure and the eaten one does. A tevul yom may eat the latter. Rosh explains that the middle territory lies near the lands of the nations and is exposed to tumah, so its challah is impure and must be burned, while a second challah is instituted for a kohen so the law of challah not be forgotten. Because the burned challah in that region retains Torah status it has a fixed measure. In the more distant Diaspora both challot are rabbinic, so the edible challah is the more important one and receives the fixed measure. The Mishnah's prohibition to zavim, zavot, niddot, and women after childbirth refers to one who did not immerse. R. Yose is lenient regarding a baal keri and requires no immersion, while the Sages require immersion for impurity arising from the body but not for ordinary contact impurity. A fourth law appears in Bechorot: Ravina rules that a niddah separates challah and a minor kohen may eat it; if no minor kohen is available, she throws one challah into the oven and separates another so challah law not be forgotten, and even an adult kohen may eat the second. Where a minor kohen exists no second challah is needed. Rosh explains that this fourth law concerns distant countries, where the challah does not resemble challah of Eretz Yisrael. There the first challah can be eaten by a minor kohen or by an adult kohen who immersed, and the extra stringencies of the nearer Diaspora do not apply. Rambam has a different approach. He effectively combines the third and fourth categories and does not distinguish near from distant Diaspora. His main distinction is geographical sanctity: the first region was conquered both by those who entered with Yehoshua and by those returning from Babylonia, so only one challah applies. The middle region was conquered by the first settlers but not by those returning with Ezra, so two challot are separated because the first is impure and burned, while a second is eaten so people not think pure terumah is burned. Outside Eretz Yisrael, two challot are separated because otherwise people might think impure terumah can be eaten. In those latter categories a fully pure kohen can make a second challah unnecessary. Rambam thus does not distinguish proximity to Eretz Yisrael. Tur's wording is difficult because it seems initially to follow Rosh's near-distant distinction, then later says that where a pure kohen is present the second challah is unnecessary, which according to Rosh applies only to the distant category. Tur then says that from Rambam it appears the leniency is specifically outside Eretz Yisrael, which sounds as though the first opinion allowed it even in Eretz Yisrael. That cannot be correct. Perhaps Tur means only that even Rambam, who does not distinguish near and distant lands, still distinguishes Eretz Yisrael from the Diaspora. Shulchan Aruch here follows Rambam. It is surprising that Beit Yosef did not note the dispute between Rambam and Rosh.
