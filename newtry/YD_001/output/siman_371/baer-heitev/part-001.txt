<b>והעליה. </b> הטעם כתב הש"ך משום דטומאה בוקעת ועולה כיון שהיא מונחת תחת הארובה ואין הטומאה מתפשטת לצדדי הבית הואיל שיש מקום לטומאה לעלות לארובה שכנגדה ואינה חוזרת ויורדת לבית כיון שאין בארובה טפח שאם היה בה טפח הוה ליה חשבינן בית ועליה כמו חדר א' וכתב הט"ז וא"ל שיהיה פתח הבית ההוא טמאה משום שסוף הטומאה לצאת דרך אותו הפחח כמ"ש בס"ד דכאן שאין שם רק כזית מן המת ואין עליו אהל הוה כמונח ברה"ר ואינו מטמא כלל בבית ומזה למדנו שאם יש מת בבית ומונח בחדר שאין עליו אהל אין לכהן להיות בכל הבית הן מטעם שהטומאה חוזרת ויורדת והן מטעם שסוף הטומאה לצאת דרך הפתח דלא הקילו כאן אלא אם אין פתוח רק פחות מטפח עכ"ל:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And the upper floor.</b> Shach explains that impurity pierces upward because the corpse is beneath the opening. Since the impurity has a direct route upward through the opening, it does not spread sideways through the house and then descend again. If the opening were a full tefach, the house and upper story would be treated like one room. Taz adds that one cannot say the doorway of the house becomes impure because the corpse will eventually leave through it, as stated below in seif 4. Here there is only an olive-sized portion of a corpse and no tent over it, so it is like impurity lying in the public domain and does not render the whole house impure. From this we learn that if a corpse is in a house, in a room without a roof over it, a kohen may nevertheless not remain elsewhere in the house, both because impurity can descend again and because the corpse will ultimately be removed through the doorway. The leniency here applies only where the opening is less than a tefach.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>They are stringent.</b> Shach writes that one need not be stringent concerning the building used for Tzidduk HaDin, because that very doorway serves to bring impurity into the structure. Piskei Mahari. Ma'adanei Melech writes that this reason is sufficient where there is only one doorway used both to bring the deceased in and take him out. Where there are two doorways, one used for entry and the other for removal, as in the Tzidduk HaDin house in Prague, a kohen should be stringent and avoid the doorway through which the deceased is taken out. Taz writes that the decisive factor is the doorway of the actual house containing the corpse, because the impurity is destined to exit there even while the door is closed. Other houses are different, and there is no special significance to their doors being locked. He therefore says that Mechaber's wording, "if all the doors of the houses are locked," is not exact. Responsa Chacham Tzvi, siman 103, disagrees and concludes that there is no concern at all in a Tzidduk HaDin house. Kohanim may enter it even when there is a corpse in the city, whether it has one doorway or two.

However, if the door in the cellar is made as they call in the vernacular "gigatir," even though no single hole is a tefach, nevertheless since in the combination of the holes together there is an opening of several tefachim, there is room to be lenient that it is within "its exit opens a tefach." Also, what he wrote to permit kohanim to close their openings and windows — this is an impossible thing, for closing the opening is by way of a key and also by way of iron hinges, and this does not interpose, as he wrote above with a clear proof; and we have never heard of practicing this permit for kohanim; and how much our rabbis were lenient to be careful for the kohen not to tell him until he dresses himself — and they did not practice this permit; rather, certainly this is not so, end of his words. (And the Bach wrote that the beam must be round so that its width be a tefach.) And I remember when we were learning in the house of my teacher, Moreinu HaRav Rabbi M. of blessed memory, and there was an open doorway from the rabbi's courtyard to the courtyard of the synagogue, and the lintel of the doorway reached on one side under the ceiling of the house in which there was a dead person, and my teacher forbade the kohanim to go out and come by way of that doorway the whole time the dead was in the house. And the Derishah wrote: And I heard that that is specifically regarding a partition that has a small roof upon it, or a partition whose lintel upon it is a tefach wide, etc.; but a partition extending from house to house with no roof upon it and also no open doorway within the partition — even if the beam resting upon it is a tefach thick, and also the roofs of the houses on both sides of the partition at their edge overshadow them — nevertheless it does not bring the tumah, since in the middle of the partition there is no roof or ceiling upon it; since there is no doorway there, it is called sealed in the middle and has no power to carry the tumah. And the Taz wrote: It appears from his words that if there is a free partition with no roof upon it and there is a doorway in its middle, it brings the tumah from house to house, since at the head of the partition there is an ohel from the roof of the tamei house, even though the doorway does not reach that part which is under the roof. And I wonder greatly at this matter: since there is an interruption in the part of the partition, who will bring the tumah from under the roof to the doorway in the middle? And even with an interruption of less than three we do not say lavud for stringency. And therefore the Maharshal only forbade where the ceiling was laid without a partition under it from under the roof of the tamei house to under the roof of another house — so that there was space under the ceiling without interruption of a sealed partition; which is not so here, where there is an interruption of a partition built on the ground and there is no protrusion of a tefach near the part under the second roof — certainly there is no bringing of tumah at all, just as with two adjacent houses with no roof protruding above them: tumah does not enter from house to house even though both are open, for we require protruding roofs. And it appears that one need not be concerned for tumah when there is a built partition, even though there is a doorway in its middle — not throughout the entire partition — unless there is also upon it a small roof or a protrusion of a tefach from that side that enters under the tamei house to the part that reaches under the second roof. And the Shach wrote: And it appears that the roof must be an ohel of a tefach from beginning to end; but whenever there is an interruption between them of a high wall, even if it is several tefachim thick — since there is no ohel of a tefach, the tumah again has no place to go; and so many great ones agreed, and such is the simple custom, end of his words.

<b>To approach.</b> Derishah writes that from here it is clear that even within four amot of a corpse lying before us a kohen may not approach, either because the corpse legally occupies four amot or because of a decree lest he touch the corpse itself. This is unlike those who are lenient and allow a kohen giving a discourse to stand even within four amot of the deceased. Shach writes that the lenient practice may be based on a distinction: where a corpse is lying permanently in a house, a kohen may not approach its fixed place because it occupies four amot. But where the corpse lies on a bier being carried out, all the more so while actually being carried, and it is merely set down temporarily during Tzidduk HaDin, it does not acquire a fixed four-amot domain. Rokeach makes this distinction.
