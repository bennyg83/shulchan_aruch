**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Perhaps because of desire, etc. </b>The Maggid Mishneh wrote in chapter 9 of Hilchos Issurei Bi'ah that this is a rabbinic matter, for the Sages were stringent through this decree; according to the Torah she is entirely permitted, for even if she saw blood without sensation she is not impure according to the Torah, all the more so in this case, where she did not even find a stain, etc. End quote. This resolves for me what is stated in the first chapter of Sotah regarding Tamar, “and she sat at the entrance of Einayim,” meaning that she gave attention to her words and told Yehudah, “I am pure from niddah.” We likewise find regarding Rus that she entered Boaz's bed after immersing. This is difficult, for they should have required seven clean days after agreeing together to marry one another. According to what I wrote, it is resolved, for this decree had not yet been enacted. However, this requires further study. Since it is rabbinic, where do we find that this decree was newly enacted? If it was through Rava's ruling that a woman who was asked to marry and consented must observe seven clean days, and Rava introduced this stringency in his time, there is a difficulty from the incident in the chapter HaCholetz. When Rav arrived in Darshis, he said, “Who wishes to be my wife for a day?” The Gemara challenges him from Rava's ruling that a woman who was asked to marry and consented must observe seven clean days. What is the challenge? This stringency was only introduced in Rava's time. It appears to me that when the Sages decreed concerning stains, as stated at the beginning of siman 190, and did not permit a woman merely because she had not sensed a flow, this decree was enacted as well; the fact that she felt no sensation does not help her. So it appears to me. <br><b>Furthermore, </b>it appears that one can answer that regarding Tamar and Rus there was no concern that they might see blood because of desire. Immediately upon Yehudah's and Boaz's consent, respectively, they were together with them in bed. This is comparable to one who has bread in his basket, unlike the case of an ordinary bride.

<b>Seven days. </b>The Tur cites this in the name of the Rashba, as follows: “This does not refer to a case in which she was asked to marry after 12 months, for the appointed time is distant and she does not experience desire because of it,” etc. It is clear that the Rashba, who holds this view, does not accept the ruling cited by the Rama below that if relations do not take place she must examine herself every day. The Rashba wrote in Toras HaBayis HaAruch that even during the seven clean days she does not require examinations, and certainly not afterward. Rather, he holds that she need only observe seven clean days with the presumption that she is pure; he does not hold that additional desire arises afterward. Therefore, he says that since she must in any event count seven clean days from the proposal before marrying, it does not help for her to observe seven clean days immediately if she will marry only after 12 months. At present she has no desire at all. Her desire arises only when she prepares herself for the chuppah, so what benefit would there be in the seven clean days observed now? Rather, the count begins when they prepare the beer for the chuppah, or when they inform her. From that point the seven clean days are effective even if much time remains until the chuppah, because after she observed seven clean days following the beginning of the proposal, when the primary desire occurred, she is thereafter like all other women and need not examine herself at all. The Derishah found it difficult why the Rashba did not explain that she will again experience desire later, shortly before the wedding, but instead explained that the time is distant and she does not experience desire because of it. He explains that the practical difference is where she was asked to marry after 12 months and began counting seven clean days, but on the third or fourth day of her count they agreed to bring her into the chuppah immediately. She must then count seven clean days anew, not from the original proposal, since there was no desire at the time of that proposal. Accordingly, the Rashba concludes that the count begins when they place the barley in water to prepare the wedding beer, etc. Even if it is customary to hold the chuppah, for example, four weeks later, the count nevertheless begins then, for if in the interim they agree to hold the chuppah immediately, she need not count another seven clean days. End quote. The Derishah understood that she must in every case observe seven clean days specifically close to and before the chuppah. He therefore explained that the Rashba means that one does not count from the proposal when the wedding will be after 12 months. That is, if she now counts seven clean days and during those seven days they decide to hold the wedding immediately, those seven clean days are ineffective, since the desire arose only when they decided to bring her into the chuppah. If, however, they began preparing the beer and she counted seven clean days, while several weeks still remained until the wedding, and they then decided to hold it after those seven clean days, she need not count another seven clean days. According to his explanation, however, if they did not advance the wedding, she would require a second set of seven clean days before the wedding. He was not precise in this interpretation. According to the Rashba, once she has observed seven clean days from the time of the desire, she requires nothing further and is like all other women. Other poskim were stringent that she examine herself from the beginning of the seven clean days until relations take place, and this is a l'chatchilah requirement, as the Rama wrote.

<b>If they postponed the marriage, etc. </b>The Mordechai writes there as follows: “If they postponed the marriage, for example because the father of the groom and the father of the bride could not reach an agreement, whether because of the dowry or the wedding expenses, then even if she already observed seven clean days, she must again observe seven clean days once they reach an agreement and resolve the matter together and intend to proceed with the marriage,” etc. End quote. This language implies that the marriage was postponed without setting another wedding date. Rather, the parties separated from one another in a manner in which they did not know when the marriage would occur. This was with the consent of both sides, as implied by the expression “they postponed the marriage,” meaning that it was definitively deferred from taking place at that time. It also implies that both sides agreed to this. Therefore, she certainly did not maintain proper awareness during her examinations. We learn from this that if either of these two conditions is absent, the law is different. Thus, if the wedding was not definitively postponed, but they failed to reach agreement regarding payment of the dowry and therefore the marriage did not take place that day, while they still anticipated reaching an agreement and holding the feast the next day, this is not considered a postponement. The same applies even if the groom firmly declares that he does not wish to marry her because her dowry was not paid, while the bride's side pursues him through legal claims and compromise in order to persuade him until he agrees. Even if this continues for one or two days, it is not called a postponement of the marriage, for throughout that time the bride relies upon the expectation that the groom will be persuaded. Were you to say that even in such a case new desire arises when the uncertainty leaves her mind and the matter becomes certain, then even on the wedding day itself there are many occasions when the groom is obstinate and explicitly says that he will not marry her until several hours later, when peace is restored between them. Would the bride then need to observe seven clean days anew? This has neither been heard nor appears correct. On the contrary, such cases occur every day and no one is concerned for this. Therefore, what difference is there whether the dispute lasts that day or continues for one or two days? The bride certainly does not remove the marriage from her mind and relies upon the expectation that the marriage will take place after they reach an agreement. The Levush wrote: “It appears to me that even if at the outset, after they had prepared for the chuppah, they postponed it from the original day but immediately stipulated and set another specific day, even only one or two days later, she must observe seven clean days anew. Since she diverted her mind from the original time, the desire is aroused a second time shortly before the later date.” End quote. His words do not appear correct at all. Even Maharik, who is stringent as cited by the Beit Yosef, was stringent only because perhaps she saw blood without realizing it once the marriage had been postponed, etc. That concern does not apply here, for she certainly knows the exact wedding date and continues to examine herself. The same is true in the case I described, where a dispute regarding payment of the dowry continues between them and the bride's side is occupied with compromise and reconciliation. She certainly remains aware that this is the ordinary course of such matters. My father-in-law, of blessed memory, likewise disagreed with the Levush where they set another date, and ruled that another seven clean days are unnecessary unless they jointly agreed not to marry one another at all. The general rule is that there is concern only when both sides agree that the wedding will not take place at the designated time and they set no other date. In that case, even if the matter remains unresolved only one or two days and they then reconcile and decide to hold the wedding immediately, she must observe seven clean days anew. This is the case discussed by the Mordechai and the Shulchan Aruch here. It further appears to me that if the groom did not arrive on the designated wedding day because of an unavoidable circumstance that befell him on the road, and only after that day sent a messenger to the bride explaining why he did not come either yesterday or today, the bride requires seven clean days anew. Since he failed to arrive on the designated day, she certainly became uncertain, for all roads are presumed dangerous, and perhaps some danger would prevent him from coming at all. When she subsequently learns that he is coming, this certainly creates new desire. I saw in the responsa Masas Binyamin that in such a case she does not require another seven clean days, because she assumes that he was delayed by circumstances beyond his control and will come when the impediment passes. I have written what appears correct to me. <i data-commentator="Nekudot HaKesef" data-label="♯" data-order="1"></i>She certainly worries either that he is in danger or that he no longer desires her, and that this is why he failed to appear on the wedding day. Therefore, when this doubt leaves her mind, new desire arises. So it appears in my humble opinion. <br><b>The Levush writes </b>that if the groom was exchanged, meaning that the first groom refused to bring her into the chuppah on the wedding day and the bride agreed that another man should marry her, and he married her immediately, she requires seven clean days anew, for new desire has arisen, etc. This is so obvious that it need not have been written. It appears to me, however, that if after agreeing to the second groom the first groom returned and reconciled with her, she likewise requires seven clean days anew, since another man intervened between them. So it appears to me. If the act has already occurred and he had relations with her in violation of the prohibition, see what I wrote at the end of this siman.

<b>יש אומרים אם היתה טהורה כו'. </b>בת"ה שם הביא דעת האוסרים כסתם לישנא דתלמודא דאמר שאם בעל א"צ שמירה ויש מתירים דמ"ש התלמוד בעל היינו ראוי לבעול והכריע בעל ת"ה להיתר וראייתו מדמצינו בשור המועד דראה שוורים שלש פעמים ולא נגח חזר לתמותו אלמא דלא אמרינן דבהאי זימנא נח יצריה והיום או למחר הדר יצריה הכי נמי בנדון זה ומ"מ המחמיר תע"ב עכ"ל ולע"ד <i data-commentator="Nekudot HaKesef" data-label="♯" data-order="2"></i>שאין הנדון דומה לראיה דמה שהביא ראיה משור המועד אינה ראיה דהא קי"ל סוף פ"ק דבבא קמא דף ט"ו סתם שוורים בחזקת שימור קיימי כו' נמצא דכל שור שהוא מועד לנגוח הוא משונה מטבע סתם שוורים ומשום הכי כל שאנו רואים שראה שוורים ולא נגח נתבטל שינוי שלו ונעשה כשאר השוורים משא"כ כאן דסתם אדם יצריה תקיף עליה ולא מוקים אנפשיה כמ"ש התוספות בפרק קמא דכתובות דף ט' ד"ה מאי לאו כו' וז"ל א"נ כיון דמתייחד ודאי בא עליה דלא מוקי אינש אנפשיה ואע"ג דגבי יבמה אמרינן דעד שלשים יום מוקי אנפשיה רוב פעמים לא מוקי אנפשיה אפילו שעה אחת כו' עכ"ל נמצא דהאי חתן שלא בעל הוא נשתנה מטבע העולם ולא אמרינן שבשביל שנשתנה שעה אחת או ימים אחדים יהיה כן לעולם אדרבה אמרינן כל זמן שאנו רואין שנשתנה הרי נשתנה ובזמן שאין רואין שנשתנה יחזיר הדבר לטבע האדם שהוא רוב העולם ואין לך בו אלא חידושו ומחידושו לא ילפינן אדרבה יש ראיה להיפך ממה שכתבו התוספות הנ"ל דביבמה עד שלשים יום מוקי אנפשיה ותו לא הרי דהוחזק שלשים יום בזה שלא בא עלי' ואפילו הכי אמרינן דאח"כ תקיף ליה ולא מעמיד נפשיה ה"ה נמי הכא דאין לך בו אלא חידושו על כן נראה לי כדברי האוסרין כמשמעות לשון התלמוד דאין היתר רק כשבעל ואין זה בכלל המחמיר תע"ב אלא דין גמור הוא שצריך שמירה:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Some say that if she was pure, etc. </b>In Toras HaBayis there, the author cites the view of those who prohibit, in accordance with the plain language of the Gemara, which says that if he had relations with her, supervision is unnecessary. Others permit, explaining that when the Gemara says “he had relations,” it means that he was fit to have relations. The author of Toras HaBayis ruled leniently. His proof is from a mu'ad ox that saw oxen three times and did not gore, thereby reverting to the status of an ordinary ox. This demonstrates that we do not say that its impulse merely rested at that time and will return today or tomorrow. The same should apply in this case. Nevertheless, one who is stringent is deserving of blessing. End quote. In my humble opinion, <i data-commentator="Nekudot HaKesef" data-label="♯" data-order="2"></i>the case is not comparable to the proof. His proof from a mu'ad ox is not valid, for we rule at the end of the first chapter of Bava Kamma, 15a, that ordinary oxen have a presumption of being guarded, etc. Thus, every ox that is mu'ad to gore deviates from the nature of ordinary oxen. Therefore, whenever we observe that it saw oxen and did not gore, its deviation is nullified and it becomes like other oxen. Here, however, the ordinary human being has a powerful inclination and does not restrain himself, as Tosafos wrote in the first chapter of Kesubos, 9a, s.v. Mai lav, etc.: “Alternatively, since he secludes himself with her, he certainly has relations with her, for a person does not restrain himself. Although regarding a yevamah we say that he may restrain himself for 30 days, ordinarily a person cannot restrain himself even for one hour,” etc. End quote. Thus, this groom who did not have relations departed from the ordinary nature of the world. We do not say that because he deviated for one hour or several days he will continue that way forever. On the contrary, as long as we observe that he continues to deviate, we accept that fact; when we no longer observe it, the matter reverts to ordinary human nature, which characterizes the majority of people. The novelty extends no further than its particular case, and one cannot derive anything beyond it. Indeed, the Tosafos cited above provide proof to the contrary. Regarding a yevamah, a man may restrain himself for 30 days, but no longer. Although he established a pattern for 30 days by not having relations with her, we nevertheless say that afterward his inclination will overpower him and he will not restrain himself. The same applies here. The novelty extends no further than its particular case. Therefore, the view of those who prohibit appears correct to me, in accordance with the plain language of the Gemara, that permission exists only if he actually had relations with her. This is not merely a case in which one who is stringent is deserving of blessing. Rather, it is the actual law, and supervision is required.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>One who remarries his divorced wife, etc. </b>It appears that if he transgressed and married her within the required period, supervision is unnecessary. Since he already had relations with her when she was his wife the first time, his inclination is not now so powerful, and supervision is unnecessary. So it appears to me. <br><b>An incident occurred </b>involving a widow whose marriage agreement fixed a specific wedding date with her groom, but the groom told her that he might advance the wedding, and he did so. She said that she relied upon this possibility, counted seven clean days, immersed, and then had relations with him.
