<b>נכסיו מתמוטטין כו'. </b>בברייתא סוף פרק (אלו נערות) [איזהו נשך] ולא עוד אלא שמשימין משה רבינו חכם ותורתו אמת (כלומר על דרך סגי נהורא) שאומרים אלו ידע שיש ריוח בדבר לא היה כותבו ולכאורה תמוה וכי לא ידע שיש ריוח טוב למלוה ברבית ונראה פירושו שאומרים שיש ריוח טוב ללוה במעות שלוקח ומרויח בו הרבה וע"כ אין עליו איסור אם נותן רבית כך הם אומרים המלוים ברבית:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>His property collapses, etc. </b>In the Baraisa at the end of the chapter Eizehu Neshech it states: Not only that, but they make Moshe Rabbeinu into a wise man and his Torah into truth, meaning by way of irony, for they say, “Had he known that there is profit in the matter, he would not have written it.” At first glance this is astonishing, for did he not know that there is substantial profit for one who lends with ribbis? It appears that the explanation is that they say there is substantial profit for the borrower through the money he takes, for he earns much with it, and therefore there is no prohibition upon him if he pays ribbis. This is what those who lend with ribbis say.

<b>ופירש בשביל שילוהו. </b>זהו דעת הטור ואפי' למה שכתב בסעיף ד' שלא התנה עמו כו' שאני הכא שהוא קודם להלואה. ובשם הרמב"ם כ' הטור גם כאן דאסור אפי' בסתם ולעיל כתב הטור בשמו דס"ל כרש"י דמותר אפי' [בסתם] (במפרש) נ"ל דהכא קאי אזהרת האיסור על הלוה שהוא יודע שהוא שולח בשביל ההלואה שילוה ממנו ורחמנא בוחן לבבות ובזה ס"ל לדעה ראשונ' דאפ"ה אין איסור אא"כ מפרש והרמב"ם אוסר אפי' בסתם אם מכוין לכך אבל לעיל קמ"ל דכל שהלוה נותן סתם אמרי' בודאי לא בשביל הלואה הוא דמסתמא אינו עושה איסור ולפי מה שפרשתי דברי הטור דכאן בסימן זה סעיף י"ז שפיר טפי דהא דהתיר הרמב"ם לעיל בנותן יותר היינו במכירה ע"ש:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And he specified that it was so that he would lend to him. </b>This is the view of the Tur. Even according to what he wrote in seif 4, that he did not stipulate with him, etc., here it is different because this occurs before the loan. In the name of the Rambam, the Tur writes here as well that it is forbidden even without specification, whereas above the Tur wrote in his name that he agrees with Rashi, who permits it even without specification. It appears to me that here the warning concerning the prohibition is directed toward the borrower, who knows that he is sending it because of the loan that the other will grant him, and the Merciful One examines the heart. Regarding this, the first opinion holds that even so there is no prohibition unless he specifies it, whereas the Rambam forbids it even without specification if this is his intent. Above, however, the Tur teaches that whenever the borrower gives extra without specification, we presume that it was certainly not because of the loan, since ordinarily he would not commit a prohibition. According to my explanation of the Tur's words below in this siman, seif 17, this is even more satisfactory, for the Rambam's permission above when one gives more refers to a sale. See there.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Even to his sons, etc. </b>This implies that they borrow money from him and pay him ribbis. <i data-commentator="Nekudot HaKesef" data-label="♯" data-order="1"></i>What is written afterward, “and he certainly gives it to them as a gift,” would then mean that he returns and gives it back to them, or that he does not take it from them at all. This is forced. The Rambam, however, writes explicitly that he is not particular with them and gives it to them as a gift, implying that he pays them ribbis, but there is no prohibition in this payment because it is merely a gift. This is likewise the intent of the Tur and the Shulchan Aruch. The phrase “it is forbidden to lend” means to enter into a loan from them, and the Levush likewise records: It is forbidden to borrow from his sons, etc. Yet there is a difficulty from what the Gemara states: A person is permitted to lend to his sons with ribbis in order to let them taste the taste of ribbis, but this is not proper, lest they become accustomed to it. Rashi explains that this means to let them taste the pain of ribbis, so that they will know how distressed the payer is, etc. According to the Rambam, we cannot explain it this way. <i data-commentator="Nekudot HaKesef" data-label="♯" data-order="2"></i>It appears that the Rambam explains the Gemara as follows: If a person wishes to train his sons so that they may earn a livelihood by lending to non-Jews with ribbis, he accustoms them by paying them ribbis, so that they will know that it is a good means of livelihood. The Gemara concludes that this is forbidden, lest they become accustomed to it and do so even with a Jew. This explanation is preferable to Rashi's for two reasons. First, according to Rashi, why did it say “it is permitted”? It should have used language indicating that there is a mitzvah to do so, in order to separate them from a prohibition. Second, the Gemara says “the taste of ribbis”; it should have said “the pain of ribbis.” According to the Rambam, this is satisfactory.

<b>לא יאמר לו הודיעני. </b>פי' שבזה שמצוה עליו והוא נכנע לו הוה רבית דברים וברמב"ם כ' דע אם בא איש פלוני כו' כמו שהביא ב"י ונדחק לפרש דלפי זה תרי מלין הוויין והך לא יאמר יהיה טעמו דה"ל רבית ממון ואע"ג דלא מהנה המלוה פי' מכיסו של לוה וגם לא התנה כו' וק"ל דהא במשנה שם וגם ברמב"ם לא הביאו דין זה רק ברבית דברים ונ"ל לדקדק עוד למה נקט הרמב"ם שמכבדו ומאכילו ולא אמר שתתן לו איזה סך מעות וגם למה זכר איש ממקום פלוני כל זה הוא שלא לצורך ע"כ נ"ל דהעיקר בזה שודאי דרך עולם לכבד אכסנאי באכילה כדי שאם יבוא הוא שם יחזור ויכבדו או שעושה כן מצד גמילות חסד וכאן שבא המלוה להזהיר הלוה שיעשה כן אע"פ שהוא היה עושה כן מעצמו ואין כאן רבית ממון מ"מ מה לו לזה שיצווהו על זה אלא ודאי סומך על שהוא לוה שלו והוה רבית דברים שמצוה עליו דברים בשביל ההלואה:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>He shall not say to him: Inform me. </b>This means that by commanding him and having him submit to his instruction, it constitutes verbal ribbis. The Rambam writes, “Know whether a certain person has arrived,” etc., as the Beit Yosef cites. The Beit Yosef is forced to explain that according to this there are two separate matters, and that the reason for “he shall not say” is that it constitutes monetary ribbis, even though the lender does not benefit from the borrower's pocket and did not stipulate, etc. This is difficult to me, for both the Mishnah there and the Rambam bring this law only in connection with verbal ribbis. It further appears to me that one should note why the Rambam specifically says that he should honor and feed him, rather than say that he should give him a certain sum of money, and why he mentions a person from a particular place. All of this appears unnecessary. Therefore, it appears to me that the essential point is that it is certainly normal practice to honor a guest with food, either so that if the host later comes to the guest's place the guest will reciprocate, or because of kindness. Here, however, the lender comes and instructs the borrower to do so. Although the borrower would have done so on his own and there is no monetary ribbis, what basis does the lender have to command him? Clearly, he relies upon the fact that the other is his borrower. It is therefore verbal ribbis, because he commands him concerning matters on account of the loan.

<b>מותר לומר לחבירו הילך זוז כו'. </b>נראה פשוט דוקא בדרך זה אבל אם אומר כל זמן שלא יתן לך פלוני מעות שאתה מלוה לו אני אתן כל חדש דינר שזה איסור גמור אע"פ שאין הרבית בא מיד לוה למלוה מ"מ זה שמקבל עליו הרבית הוי ליה כלוה גם הקרן דכל זמן שאין הלוה פורע לו הקרן יש על זה חוב הרבית נמצא גם אחריות הקרן עליו וסברא זאת כתבה ב"י בשם בעל התרומות תמצאנה בסי' קס"ט סעיף ט"ז בדברינו:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>It is permitted to say to his fellow: Here is a zuz, etc. </b>It appears obvious that this is permitted only in this manner. If, however, he says, “For as long as that person does not repay you the money that you are lending him, I will give you one dinar each month,” this is an absolute prohibition. The Beit Yosef writes this reasoning in the name of the Baal HaTerumos. You will find it in siman 169, seif 16, in our comments.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Tell a certain person to give you four dinars. </b>The Beit Yosef writes this in the name of Rabbeinu Yerucham and gives a weak reason for it, namely, that since there is a prohibition where one says, “Give a maneh to a certain person and I will lend to you,” the same should be forbidden here. There is no need to answer this, for it refutes itself. My revered father-in-law, of blessed memory, wrote that this ruling is a scribal error, since the borrower suffers no loss, and that it should read: “that he should give me four dinars,” etc., which is obviously forbidden. It appears to me that there is no scribal error, and Rabbeinu Yerucham's words are correct. I will set out before you the words of the Gemara in the first chapter of Kiddushin, 6a. A woman who said to a man, “Here is a maneh, and I shall become betrothed to you,” is betrothed when he is an important person, for through the benefit that he accepts from her she resolves to transfer herself to him. Rashi explains that the benefit is that she would be willing to give a perutah to another person to persuade this man to accept a gift from her. End quote. So too is the ruling in Even HaEzer, siman 27. It appears to me that there the requirement that he be an important person applies only where he himself accepts the gift. If he were not important, although she derives pleasure from his accepting it, since she desires this, the benefit nevertheless has no monetary value through which we could say that she transfers herself to him, for a woman is betrothed specifically through money, not through a benefit that is not worth money. Rashi therefore had to explain that, with an important person, she benefits by not having to give a perutah to someone else to persuade him to accept the gift. Accordingly, if she said, “Tell a certain person to give you a gift, and I shall become betrothed to you,” she is betrothed even if he is not an important person. Since he spoke at her direction and she desires this, had he not done so she would have paid another person a perutah to say it. She therefore derives monetary benefit from his agency, for a person does not trouble himself to speak without compensation, and she is particular that the statement be made. It therefore appears to me that she is betrothed in this case even if he is not important. As a matter of practical halacha, although we would not rely upon this view leniently to say that she is certainly betrothed, such that if another man betrothed her she would not require a get from him, nevertheless stringently we certainly say so, and she may not marry another without a get from the first. The same applies to the prohibition of ribbis. If a lender says to a borrower, “I will lend you money on condition that you accept a gift from me,” then if the borrower is an important person who does not ordinarily accept gifts without persuasion, there is a prohibition of ribbis; if he is not important, there is no prohibition. But if he says, “Tell a certain person to give you a gift, and I will lend you,” the borrower desires that statement, for otherwise he would pay someone else a perutah to make it. This is therefore absolute ribbis. It appears clear to me that Rabbeinu Yerucham's words are correct and well founded.

<b>And he is not supported at his father's table. </b>The Tur concludes: But if he is supported at his father's table, it is forbidden. Since he did not conclude, “but if he is a minor and is supported,” this implies that whenever he is supported it is forbidden in every case, even if he is an adult. A minor, however, is forbidden even if he is not supported at his father's table, for his father is nevertheless obligated to support him when he has nothing, as the Beit Yosef writes. Here, however, the reason is that it is considered as though the father received it, since when the minor son has nothing the father is obligated to support him. So it appears to me.

<b>There is no agency for a transgression. </b>The Mordechai writes this in the name of Rashi. The Beit Yosef writes that an erring student wrote it and attached it to a great authority, for it is absolutely forbidden. He proves this from the Tur's statement that the donor may not go back and take it from the borrower, and also from the law of a Jew who borrows with ribbis through a non-Jew, etc. The Darkei Moshe defends Rashi's words, arguing that there is no proof from the Tur's statement that the donor may not go back and take it, because there the loan itself passes from the lender to the borrower, and only the payment of ribbis is through another person. The Mordechai distinguishes this case. Based upon this, the Rama writes below: Nevertheless, if the borrower himself received the money, etc. The Levush disputes the Rama with strong reasoning, although his statements contain no proof. <i data-commentator="Nekudot HaKesef" data-label="♯" data-order="3"></i>With God's help, I will bring proof for his words. First, the distinction between whether the loan itself is also carried out through an agent or not has no basis. Either way there is a difficulty. If the intermediary is not considered the borrower's agent vis-à-vis the lender, then there are two transgressions. The principal and the intermediary are like a lender and borrower, for the intermediary is the principal's counterparty and gives him ribbis. Afterward, the intermediary is like a borrower vis-à-vis the lender, who lends him the money and receives ribbis from him. We find similarly that where a Jew holds a non-Jew's money and lends it to another Jew as the non-Jew's agent, it is forbidden, since there is no agency for a non-Jew, as stated in the Gemara, 72b. Here too, since there is no agency for a transgression, the intermediary becomes the borrower's counterparty and likewise the lender's counterparty. Furthermore, if there is no transgression in the ribbis payment made to the intermediary, what transgression occurs if the intermediary takes the loan into his own hand from the lender? There is no prohibition in the loan itself, even if he says, “Lend to me with ribbis,” provided that he afterward pays no ribbis. What then is his sin? If you say that the statement itself is sinful because he mentions ribbis, then if he merely says, “Lend to me,” without mentioning ribbis, there should be no prohibition, since the loan is permitted and the later payment of ribbis to the intermediary would supposedly be meaningless. This is astonishing, for there is an explicit Mishnah at the end of Eizehu Neshech regarding advance and delayed ribbis: If he sends him a gift so that he will lend him his money, or afterward because his money had remained idle with him, it is forbidden. There, at the time of the loan they knew nothing of the ribbis, and nevertheless it is forbidden when an agent brings the ribbis. There is a further difficulty from one who is forbidden by a vow to derive benefit from another. In siman 221, seif 8, we rule that it is forbidden to give him benefit through his agent, and we do not say that there is no agency for a transgression. Rather, it is clear that the principle that there is no agency for a transgression is effective only to exempt the principal from the punishment of kares, as in the case of slaughtering offerings outside the Temple in the chapter HaIsh Mekadesh, or from lashes, as in the first chapter of Bava Metzia regarding one who rounds another person's hair through an agent. A prohibition nevertheless remains, for he commits a forbidden act and his intent is fulfilled through the agent. It is obvious that this is forbidden. There is another proof from the Gemara in Eizehu Neshech, Bava Metzia 70a: What should be done with orphans' money? We find a trustworthy person and give it to him through beit din under terms close to profit and remote from loss. Rashi explains that beit din has authority to declare the recipient's property ownerless for the benefit of the orphans and to stipulate that he give them terms close to profit, etc. There too, the ribbis does not pass directly from the recipient to the lenders, who are the orphans, yet ordinary ribbis is forbidden; only terms remote from loss and close to profit are permitted through the power of beit din. We do not say that even absolute ribbis is permitted merely because the loan itself does not pass directly from lender to borrower. One cannot reject this by saying that beit din acts as the father of the orphans and therefore stands in their place, for this is true only to benefit them, not to their detriment. Moreover, if so, the question remains: Why do they act in this manner? They should appoint an agent and thereby allow the orphans to take even absolute ribbis. It is therefore clear that this is absolutely forbidden. This is likewise explicit in the Beit Yosef later in this siman, s.v. Apotropus, in the name of the Rashba: “Because he did not give it to the guardian for the guardian to retain for himself, but so that he would give it to the widow or the owner of the money; the guardian is merely an intermediary in the sin,” etc. I copied this in seif 20. The Levush therefore wrote correctly that this ruling should be erased from the Shulchan Aruch, and no person should rely upon it at all, even in pressing circumstances and great need. Certainly an erring student wrote it in Rashi's name, and such a stumbling block did not emerge from the mouth of that righteous man.

גריעותא באמת מ"מ כיון שזכר לשון מתנה מהני וכמו שאמרנו והכל ניחא:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Even if they stipulated, etc. </b>The Beit Yosef cites the Hagahos Ashiri: “If he is not a Torah scholar, even if they stipulated initially that it would be a gift, it is forbidden.” The Beit Yosef explains that stipulating that it be given as a gift is preferable to making no stipulation at all. He afterward brings proof from an Ashkenazic responsum, which states that the Or Zarua distinguished a case where he stipulated initially that it would be a gift, etc. According to this, the word “even” used here by the Rama in permitting it is not satisfactory. In truth, however, I am astonished at the Beit Yosef, for that Ashkenazic responsum is the responsum of the Maharil, siman 37. It concerns a man who held the money of his orphaned stepson on terms of fixed ribbis, fed the orphan, provided all his needs, and also taught him. The Maharil ruled that there was certainly a prohibition of fixed ribbis, but he sought merit for the orphan. He reasoned that it was at least as though they had stipulated that the guardian would provide from the profits the amount needed for the orphan's minimal support. If he spent more than the minimum, the excess could be viewed as a gift made in honor of his wife. Concerning this he wrote: “The excess he gives him as a gift. Although some authorities forbid rabbinic ribbis even when designated as a gift, nevertheless the Or Zarua distinguished that where he stipulated from the outset that it be a gift, it is permitted for a Torah scholar. The same applies to a minor orphan who is not subject to punishment. All the more so, this is not actual ribbis but only appears like ribbis, and all the more so here, where he stipulated nothing. Rather, by rabbinic enactment it is as though they stipulated that he should provide the orphan's support up to the amount of the profit, while the remainder is not an obligation but charity and affection for his wife, as is the practice of householders.” End quote. His intent is clear: A stipulation is worse. The Maharil was troubled by his own statement that the excess is a gift, because some authorities forbid ribbis even when given as a gift, apparently referring to the Rambam cited by the Tur in this siman. It appears that there is a scribal error in the responsum, and it should read: “Although some authorities forbid ribbis when designated as a gift,” namely the Rambam, who disputes the Geonim who permit even Torah-level ribbis in such a form. To this the Maharil answered that here it is not actual ribbis but only appears like ribbis, because it is normal for a man to support his stepson, as he writes there. He afterward adds, “All the more so, here he stipulated nothing,” meaning that he did not stipulate to support him, and in this respect the case is even better. When he writes that the Or Zarua distinguished, his meaning is as follows: He first said that even if there had been a stipulation with the orphan to provide all his needs as a gift, which would be forbidden with another person because merely mentioning the word “gift” would not help, nevertheless for a Torah scholar the use of the word “gift” is effective, and it is as though he made no stipulation for fixed ribbis at all. The same therefore applies to an orphan: Mentioning that it is a gift is effective, and the stipulation to provide all his needs does not harm him. He then concludes, all the more so here, where there was no stipulation at all and no correction is needed. The Hagahos Ashiri is likewise readily understood. Its meaning is that for a Torah scholar, if he stipulated and explicitly mentioned that it was a gift, this is effective. If he is not a Torah scholar, however, even though he stipulated and mentioned that it was a gift, this correction is ineffective, and because he stipulated it constitutes fixed ribbis. The Rama therefore properly writes here: “There are those who say that even if he stipulated initially in this manner,” for the stipulation is indeed a disadvantage; nevertheless, since he mentioned that it was a gift, it is effective, as we have explained. Everything is thus satisfactory.

There, it is different because the kohen receives the terumah without payment, which is a substantial benefit. It is therefore correct to say that the donor gives him that benefit because of the loan. Here, however, with labor or the sale of land, the benefit does not come to him without payment. The giver therefore grants no benefit relevant to ribbis. One should not object that it must be forbidden because Choshen Mishpat, at the beginning of siman 307, states that a craftsman is considered a paid custodian by virtue of the benefit that the owner gives him the object to repair and thereby allows him to earn the fee. This is not so. Although the lender receives this benefit, it is not considered any loss to the borrower upon which the designation of ribbis could fall. Otherwise, the case in siman 172 would also be forbidden, since an ordinary sale is beneficial to both buyer and seller, as stated below in siman 227, seif 1, regarding an average sale, etc. Rather, it is obvious that ribbis does not apply merely because of such benefit. The same is true in the present ruling. This requires further study.
