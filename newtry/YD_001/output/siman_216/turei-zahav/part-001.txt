And it appears that the view of Rosh is also thus, who wrote on page 56 that growths of growths are forbidden in the latter part because it is the body of the prohibition since its seed is not consumed. If so, also when he says these fruits are upon me — nevertheless he benefits from the body of the prohibition. And it is a fortiori, for in the latter part where he says she'eini to'em, he is permitted in substitutes and growths when its seed is consumed — nevertheless it is forbidden even in growths of growths when its seed is not consumed. All the more so in the first part where it is forbidden in substitutes and growths even when its seed is consumed — that it should be forbidden when its seed is not consumed even in growths of growths. And for this reason Rambam and Tur did not need to write explicitly in the first part that it is forbidden in growths of growths when its seed is not consumed, for they relied on the fortiori we mentioned. And therefore he properly ruled here in Shulchan Aruch in this law that growths of growths are forbidden when its seed is not consumed. And Maharshal wrote that this is the view of the Ran alone, but Rambam, Rosh, and Tur hold that in the first part growths of growths are permitted — and he derived this ruling from halachah. And it is surprising for the reason we mentioned; on the contrary, Rosh wrote explicitly that even substitutes of substitutes are forbidden, all the more so growths of growths, since it is substance in existence, for its seed is not consumed — end quote. Certainly that ruling is clear. And Beit Yosef wrote in the name of the Ran: specifically here where he specifies and says these fruits — we treat them like hekdesh in substitutes and growths; and likewise when he says fruits of such-and-such place are upon me. But when he says konam olives and grapes are upon me, as is written at the end of this siman — he did not intend except to eat that species and did not treat them like hekdesh — therefore they are not forbidden in substitutes and growths — end quote. And that growths written at the end is without precision, for in any case it is forbidden in their growths, for they are fruits of that species and he forbade the entire species upon himself — only in substitutes alone is there a distinction. And so Rosh wrote explicitly there in the Mishnah that we do not require that he say "these," but konam alone; only because of substitutes alone they forbid only when he says "these." And it is possible the Ran deals with one who says konam olives and grapes of this year are upon me — then he is permitted in growths that will grow afterward, provided he did not say "these." And in this substitutes and growths are equal:

And the latter part of she'ani ocheil or to'em teaches a novelty that even when he says these fruits she'ani to'em, it is as if he did not say "these," and we follow the language she'ani to'em — for here it is as one who says "these," since he specifies, as I wrote. Only it is difficult for me: since here are two languages that contradict each other — from the language mimaseh yadeikh it is as one who says "these," for for this reason it is forbidden in substitutes and growths; and also the Tur necessarily holds thus, for otherwise it would be as one who says konam olives and grapes, where there is no prohibition in substitute and growth except that it is as one who says "these," as Ran wrote. And from the language she'ani to'em it implies permission in substitute and growth — and we must follow stringency, as the Tur wrote afterward regarding leveitcha zeh. Granted for Ran it is difficult, for he holds the latter language is primary both for leniency and stringency, as Beit Yosef wrote in his name. And Ran arranged this law regarding his wife not in the order of the Tur, but he placed first konam maseh yadeikh and afterward she'ani to'em — automatically we follow the latter language. But for the Tur it is difficult. And it appears to me the Tur holds that one may not say we follow stringency except where one language can stand without its companion — that is, when he says beitecha zeh, where it was possible to say beit zeh or conversely beitecha alone — therefore we do not know which is primary. Unlike when he says she'ani ocheil mimaseh yadeikh — the language she'ani ocheil is primary according to the order of his words, for certainly it does not suffice otherwise; and likewise it does not suffice unless he says mimaseh yadeikh. If so, there are no contradictory languages — rather he means: from the work of your hands the prohibition shall be only what I eat — it follows the language she'ani ocheil remains in any case primary, as explained — correct:

And likewise Maharshal wrote thus, and wrote that nevertheless it is permitted since he said first leveiti — afterward in my life and in my death do not help, even though above shortly when the one who vows said leveitcha zeh we follow stringency, for there too are contradictory languages — I distinguish there, for the one who vows says thus, for it is possible he will never enter that house; but here the one who forbids says to Shimon leveiti she'ata nichnas — what does he care after it left his possession? Therefore it is permitted to enter after the death of the one who forbade, even though he says in my life, etc. And this reasoning Rivash wrote siman 357 — end quote. And I saw in Rivash there that he did not distinguish between one who vows and one who forbids except when he does not say in my life and in my death. And so it is in Ran page 46 in the matter of "he asked Avimei," etc. — and it is straightforward. But when he says in my life and in my death I did not know even the beginning of this matter, for there are no contradictory languages at all — for thus he says: that one which is my house now you shall not enter forever, even after my death. And it is very surprising — behold Tur and Shulchan Aruch wrote here shortly "one who says to his son konam she'ata neheneh li bechayai uvemosai" — he shall not inherit him, etc. And there he said first she'ata neheneh li, which is substance like leveiti — and nevertheless what he said afterward in my life, etc. helps. And likewise in the Gemara the question is raised: "my loaf upon you" and they gave it to him as a gift — "my loaf" he said to him, when it is in his possession it is forbidden; "upon you" he said to him, he made it hekdesh. And Beit Yosef brings it — behold that even though the language of ke'ari implies specifically in his possession, nevertheless "upon you" comes and forbids it. And all the more so if he afterward said in my life, etc. And it appears to me according to the words of Rosh and Tur it is in this manner: the reason he must say in my life and in my death even though he said konam this house that you enter is because a person does not put to heart to forbid his house after his death — for a person is not particular as a rule, only that he not see his enemy in his house. And therefore above when he said konam to this house that I enter — it is forbidden even if he dies, etc. — he does not need in my life and in my death, for it is obvious that one who forbids himself intends all his days, since he said this house — certainly he has some hatred for the house itself. Unlike here where he forbids his upon his fellow — even though he said zeh, his intention is that he not see his enemy benefit from his property; and after his death it is the way of the world to forgive all enemies — therefore he must say in my life and in my death. And according to this it appears to me further that even though Rosh and Tur required saying in my life, etc. — it is for the matter after death for the reason I wrote; but after sale it is certainly forbidden even without specifying after my sale, for it is one reason — that he not see his enemy benefit from this house. And this is clear in this: if for the matter of sale, what did he fix with the language in my life and in my death? Rather he should have said whether it will be in my hand or another's hand. And he told him that the language in my life implies this, for this language is needed — for if he only said in my death there would be permission in his lifetime immediately; rather certainly he needs no fix except for death but not for sale. And so it appears in siman 226 where Tur wrote: but if he said this courtyard it does not help to force him, for behold it is forbidden even after he sells. And according to this the challenges of Beit Yosef are resolved: the latter part where Tur wrote konam leveiti she'ata nichnas, etc. — deals without specifying, and he teaches that for the matter of sale it is permitted when he says leveiti, and he forbids only when he says leveit zeh; for regarding sale we do not care at all whether he says in my life and in my death. And incidentally regarding sale Tur also mentioned death — so it appears correct according to the Tur. But for halachah Shulchan Aruch already ruled that here one need not say in my life and in my death — and therefore he did not mention them, only that whoever says this house is forbidden even if he dies or sells it, for since he said zeh he forbade it forever:
