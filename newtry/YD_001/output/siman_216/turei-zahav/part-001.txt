<b>וכן אסור בגדולי גידוליהן. </b>כ"כ הר"ן הביאו ב"י דכמו דבסיפא בסעיף ב' באומר שאיני אוכל או טועם מהם אוסר אפילו בגידולי גידוליהן הכי נמי כאן ונראה דגם דעת הרא"ש כן שכתב בדף נ"ו שהגידולי גידוליהן אסורים בסיפא משום דהוה גוף האיסור כיון שאין זרעו כלה וא"כ גם באומר פירות האלו עלי מ"מ הרי נהנה מגוף האיסור וק"ו הוא דהרי בסיפא שאומר שאיני טועם דמותר בחילופיהן וגידוליהן בזרעו כלה אפ"ה אסור אפילו בגדולי גידוליהן באין זרעו כלה כל שכן ברישא דאסור בחלופיהן ובגידוליהן אפי' בזרעו כלה דיהיה אסור באין זרעו כלה אפילו בגידולי גידוליהן ומטע' זה לא הוצרך הרמב"ם והטור לכתוב בהדיא ברישא דאסור בגידולי גידולין באין זרעו כלה דסמכו על ק"ו שזכרנו וע"כ יפה פסק כאן בש"ע בדין זה דגידולי גידולין אסורין באין זרעו כלה ומו"ח ז"ל כתב דזו היא דעת הר"ן לחוד אבל הרמב"ם והרא"ש והטור סבירא להו ברישא דגידולי גידולין מותרין והוציא פסק זה מהלכה ותמוה הוא מטעם שזכרנו ואדרבה הרא"ש כתב להדיא דאפילו חליפי חליפין אסורין כ"ש גידולי גידולין כיון שהוא ממש בעין שהרי לא כלה זרעו ע"כ ודאי פסק ההוא ברור וכ' ב"י בשם הר"ן דדוקא כאן שהוא פורט ואומר פירות אלו שווינהו כהקדש בחילופין וגידולין וכן באומר פירות מקום פלוני עלי אבל באומר קונם זיתים וענבים עלי כמ"ש סוף סי' זה שלא נתכוין אלא מאכילת אותו מין ולא שוינהו כהקדש ומ"ה לא מיתסר בחילופיהן וגידוליהן ע"כ והך גידולין שכתב בסוף הוא שלא בדקדוק דהא על כל פנים אסור בגידוליהן דהא פירות אותו מין הם והוא אסר כל המין עליו אלא בחילופין לבד יש חילוק וכן כתב הרא"ש בהדיא שם במשנה דלא בעינן שיאמר האלו אלא אמר קונם לחוד אלא משום חילופין לחוד דאין אוסרין אלא באומר אלו ואפשר שהר"ן מיירי באומר קונם זיתים וענבים של שנה זו עלי דאז מותר בגידולים שיגדלו אחר כן כל שלא אמר אלו ובזה חילופים וגידולים שוין:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And he is likewise forbidden in the growths of their growths. </b>So wrote the Ran, as cited by the Beit Yosef. Just as in the latter clause, in seif 2, when he says, “that I will not eat” or “that I will not taste from them,” he forbids even the growths of their growths, so too here. It appears that this is also the view of the Rosh, for he wrote on 56 that in the latter clause the growths of their growths are forbidden because they are the very substance of the prohibition, since their seed does not disintegrate. If so, when he says, “These fruits are forbidden upon me,” he likewise benefits from the very substance of the prohibition. It is also a kal vachomer. In the latter clause, where he says, “that I will not taste,” he is permitted in their exchanges and growths when the seed disintegrates, yet even there he is forbidden in the growths of their growths when the seed does not disintegrate. All the more so in the first clause, where he is forbidden in their exchanges and growths even when the seed disintegrates, that when the seed does not disintegrate he should be forbidden even in the growths of their growths. For this reason, the Rambam and the Tur did not need to state explicitly in the first clause that the growths of their growths are forbidden when the seed does not disintegrate, for they relied upon the kal vachomer that we mentioned. The Shulchan Aruch therefore ruled correctly here that the growths of their growths are forbidden when the seed does not disintegrate. My revered father-in-law, of blessed memory, wrote that this is only the opinion of the Ran, whereas the Rambam, Rosh, and Tur hold that in the first clause the growths of their growths are permitted, and he removed this ruling from the halacha. This is astonishing for the reason we mentioned. On the contrary, the Rosh wrote explicitly that even the exchanges of the exchanges are forbidden, and all the more so the growths of the growths, since the actual substance remains present, for the seed did not disintegrate. This ruling is therefore certainly clear. The Beit Yosef wrote in the name of the Ran that this applies specifically here, where he specifies and says, “these fruits,” for he made them like hekdesh with respect to exchanges and growths. The same applies when one says, “The fruits of such-and-such place are forbidden upon me.” If, however, he says, “Olives and grapes are a konam upon me,” as written at the end of this siman, he intended only to forbid eating that species and did not make them like hekdesh. Therefore, their exchanges and growths are not forbidden, end quote. The word “growths” at the end is imprecise, for their growths are certainly forbidden, since they are fruits of that species and he forbade the entire species upon himself. The distinction applies only to exchanges. The Rosh likewise wrote explicitly there in his explanation of the Mishnah that it is unnecessary for him to say, “these,” and that merely saying “konam” suffices. Only with respect to exchanges do we require that he say, “these.” It is possible that the Ran is discussing one who says, “The olives and grapes of this year are a konam upon me.” In that case, so long as he did not say, “these,” he is permitted in growths that grow afterward, and in this respect exchanges and growths are equal.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And likewise, if he said, etc. </b>This is a question in the Gemara, whether he is forbidden in their exchanges. The issue is whether he can forbid upon another person something that has not yet come into existence, since he cannot forbid another person’s fruits upon that other person. This differs from when he forbids them upon himself, for he can even forbid another person’s fruits upon himself. Alternatively, perhaps with respect to exchanges and growths there is no distinction between himself and another person. We therefore rule stringently.

<b>אמר לאשתו קונם מעשה ידיך כו'. </b>כתב הר"ן קמ"ל רבותא דמייחד פירותיו של פלוני כי היכי דמייחד מעשה ידי אשתו כאומר האלו דמי ומיתסר בחילופים וגידולים וסיפא דשאני אוכל או טועם קמשמע לן רבותא דאפילו באומר פירות האלו שאני טועם הוה ליה כאילו לא אמר האלו ואזלינן בתר לשון שאני טועם דהא כאן הוי כאומר האלו כיון שמייחד כמו שכתבתי אלא דקשה לי כיון שיש כאן ב' לשונות הסותרים זה את זה דמלשון ממעשה ידיך הוה כאומר האלו דהא מטעם זה אסור בחילופיהן וגידוליהן וגם הטור ע"כ ס"ל כן דאל"כ ה"ל כאומר קונם זיתים וענבים דאין איסור בחילוף וגידול אלא ע"כ דהוי כאומר האלו כמ"ש הר"ן ומלשון שאני טועם משמע להיתר בחילוף ובגידול ויש לנו לילך לחומרא כמ"ש הטור אחר כך גבי לביתך זה בשלמא להר"ן לק"מ דס"ל לישנא בתרא עיקר בין לקולא בין לחומרא כמ"ש ב"י בשמו והר"ן סידר דין זה דלגבי אשתו שלא כסדר הטור אלא דנקט תחילה קונם מעשה ידיך ואח"כ שאני טועם ממילא יש לנו לילך אחר לישנא בתרא אבל להטור קשה ונ"ל דהטור ס"ל דאין שייך לומר דאזלינן לחומרא אלא במקום שאפשר להיות לשון אחד בלא חבירו דהיינו באומר ביתך זה דהיה אפשר לומר בית זה או להיפך לביתך לחוד ע"כ לא ידעינן הי מינייהו עיקר משא"כ באומר שאני אוכל ממעשה ידיך הוי לשון שאני אוכל עיקר לפי סידור דבריו דודאי לא סגי בלא"ה וכן לא סגי אם לא יאמר ממעשה ידיך א"כ אין כאן לישני דסתרי אלא ה"ק ממעשה ידיך לא יהא איסור אלא מה שאני אוכל נמצא דלשון שאני אוכל הוא נשאר עכ"פ עיקר כנ"ל נכון:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>He said to his wife, “The work of your hands is a konam,” etc. </b>The Ran wrote that this teaches a novelty. One who specifies the fruits of a particular person, just as he specifies the work of his wife’s hands, is considered as though he said, “these,” and is forbidden in their exchanges and growths. The latter clause, where he says, “that I will eat” or “that I will taste,” teaches that even if one says, “These fruits that I will taste,” it is regarded as though he did not say, “these,” and we follow the phrase, “that I will taste.” Here too, specifying the work of his wife’s hands is like saying, “these,” as I wrote. However, this is difficult for me, for there are two expressions that contradict one another. The phrase, “from the work of your hands,” is like saying, “these,” and for this reason he is forbidden in their exchanges and growths. The Tur must also hold this, for otherwise it would be like one who says, “Olives and grapes are a konam,” where exchanges and growths are not forbidden. It must therefore be like saying, “these,” as the Ran wrote. Yet the phrase, “that I will taste,” implies permission in exchanges and growths, and we should rule stringently, as the Tur writes afterward regarding, “this house of yours.” According to the Ran there is no difficulty, for he holds that the latter expression is primary, whether it produces leniency or stringency, as the Beit Yosef wrote in his name. The Ran also arranged this law regarding one’s wife in a different order from the Tur, stating first, “The work of your hands is a konam,” and afterward, “that I will taste.” We therefore follow the latter expression. According to the Tur, however, it is difficult. It appears to me that the Tur holds that we rule stringently only where either expression could have been stated independently, such as when he says, “this house of yours,” for he could have said, “this house,” or, conversely, only, “your house.” We therefore do not know which expression is primary. When, however, he says, “that I will eat from the work of your hands,” the phrase, “that I will eat,” is primary according to the structure of his words, for the statement cannot stand without it, just as it cannot stand without saying, “from the work of your hands.” There are therefore no contradictory expressions. Rather, he means that from the work of her hands only what he eats should be forbidden. It follows that the phrase, “that I will eat,” necessarily remains primary. This appears correct.

<b>אסור בו לעולם. </b>דיש לנו ספק אם ניזול בתר לישנא דלביתך ויהיה מותר במכירה ואסור בנפל או ניזול בתר זה ויהיה איפכא ואזלינן לחומרא ואסרינן בשניהם ויש שמתיר במת כו' דס"ל דלביתך עיקר דבתר לישנא קמא אזלינן ויש שאוסר בזו דס"ל לישנא בתרא דהוא זה עיקר:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>He is forbidden in it forever. </b>We are uncertain whether to follow the expression, “your house,” in which case it becomes permitted if sold but remains forbidden if it falls, or to follow the word, “this,” in which case the reverse is true. We therefore rule stringently and forbid it in both cases. There are those who permit it if the owner dies, for they hold that “your house” is primary and we follow the first expression. There are those who forbid it in that case, for they hold that the latter expression, “this,” is primary.

<b>Even though it left his possession, etc. </b>The Tur writes: “For example, if he specified, ‘during my lifetime and after my death, your entering my house is a konam,’ if he died or sold it to another, it is permitted,” end quote. This follows the Rosh, as the Beit Yosef cited. The Beit Yosef wondered about the latter clause, which permits it. If he did not specify, “during my lifetime and after my death,” then even if he said, “this house,” it is permitted according to the Rosh. If he did specify it, why should it be permitted? The Prishah cited the Maharshal that the latter clause concerns one who said, “during my lifetime and after my death.” My revered father-in-law, of blessed memory, likewise wrote this and explained that it is nevertheless permitted because he first said, “my house,” and the later phrase, “during my lifetime and after my death,” is ineffective. Although above, when the one who made the vow said, “this house of yours,” we ruled stringently because the two expressions contradict one another, that case is different. There, the one making the vow says it, and it is possible that he will never enter that house. Here, however, the one imposing the prohibition says to Shimon, “your entering my house is forbidden,” and what does he care once it has left his possession? It is therefore permitted after the death of the one who imposed the prohibition, even though he said, “during my lifetime,” etc. The Rivash wrote this reasoning in siman 357, end quote. I saw, however, that the Rivash there distinguished between one who vows and one who imposes a prohibition only where he did not say, “during my lifetime and after my death.” The same appears in the Ran on 46, s.v. “Avimi inquired,” and it is straightforward. Where he explicitly says, “during my lifetime and after my death,” I do not know even the beginning of a basis for this distinction. There are no contradictory expressions, for he means: “You shall never enter the house that is presently mine, even after my death.” It is extremely surprising, for the Tur and Shulchan Aruch write here shortly that one who says to his son, “Your benefiting from me is a konam during my lifetime and after my death,” does not inherit him. There too he first said, “your benefiting from me,” which is exactly like, “my house,” and nevertheless the later phrase, “during my lifetime,” etc., is effective. The Gemara likewise asks regarding one who says, “My loaf is forbidden upon you,” and then gives it to him as a gift. Did he say, “my loaf,” meaning that it is forbidden only while it is in his possession, or did he say, “upon you,” thereby making it like hekdesh? The Beit Yosef cites this. Thus, although the phrase, “my loaf,” indicates specifically while it remains in his possession, the phrase, “upon you,” nevertheless forbids it, and all the more so where he subsequently says, “during my lifetime,” etc. It appears to me that the words of the Rosh and Tur should be understood as follows. The reason he must say, “during my lifetime and after my death,” even though he said, “this house that you enter is a konam,” is that a person ordinarily does not consider forbidding his house after his death. Ordinarily he is particular only that he not see his enemy in his house. Therefore, above, where one says, “My entering this house is a konam,” it remains forbidden even if the owner dies, and he need not say, “during my lifetime and after my death.” It is obvious that one who forbids himself intends all the days of his life, and since he said, “this house,” he evidently has some aversion to the house itself. Here, however, where he forbids his property to another, even though he said, “this,” his intention is that he not see his enemy benefiting from his property. After death, it is the way of the world to forgive all enemies. He must therefore say, “during my lifetime and after my death.” According to this, it further appears that although the Rosh and Tur required him to say, “during my lifetime,” etc., this applies only with respect to after death, for the reason I wrote. After a sale, however, it is certainly forbidden even if he did not specify, “after I sell it,” for the same reason remains, namely, that he does not want to see his enemy benefit from this house. This is clear, for if the issue were also sale, what did he accomplish by saying, “during my lifetime and after my death”? He should instead have said, “whether it is in my possession or in another’s possession.” One cannot answer that “during my lifetime” indicates this, for that phrase is necessary to prevent immediate permission during his life if he said only, “after my death.” It is therefore clear that no qualification is needed except concerning death, but not sale. This is also implied in siman 226, where the Tur writes that if he said, “this courtyard,” coercion is ineffective because it remains forbidden even after he sells it. This resolves the Beit Yosef’s questions. The latter clause in the Tur, “your entering my house is a konam,” concerns one who did not specify, and it teaches that with respect to sale it is permitted when he says, “my house,” and forbidden only when he says, “this house.” Regarding sale, it makes no difference whether he says, “during my lifetime and after my death.” The Tur mentioned death incidentally together with sale. This appears correct according to the Tur. As practical halacha, however, the Shulchan Aruch already ruled that here one need not say, “during my lifetime and after my death.” It therefore did not mention those words, but ruled that whenever he says, “this house,” it remains forbidden even if he dies or sells it, for by saying, “this,” he forbade it forever.
