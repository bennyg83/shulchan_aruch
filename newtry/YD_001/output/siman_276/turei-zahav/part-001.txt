**** ENGLISH ****
<b>He must say that he is writing for the sanctity of the Name, etc. </b>The Tur writes here that he must think that he is writing for the sanctity of the Name. The Beit Yosef understood that the Rosh and Tur require verbalization and therefore wrote here that he must say it. In truth, according to the Rosh and Tur, thought suffices for the Divine Names after the scribe has verbally declared at the beginning that he is writing the Torah scroll for its sanctity. The Rosh states explicitly that verbalization is needed at the beginning of the writing, but at the time of writing the Names he must think of the sanctity of the Name. Nevertheless, the Shulchan Aruch ruled correctly that explicit speech is required even for each Name, because the Mordechai cites Rabbeinu Yaakov of Orleans, R' Elchanan, and Rashi that verbal articulation is required, and Rabbeinu Shimshon distinguishes between the rest of the scroll, where an ordinary intention to write a Torah scroll suffices, and the Names, where explicit verbal sanctification is required. His proof from one who intended to write Yehudah but omitted the dalet is sound. The Baraisa teaches specifically regarding the Name that he must pass the pen over it and sanctify it, showing that the Divine Names require a higher level of explicit intent than ordinary words. Therefore, although the Shulchan Aruch attributed this to the Tur, the practical ruling itself is correct: a Divine Name written without explicit verbal sanctification is invalid.

<b>כמקדיש בעלי מומין. </b>משמע דאם כתב שם במקום אחר בטעות דהיינו במקום שאין מקום השם שם וכתבו לשם קדושה הוה קודש וצריך גניזה דלא גרע משם הנכתב על ידות הכלים כמ"ש בסעיף י"ג והוא פשוט אבל שם שנכתב שלא בכוונה אין בו קדושה כמ"ש בהגמי"י פרק ו' דהלכות יסודי התורה בשם רא"ם:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Like one who consecrates blemished animals. </b>This implies that if a Divine Name was written by mistake in a place where no Name belongs, but was written with sacred intent, it remains sacred and requires genizah. It is no less than a Name written upon the handles of utensils. A Name written without intent, however, has no sanctity.

<b>כתב האזכרות כו'. </b>ה"ה שאר ס"ת כמ"ש בסי' רע"א סעיף ו' וגם בברייתא נקט אזכרות ונראה שנקט אזכרות לפי שהיו בימיהם שרצו לחלוק כבוד לשמות לזרוק עליהם זהב כדאיתא במסכת סופרים מעשה בתורתו של אלכסנדר שהיו אזכרותיה כתובות בזהב ואמרו חכמים יגנז ע"כ נקטו אזכרות ובאמת ה"ה שאר ס"ת והפוסקים נגררו אחר הבריית':
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>If he wrote the Divine Names, etc. </b>The same law applies to the rest of a Torah scroll, as stated in siman 271. The Baraisa mentioned Divine Names because in their times some wished to honor the Names by writing them in gold, as related in Maseches Sofrim concerning the Torah scroll of Alexander. In truth, writing any part of the Torah scroll in gold invalidates it.

<b>חוץ לדף. </b>פי' על הגליון חוץ לשיטה ובבית יוסף מביא בשם הגה' מיימון החדשות כתוב פירוש לא יכתב השם בסוף הדף שלא יהיה כתוב אחר בך עכ"ל וזה חומרא בעלמא ולהכי לא מביא כאן בש"ע אלא שלא יצא חוץ לדף והרבה סופרים עושים עצמם שרטוטים בדיו על הגליון אצל השם והוא טעות גדול ויש בו איסור וכמ"ש סימן רע"ד סעיף ז':
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Outside the column. </b>This means in the margin outside the written line. Some later glosses explain that the Divine Name should not be written at the end of a column so that no word appears after it, but this is only an added stringency. The essential law is that the Name must not extend outside the column. Scribes who draw ink guidelines in the margin beside a Name commit a serious error, as explained in siman 274.

<b>הנטפלות מאחריהם. </b>פי' מה שלפניהם מותר למחקו כגון ל' של לה' או ו' של וה' שאין בזה קדושה אבל הנטפל לאחריהם יש בו קדושה שכבר קדשו השם וזהו כאחרים בברייתא ולא כת"ק דאמר כ"ם של אלהיכם ונ"ו של אלהינו נמחק ונראה כיון שאין בזה אלא משום דבוק לקדוש' יכול לתלותו כגון אם שכח כ"ם של אלהיכם דאין בזה משום תולה מקצת השם דהא לא מן השם הוא זה דאינו שם אלא שנתפס עליו קדושת השם שלפניו ועל כן יכול לתלותו כנ"ל ואף מי שעולה על לבו להחמיר בזה לכתחלה מכל מקום בדיעבד פשיטא שאין לפוסלו כנ"ל:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>The letters attached after them. </b>Letters preceding a Divine Name, such as the lamed in “to Hashem” or the vav in “and Hashem,” may be erased because they have no sanctity. Letters attached after the Name possess sanctity because the Name sanctifies what follows it. Therefore, if the final letters of a form such as “your God” were omitted, they may be suspended above the line. This is not considered suspending part of the Divine Name itself, since those letters are not intrinsically part of the Name but merely acquire sanctity through attachment. Even one who is stringent initially should not invalidate the scroll after the fact.

<b>Eloah, Elohim. </b>The Tur also lists the form “Elohei,” and some Rambam texts do likewise. The yud in “Elohei” is part of the root and is sacred. A responsum in Masas Binyamin discusses a scribe who wrote “Elohenu” without the yud. Some wished to erase the final nun-vav and rewrite the yud and those letters, arguing that the incomplete Name had not yet sanctified what followed. Others required removal of the entire sheet. Masas Binyamin preferred suspending the yud, reasoning that it might not be a root letter and that suspension is only rabbinically restricted. In my opinion this is incorrect. The yud is not merely a sign of construct state. It indicates the plural form of sovereignty, as in “Adonei Yosef” and “Elohei hashamayim,” and is therefore an essential part of the Divine Name. The Yerushalmi's discussion of appended forms does not prove otherwise, for it refers to a differently vocalized form meaning “my God,” not to the plural construct “Elohei.” Nor may the nun-vav be erased. The preceding letters already form the sacred Name “Eloah,” and they sanctify meaningful letters attached to them even though the yud was accidentally omitted. The rule that letters following a Name may lack sanctity applies only where they form no meaningful attachment and are plainly an accidental addition. Accordingly, the preferable repair is to remove the entire sheet. Where that is very difficult, there is support from Maseches Sofrim and responsa for cutting out the mistaken Name and rewriting it. If the word “Eloah” was written without the vav in a place where our scrolls have it full, it likewise requires correction; one cannot dismiss the omission merely because the word is sometimes written defectively elsewhere.
