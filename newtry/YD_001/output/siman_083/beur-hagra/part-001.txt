<b>And if there is found, etc.</b> There, in accordance with Rav Huna and Rav Pappa, and what is stated there, "When was this taught," etc. Rosh writes that the same applies to a spine, since some kosher species resemble nonkosher ones, and the Gemara abbreviated the matter. This applies specifically where it is known that the fish has no scales. Ordinarily, however, we rely on the head and spine, because those exceptional species are uncommon. Rav Huna and Rav Nachman spoke in such a case. This resolves Ran's question in the name of Raavad concerning the incidents there, where they found no scales and conducted an additional examination. The Mechaber omitted this because Raavad and Raah, on 63a, explain "head and spine" to mean that one recognizes them as belonging to a particular kosher fish. Rav Huna said that only when the fish is whole may one rely on visual recognition of its head and spine, but not when it is cut and one recognizes only the head or spine. Rav Nachman said that even when it is cut, visual recognition of either one suffices. See Bedek HaBayit. Ramban and Rashba challenged this explanation, as discussed in Torat HaBayit. Nevertheless, Ramban writes there: Although Rashi's explanation is primary, I am astonished that I have not seen in the works of the Geonim that the head and spine serve as signs for fish, and the matter requires great investigation. Torat HaBayit HaKatzar likewise states that one should be stringent, since none of the Geonim or earlier authors wrote this. Beit Yosef writes that Rambam, in his Commentary to the Mishnah, also explained it like Raavad. In Bedek HaBayit he explains the objection, "Rav Ukva challenged," etc.: According to Rav Huna, a sign is needed because the fish is cut, but according to Rav Nachman the difficulty remains. The Gemara answers: with ara, etc.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Unless he was, etc.</b> In Rashi's name, "expert" means knowledgeable regarding them. Ramban, however, explains it like every "expert" in this discussion, meaning trustworthy and established as reliable. The Yerushalmi likewise groups them together: One may not purchase fish intestines and entrails except on the word of an expert, and one may not purchase tekhelet except on the word of an expert. If he says, "I salted them," even a suspected person or a non-Jew is believed, as stated in Chullin 63b: The Sages taught, etc. Raavad writes that only with one who is not an expert does "I salted them" suffice. With a non-Jew or a suspected person, all agree that he must say, "These are the fish and these are their entrails," as stated there in Chullin regarding one who says, "They are from a particular bird," etc. Here the ruling is stated without qualification, following Ramban and Rashba, who hold that the same applies to a non-Jew and a suspected Jew. Rambam has another approach, that only a Jew's statement helps, but a non-Jew's statement never suffices. <br><b>(Addendum) A person established as trustworthy.</b> Comparable to the "expert" discussed there regarding the blood of a menstruant woman and the blood of a wound. This also appears from the Yerushalmi: One may not purchase fish intestines and entrails except on the word of an expert, and one may not purchase tekhelet except on the word of an expert. This implies one comparable to the expert regarding tekhelet. The reason is that unlearned people were suspected of purchasing them from non-Jews under the presumption that they were kosher. Ran, unlike what appears from Rashi, who explains "expert" as one knowledgeable regarding them. See Rashi there, 63b, s.v. "These are fish," etc.
