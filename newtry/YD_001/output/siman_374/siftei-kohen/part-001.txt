**** ENGLISH ****
<b>That is, until after the first Shabbos.</b> Whether the relative is close or more distant. Bach writes that all the more so one may not attend a mitzvah meal, such as a brit milah meal, until Motzaei Shabbos. Responsa Masat Binyamin, siman 83, writes that customs differ regarding relatives who are not actually obligated in mourning but display some signs of it. Some places require this only of relatives who are disqualified as witnesses for the deceased, while others extend it even to more distant relatives such as a third relative in relation to a fourth. Where there is an established custom, it is followed. Where there is no custom, only those disqualified as witnesses should display mourning, in accordance with Terumat HaDeshen, siman 298. Even among relatives, however, distinctions remain. Those relatives who by strict law would have been obligated to mourn together with the primary mourner, under the principle "whomever you mourn, you mourn with him," should, although this is no longer practiced as full mourning, still show real mourning on the day of death or on the day they receive a recent report, until the end of the first Shabbos. They should not change from weekday clothes to Shabbos clothes, wash with hot water, shampoo the head except with cold or lukewarm water, or eat outside the home at either a mitzvah meal or social meal. A clean white shirt may be worn in honor of Shabbos, and they need not change their seat in the synagogue. After Motzaei Shabbos everything becomes permitted whether the relative is near or distant. More remote relatives who would not by strict law mourn together need not be so stringent about hot bathing, shampooing, or changing Shabbos clothing; it is enough to retain the outer weekday garment and not eat outside the home. Certainly in places that extend this custom even to relatives who remain valid witnesses, one should not impose the stronger restrictions. In my opinion, however, Masat Binyamin's leniency regarding hot washing and shampooing for relatives who do not mourn together does not fit Terumat HaDeshen or Rama, since they prohibit washing for relatives disqualified as witnesses, including second-degree relatives, even though those people are not obligated to mourn together. His stringency that closer relatives not change any weekday clothing into Shabbos clothing likewise does not appear from their words; rather, they change only some garments. In all these details one should follow local custom.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>One does not mourn for him.</b> This implies the case is one where we do not know that his months of gestation were complete. This is precisely Beit Yosef's objection to Hagahot Ashri: why did it require that the hair and nails were not fully developed? This is unlike Bach, who criticizes Beit Yosef and says that if the hair and nails were complete, mourning would certainly be required because the child survived until day thirty and thus we presume its months were complete. I do not understand Bach. Beit Yosef is presently discussing a child who died within thirty days, as is Hagahot Ashri itself: where it is unknown whether gestation was complete, the hair and nails were not fully developed, and the child died on day thirty, one does not mourn. It is strained to say day thirty is treated as after thirty, because regarding mourning we rule that day thirty is within thirty, as Rav Ashi concludes at the beginning of Perek Yesh Bechor, Bechorot 49a. Ramban and Tur explicitly hold that if the child dies on day thirty, even with developed hair and nails, one does not mourn where completion of gestation is unknown. Mechaber likewise rules so.
