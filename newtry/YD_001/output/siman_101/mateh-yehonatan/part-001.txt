(סימן ק"א בש"ע סעיף א') <b>חתיכה הראויה להתכבד דינה כבריה</b>. וכתב התה"ד דאם אחר שנתבשל לא הוי דבר חשוב רק קודם שנתבשל הוי דבר חשוב נמי לא בטיל ויש להביא ראיה לדבריו מפ' התערובות דמקשה תוס' מפ' וכי גרע שור גדול משאר חתיכות ה"ל ומפרשי' דקושית הגמרא הוא מחטאות המתות ואיכא בהו תורים שלא היה ראוי להתכבד ע"ש וע"כ צ"ל דס"ל כיון דמקודם היה ראוי להתכבד נמי ל"ב דהא מיירי שנתערב בקדשים וכבר כתבו תוס' דבקדשים ל"ש ראוי להתכבד דאין מחזיקין טובה זה לזה וא"כ אחר הביטול הא אינו ראוי להתכבד א"ו כיון דמקודם היה ראוי להתכבד נמי לא בטיל:
**** ENGLISH ****
(Siman 101 in the Shulchan Aruch, seif 1) A piece worthy of honor has the law of beriah. And Terumat HaDeshen wrote that if after it was cooked it is not an important item — only before it was cooked it is also an important item and is not nullified. And one can bring proof for his words from the chapter of mixtures, where Tosafot challenge from the chapter "And is a large ox worse than other pieces," etc., and they explain that the Gemara's challenge is from dying sin-offerings, and among them are birds that were not worthy of honor — see there. And therefore one must say that they hold that since from the outset it was worthy of honor, it is also not nullified; for it deals with kodashim, and Tosafot already wrote that regarding kodashim there is no worthiness of honor, for the priests do not hold favor for one another; and if so, after nullification it is not worthy of honor. Or: since from the outset it was worthy of honor, it is also not nullified.

(There in the hagahah) And even if it is forbidden only d'rabbanan it is not nullified. Rabbenu Yerucham proved this from the Rambam in Laws of Disqualified Offerings that chullin slaughtered in the Temple Court is forbidden only rabbinically; and in the second chapter of Avodah Zarah we learn that these forbid in any amount — chullin slaughtered in the Temple Court, etc. — from the reason that it is a piece worthy of honor for guests. If so, it is proven that even a d'rabbanan prohibition that is a piece worthy of honor is not nullified. And one might say that therefore the Rambam must hold that chullin slaughtered in the Temple Court is d'oraisa; for wherever it says "you shall not eat," benefit is also included, and proof is from nevelah where Scripture had to permit giving to a ger and selling to a non-Jew. The Gemara in Pesachim discusses whether we derive all prohibitions from "to the dog you shall throw it"; the Rambam rules that benefit is included from "you shall not eat," and in Laws of Idolatry rules like R' Yosi that only selling to a non-Jew is permitted. It appears the Rambam derives benefit from "that" (oto) and if so there is no source to forbid chullin in the azara — unless it is d'oraisa. After investigation, however, the Rambam may derive benefit from "you shall not give them a free gift" (lo techenam) regarding a non-Jew, not from "to the dog"; and the verse "sell to a non-Jew" establishes an extra positive prohibition. Thus the entire Pesachim discussion may be according to R' Yosi who learns from lo techenam, and there is no proof to all Torah prohibitions — we still need "to the dog." And since the Rambam did not cite that verse for chullin in the azara, he may hold it is d'oraisa. The Rosh in Eruvin explains the Mishnah of chullin in the azara as teaching that a piece worthy of honor is not nullified; if it were only rabbinic, the Mishnah of basar b'chalav would suffice. Thus the Rosh may hold like the Tosefta that it is not nullified, and that even benefit prohibitions are not nullified — unlike our conclusion that for eating prohibitions without benefit, even beriah and a piece worthy of honor are nullified according to Rif. Therefore one may be lenient regarding non-Jewish cheese when there is great loss, since it is a d'rabbanan eating prohibition. See the long discussion in the author's hagahah on siman 101 and siman 102.

(Seif 2) It does not have the status of a piece worthy of honor unless its prohibition is on account of itself, such as nevelah and basar b'chalav. One could say: how is it logical to say it is not nullified on account of a piece worthy of honor? For it can be established on this matter for the reason of each piece individually — that piece which has taste in it is forbidden and the rest are permitted. And one could answer that at first it absorbed taste from the milk, and afterward it fell into a pot that has sixty, and its taste was emitted — nevertheless that piece is forbidden, because we hold that if it is possible to squeeze it out it is forbidden; and the Mishnah that teaches "these forbid in any amount" regarding basar b'chalav comes according to the one who holds that if it is possible to squeeze it out it is forbidden. If so, Tosafot's challenge in the first chapter is from the chapter "And is a large ox worse," etc. — "and let it also teach nevelah, for it taught it there," etc. — and they answer: let it not also teach basar b'chalav, lest it teach us that if it is possible to squeeze it out it is forbidden. And it is impossible that it is not like the Mechaber in the first chapter, for if a piece fell into basar b'chalav there is a doubt whether it became forbidden, and it was mixed with nevelah or impure meat — it is impossible that it is not nullified also for benefit, or perhaps specifically for eating we say a piece worthy of honor is not nullified, but not for benefit. And it is impossible that for benefit too we say a piece worthy of honor is not nullified. One could say it deals with meat of a wild animal mixed with all of them where there is taste of milk, and therefore there is no way to establish the matter through tasting; and we properly say it is not nullified on account of a piece worthy of honor, even though nevertheless meat of a wild animal is also forbidden d'rabbanan — from where do we know for benefit? But the Mechaber in the first chapter decided the matter that we do not say a piece worthy of honor for benefit; therefore there is no reason to say it deals in such a case, and one must say as Terumat HaDeshen wrote, that even though it can be established on this matter, nevertheless we say it is nullified and tasting is not required; and if so, he properly said that in basar b'chalav it is a piece worthy of honor that is not nullified — meaning that one must taste whether there is taste in it or not. [Hagahah of the great Rav, etc., Mohar R. Shlomo HaKohen, may his light shine.]

(There in seif 2 in the hagahah) And even a piece that was not salted is nullified, for its prohibition is not on account of itself, etc. Sha'ar Dura in chapter 40 wrote that a nevelah piece mixed with kosher pieces, none of which were salted, is not nullified if it was worthy of honor, even though it lacks preparation — after nullification it still needs salting; nevertheless lacking preparation is not like a chicken in its feathers. An incident came before Rabbeinu Tam who ruled thus; Hagahot ShaDa concludes, but I saw great authorities who say even unsalted nevelah is nullified. All this is because they hold it must be worthy of honor before Israelites specifically; if we say when nullified it would become worthy, they argue about unsalted pieces. The Mechaber in Darkei Moshe seif 1 implies one need not require worthiness before Israelites — even worthiness before a non-Jew suffices; unsalted nevelah not deveined that was mixed remains significant if it was worthy of honor. Peri Megadim and Noda BiYehudah discuss whether deveining is a great preparation like feathers; the Mechaber may disagree with Ran who requires worthiness before everyone.
