**** ENGLISH ****
Seif 1 in Shulchan Aruch 196 — they consider this day be-di'avad; see Maharatz Chajes s.k. 4 resolving Shach's question siman 375 why regarding mourning that day does not count. In my humble opinion I would answer: here if we are stringent to consider it night, sometimes there is also a leniency — as explained s.k. 89, a woman establishes a veset on the second day and need not be concerned further until she uproots it twice; all ketamim she attributes leniently; similarly if she uproots twice she need not be concerned. It is clear it must be specifically in one onah — either day or night. If a woman sees on the second day at night on the twentieth of the month, and on the third saw on a festival while the day is still extended after the congregation prayed Ma'ariv — if we consider it night, this woman establishes her veset on the tenth and need not fear any other veset. Similarly if she establishes a veset by day and uproots it on the second day at night, and on the third saw on another day after Ma'ariv prayer — if we consider it night, it is uprooting and she is permitted even at that time she was accustomed to before, for the uprooting is as law, and this would be a great leniency; therefore they consider it day, for in truth the day is still extended — unlike mourning where we are stringent because no leniency emerges; understand well. One may reject: regarding mourning too there is leniency for distant hearing — if it is night it would be after thirty days.

Seif 5 there in the gloss — some say during the first three days: I am amazed at this law itself — is it not clear in the Gemara that if she examined herself on the second day of seeing, found no blood, and did not examine again until the eighth day and found herself tamei, we do not declare her tamei retroactively, for we establish her in a presumption of purity — yet she was not presumed for three days to stop? Learn that with one examination on the first day when she found nothing, she was presumed. Moreover, you would hold her in a presumption of seeing — for which Rabbi holds she is not pure, attributing to one who keeps day against day on the second day, for they are comparable; Rashi explained she too is in a presumption of purity. Difficulty: she was not presumed to stop for three days! Consider: the pure woman can attribute ketem to a wound and one who keeps day against day cannot attribute — how are they comparable? She is in such a presumption of impurity that I attribute to her more than a wound — how did the Mechaber write she attributes to a wound yet cannot attribute for Rabbi? Even Rashbag who disagrees does so from the reason we attribute corruption to the corrupted, not from the above reason. It would seem Mordechai and Sefer ha-Terumah discuss one who saw during three days, therefore she needs three days she did not see to remove the presumption; but if she saw only two days she need not — the hefsek suffices; however his language does not imply this. Also if she examined herself on the fourth day, why do we also hold her in presumption of purity? The Gemara implies she invalidates in presumption of purity on the fourth day from the third and second. Therefore it must be as I wrote siman 50: presumption from live birth does not apply since there is presumption of prohibition from the womb — even though permitted by slaughter we do not say presumption; similarly here. She is tamei in any case; and although this would permit in this counting, nevertheless there is no presumption; thus all ketamim should be stringent — we only say since blood does not commonly come from a woman we attribute ketam; but one not presumed for three days, ketamim are still common in her and we attribute to common things — see Shach siman 53. Therefore Rama Chalav wrote well: a wound discharging blood is more common than blood from the source — she is permitted; and this is without sensation, a rabbinic doubt leniently, since both are common — the later authorities were unnecessarily stringent regarding her wound discharging blood, for the above law requires great analysis.
