<b>Because they are suspected of bestiality.</b> A gentile is prohibited in this, as Scripture says, "and they shall become one flesh," excluding animals and beasts whose flesh does not become one with his. There is therefore a prohibition of lifnei iver, placing a stumbling block before the blind. This is stated at the beginning of Perek Ein Ma'amidin. It implies that the only concern is lifnei iver. If the animal was already left with the gentile and the prohibition was violated, there is no resulting prohibition upon eating the animal, even if it is a kosher species. Otherwise, the Gemara should have taught that a kosher animal becomes forbidden after the fact. Rather, the meat remains permitted even if a gentile certainly committed bestiality with it, and the same applies if a Jew did so. The reason is that Bava Kamma 96 teaches regarding an animal with which a transgression was committed that not all oxen are destined for the altar, as Rashi explains there. Thus the animal is disqualified from the altar but remains permitted to ordinary use. At first glance one might bring proof to prohibit from Tosafot, Zevachim 71a, s.v. berove'a, which says that according to Rashi an animal known to have committed or undergone such an act by one witness or by admission of the owners is prohibited to the altar but not to ordinary consumption, whereas with two witnesses it would be prohibited even to ordinary use, and this also appears in Perek HaIsh Mekadesh. Temurah 28 likewise says that testimony of two witnesses disqualifies it from eating. In truth, those sources prove the opposite. The reason it becomes prohibited for eating when there are two witnesses is, as Rashi explains in Temurah, because the animal is then liable to stoning. That applies only after its judgment has been concluded. Rashi in Kiddushin 56 likewise says an animal that committed or underwent such an act is prohibited to ordinary use only after the judgment is concluded. The Gemara in Bava Kamma 41 states explicitly: from "the ox shall surely be stoned" do we not already know it is a carcass and a carcass is prohibited to eat? Why then does Scripture add, "its flesh shall not be eaten"? To teach that if it was slaughtered after its judgment was concluded, its meat is prohibited. It follows expressly that if slaughtered before conclusion of judgment, it is permitted. Accordingly, the Temurah passage and Tosafot in Zevachim refer to after the animal was sentenced to stoning. Nowadays, therefore, there is no prohibition upon eating it even if a Jew committed bestiality with it in the presence of two witnesses, since there is no judicial conclusion sentencing the animal. I saw someone rule the meat prohibited in such a case, and that ruling is mistaken.

<b>In a place where the custom is to go to the bathhouse, and so forth.</b> Rama follows Mordechai at the beginning of Perek Kol HaBasar. Mordechai writes that Rabban Gamliel bathed with a governor, from which it follows that if the Jew entered the bathhouse first and the gentile entered afterward, the Jew may continue bathing with him. But if the gentile entered first, the Jew may not bathe with him, as stated in Pirkei DeRabbi Eliezer. If the Jew distances himself four cubits, it is permitted, as we see regarding excrement, from which one distances four cubits and then prays; we do not say the entire house is considered four cubits. Rama was troubled that we are not careful about this nowadays and answered in Darkei Moshe that in their time gentiles went without undergarments and their nakedness was exposed, while nowadays they cover themselves. He therefore codified the law here this way. I am very surprised. His wording implies that if the Jew entered first, he may bathe with a gentile even when the gentile's nakedness is exposed. It is obvious that this is forbidden, because there is concern the Jew will see him and come to improper thoughts. Pesachim 51 says that two brothers may bathe together, and Rashi explains that we are not concerned that seeing one another naked will lead to homosexual conduct. From there it is clear that where there is actual concern for improper thoughts, such as exposed nakedness, there is a complete prohibition and the Jew must leave. This is unlike Rama's gloss. Although Agudah in Perek Makom Shenahagu writes that in the days of the Sages they bathed without undergarments and that nowadays, when people cover their nakedness, one may bathe even with his father, and Rama cites this in Even HaEzer siman 23, nevertheless they certainly refrained from staring at one another. Nowadays, when people may look at one another, it is plainly forbidden to bathe with anyone whose nakedness is uncovered, even another Jew. As for Rama's question why we do not observe Mordechai's restriction, I am surprised in the opposite direction. I examined Pirkei DeRabbi Eliezer, chapter 29, and it says that Avraham circumcised his servants so that they would not render him impure. If this were a continuing prohibition for us, we should prohibit even touching an uncircumcised gentile, and certainly eating and drinking with him at one table. Rather, Pirkei DeRabbi Eliezer speaks only within the framework of purity and impurity, and Mordechai likewise. We, who do not presently observe those purity laws, have no such bathhouse prohibition. Bathing together is permitted in every circumstance provided the nakedness is covered. <i data-commentator="Nekudot HaKesef" data-label="♯" data-order="1"></i> If it is uncovered, the Jew must leave even if he entered first. This appears entirely clear to me. My father-in-law also concludes against Rama's gloss, but says that because bathhouse bathing was formally prohibited by a vote when purity laws were observed, another formal vote would be required to permit it. According to his logic, even touching would have been prohibited by that vote, because Pirkei DeRabbi Eliezer places them together. Rather, it is clear there was no independent prohibition to begin with; it was only a distancing measure connected to impurity and the pursuit of purity.
