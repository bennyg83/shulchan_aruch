In his Commentary to the Mishnah, at the end of chapter 10, Rambam writes twice; but the Rosh at the end of Yoma agrees with the Tur, writing, “Nevertheless, it appears,” etc., “and therefore,” etc. <br><b><small>(Supplement)</small> Three times.</b> As stated at the end of the chapter Four Death Penalties: “The vow is released,” etc. Although the formula there is stated only twice, that is not exact, for two repetitions alone never have independent significance. A formula of only two repetitions is never found. <small>(End.)</small>

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Some say that they return, etc.; and some say, etc.</b> See Mordechai at the end of chapter 3 of Shevuot, who discusses this at length. In brief, Maharam writes that Nedarim chapter 3 concludes that one may open with regret, and the entire discussion there implies that simple regret suffices, as in “Do you still have the same resolve?” This is unlike Rashi in chapter HaDar, who requires an opening based on a circumstance. Likewise, the passage says that one who presents an opening says, “Had you known,” etc. Tosafot, Eruvin 64b, s.v. “they open,” cite three forms of regret: a wholly new circumstance, with which one may not open; a circumstance already existing but unknown, with which all agree one may open; and a circumstance not yet present but regularly anticipated, concerning which there is a dispute. The question, “Is your heart still upon you?” after an opening was found, teaches the court to caution the petitioner not to lie. The language implies that the circumstance arose only after the vow. <small>(End.)</small>
