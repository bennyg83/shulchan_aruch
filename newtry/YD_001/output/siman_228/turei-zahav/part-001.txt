<b>והאידנא אין מומחה. </b>משמע שאפילו בדיעבד לא הוה היתר ומה שמסיים להתיר ביחיד בטור כתוב במקום ביחיד בפחות משלשה נראה כונתו למעוטי שנים דלא כמו שמצינו בדין אליבא דשמואל דשנים שדנו דיניהם דין הכא אינו כן דאפילו דיעבד לא מתכשר אפילו בשנים דלא עדיפי ממומחה בזמן הזה:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Nowadays there is no expert. </b>This implies that even after the fact, release by one person is not valid. Where the Tur concludes that release may be performed by one person, its text reads, “by fewer than three,” rather than, “by one.” It appears that its intent is to exclude two. This differs from monetary law according to Shmuel, who holds that if two judged, their judgment is valid. Here, even after the fact, two are insufficient, for they are no better than an expert nowadays.

<b>בכל לשון שיאמר. </b>פי' אפילו בלשון לעז מחילה והתרה שענינם לעקור הנדר מעיקרא וסיים רמב"ם בזה אבל אם אמר מופר לך או נעקרה שבועתך וכיוצא בענין זה לא אמר כלום שאין מפיר אלא הבעל והאב אבל החכם אינו אומר אלא ל' התרה ומחילה ע"כ. והביא ב"י בשם רמב"ן דל' התרה ומחילה הוא כמתיר את הקשר שחוזר לענין שהיה כאדם שמתיר חבל קשור משא"כ בל' הפרה שהוא כדבר שישנו אלא שיש מעכב מלהשלימו כמו מפר מחשבו' ערומים כו' ע"כ פי' דבריו שמצד הנודר הוא קיים אלא שהמתיר שאומר בלשון הפרה כוונתו שאינו רוצה שיקויים הנדר ואין כח זה אלא לבעל עם אשתו:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>In whatever expression he uses. </b>This means that even in a foreign language he may use an expression of pardon or release whose meaning is to uproot the vow retroactively. The Rambam concludes: If, however, he says, “It is annulled for you,” or, “Your oath is uprooted,” or a similar expression, he has said nothing, for only a husband or father annuls. A sage uses only language of release or pardon. The Beit Yosef cites the Ramban that language of release and pardon is like untying a knot, returning the matter to its prior state, like one untying a bound rope. Language of annulment, however, refers to something that exists but is prevented from reaching completion, as in, “He frustrates the plans of the cunning.” Thus, from the perspective of the one who vowed, the vow remains in existence, but the person who says language of annulment means that he does not wish it to be fulfilled. Only a husband regarding his wife has that power.

<b>הלכך צריך למצוא לו פתח. </b>פי' שצריך שיהיה באותו פתח סברא חזקה שבשבילה היה נמנע מלידור אע"פ דניחא ליה בנדרו שעשה בעת ההיא לולי הפתח הזה והוקשה לו על זה כיון דתלוי הדבר בפתח וכיון שאנו יודעים דעדיין ניחא ליה בנדרו מתחלה היאך נאמין לו בשלמא בחרטה הרי עכ"פ אנו רואין שהוא מתחרט ואין אנו רואים שום סברא שלא יתחרט מעיקרא משא"כ ע"י פתח ע"ז תירץ וכל שהוא משקר אין התרתו כלום ואנו מודיעים לו כן ע"כ ודאי יזהר מלשקר וכיון שענין הפתח הוא דבר שצ"ע וכמו שאמרנו שצריך סברא חזקה ע"כ יהיה המתיר מיושב אבל מענין הנודר עצמו שמתירין לו לא אכפת לן בעמידתו או ישיבתו דהא אינו כמו דין כמו שזכרנו כן נ"ל ביאור הדברים שזכרנו:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Therefore, one must find an opening for him. </b>This means that the opening must contain a strong reason on account of which he would have refrained from making the vow, even though he was satisfied with the vow when he made it were it not for this opening. A difficulty was raised: Since everything depends on the opening, and we know that he was initially satisfied with his vow, how can we believe him? Regarding regret, at least we see that he presently regrets it, and we see no reason to assume that he did not regret it from the outset. With an opening, however, that is not so. The answer is that if he lies, the release is entirely ineffective, and we inform him of this. He will therefore certainly be careful not to lie. Since the matter of an opening requires careful consideration and a strong rationale, the person releasing him should be seated. The posture of the person seeking release is irrelevant, for this is not equivalent to a judicial proceeding, as explained.

<b>אסור להשאיל על נדרו לחכם כו'. </b>בירושלמי פרק השותפין איתא כן והטור מביאו ומסיים אסר הנאת בני עירו עליו נשאל לחכם שבעיר אית תנא תנא אפילו בקדמייתא נשאל לחכם שבעיר שאינו כמפר נדרי עצמו ומשמע מזה דת"ק דאוסר טעמו דהוה כמיפר נדרי עצמו וכ"כ ב"י בשם ריב"ש בסימן ת"ו והב"י הקשה עליו דשאני מיפר נדרי עצמו דכתיב לא יחל דברו ולא קשה מידי דהא לא קאמר דהוה באמת מפר נדרי עצמו אלא כמיפר בכ"ף הדמיון ופירושו שכיון שהחכם שבעיר נהנה מהיתר זה דאחר ההיתר יהיה מותר ליהנות מזה הוה כנוגע בדבר ויש חשש שלא יעיין יפה דהא כבר למדנו שצריך להיות מיושב בדבר שצ"ע הכי נמי כן הוא והוה ממש כמו בדיני ממונות שכל שיש לדיין צד נגיעה לא ידון אותו הדין הכי נמי כאן במילתא זו הוה כדין וא"ל מן רבי עקיבא שהתיר לכלבא שבוע חמיו הנדר בפ' אע"פ תירץ ריב' דהוא לא התיר בעצמו אלא ע"י צירוף אחרים עמו אלא דמ"מ בדיעבד מותר דלא הוה נדר כדין ממש כמו שכתבנו בסמוך. <i data-commentator="Nekudot HaKesef" data-label="♯" data-order="1"></i>ובמה שסיימו הירושלמי והטור אסר הנאת בני עירו נשאל לחכם דלשם הקשה ב"י דהא נהנה מהחכם שבעיר שמתיר לו הנדר ומש"ה פי' איפכא כמבואר בדבריו אבל הוא דוחק וא"צ לכך דלק"מ לפי ע"ד דמ"ש מסימן רכ"א סעיף ב' דהנאסר לומד מן האוסר הלכות ואגדות ואמאי לא אסרינן ליה דע"י זה שיוכל לידע לימוד הלכות מהו מותר ימשוך לו הנאה בדבר שהיה סבר תחילה שהוא אסור אלא פשוט דזה לא מחשיב הנאה שמורה לו איסור והיתר והכא נמי הוה כן אלא דב"י הביא בסמוך דברי תשובת רשב"א בנשבע שלא יהיה גבאי כו' וכמ"ש בסעיף שאחר זה דאסור וזה נראה שחולק על הירושלמי והטור שהביאו:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>It is forbidden to ask the sage concerning his vow, etc. </b>The Yerushalmi in the chapter “The partners” states this, and the Tur cites it. It concludes that if he forbade himself from benefiting from the townspeople, he may ask the sage of that town. One Tanna taught that even in the first case he may ask the town’s sage, because this is not truly like annulling one’s own vows. It is implied that the first Tanna forbids it because it resembles annulling one’s own vows. The Beit Yosef cites the Rivash, siman 406, accordingly. The Beit Yosef objected that annulling one’s own vow is different because the verse states, “He shall not profane his word.” There is no difficulty, for the comparison is not exact. Since the town’s sage benefits from the release, because after the release he may benefit from the person, he is an interested party and there is concern that he will not examine the matter adequately. We have already learned that release requires careful consideration. It is therefore like monetary law, where a judge with a personal interest may not judge. Rabbi Akiva’s release of the vow of his father-in-law, Ben Kalba Savua, is no objection, for the Rivash answers that he did not release it alone but joined with others. Nevertheless, after the fact the release is valid, since it is not literally a judicial proceeding. Regarding the Yerushalmi’s conclusion that one who forbade himself from the townspeople may ask the town’s sage, the Beit Yosef asked that he benefits from the sage’s release and therefore explained it in the opposite manner. This is forced and unnecessary. It is no different from siman 221, seif 2, where one prohibited from benefit may learn halachos and aggados from the person who imposed the prohibition. We do not forbid that because the ruling may enable him to benefit from something he had thought forbidden. Teaching him what is forbidden and permitted is not considered benefit. The same applies here. However, the Beit Yosef shortly afterward cites a responsum of the Rashba regarding one who swore not to serve as a communal collector, as discussed in the next seif, and that appears to disagree with the Yerushalmi and Tur.

<b>שלא יהיה גבאי בו'. </b>בב"י בשם רשב"א מבואר שדבר זה הוא הנאה כיון שמינוי זה הוא פסול לעובד כוכבים וגרים נחשב לו לשררה ובסעיף הקודם כתבנו מזה. <i data-commentator="Nekudot HaKesef" data-label="♯" data-order="2"></i>אבל באמת צריך עיון להבין דבר זה דבבית יוסף העתיק זה בשם הרשב"א דבמה שאסר עצמו הגבאות אוסר בזה הנאה זו לו ולבני עירו וסיים וכיון שהוא נהנה אין נשאל לחכם שבעיר וקשה הא אף על גב דאסר עצמו מהם בהנאה פרטית דהיינו הגבאות מכל מקום שאר הנאות לא אסר על עצמו הא זה דומה לאוסר עצמו מיין של בני עירו וכי יהיה אסור להיות נשאל לחכם שבעיר על זה הכי נמי ממש כן הוא ואי נימא דאין נשאל להחכם שבעיר מצד שיש בזה הנדר איסור הנאה להם והוה נוגע בדבר ההיתר וכמ"ש אני למעלה בפירוש דברי הטור דקשה על זה הא בבית יוסף לא סבירא ליה הך סברא ותו דלמה סיים הרשב"א וכיון שהוא נהנה הוי ליה למימר וכיון שהם נהנים ולפי הנראה לע"ד דטעות סופר יש בדברי הרשב"א וצריך להיות וכיון שהם נהנים כו' וצ"ע. גם צריך עיון דלפי זה היה הרב שבעיר אסור להתיר שום נדר על כל מינויי הקהל באם נשבע אחד שלא ירצה לקבלו וכמדומה שאין נוהגין כן ועיין מה שכתבתי בסימן זה סעיף ל"ג:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>That he should not serve as a communal collector, etc. </b>The Beit Yosef cites the Rashba that this appointment is considered a benefit, because the position is disqualified for a non-Jew and for converts and is therefore regarded as an office of authority. I wrote regarding this in the previous seif. In truth, however, this requires study. The Beit Yosef copied from the Rashba that by forbidding himself from the office, he forbade this benefit to himself and to the townspeople, and concluded that since he benefits, he may not seek release from the town’s sage. This is difficult. Although he forbade himself from one particular benefit, namely, the office, he did not forbid all other benefits. Is this different from one who forbids himself from the wine of the townspeople? Would he thereby be forbidden to seek release from the town’s sage? If the concern is that the town’s sage benefits from the release and is therefore interested, that is the explanation I gave above, but the Beit Yosef rejected that reasoning. Furthermore, the Rashba should have said, “Since they benefit,” not, “Since he benefits.” It appears that there is a scribal error and the text should read, “Since they benefit.” This still requires study. According to this, a town rabbi would be forbidden to release any vow concerning a communal appointment when one swore not to accept it, yet it appears that the custom is not so. See what I wrote below in seif 33.

<b>פתח וגם חרטה. </b>זהו שהביא ב"י בשם המרדכי בשם רבינו יוסף בכור שור שכתב במה שאמרו פרק ד' דנדרים ההוא דאתא לקמיה דר' אסי אמר ליה כדו תהית כלומר אתה מתחרט על נדריך א"ל לא פי' לשון בתמיה ושרייא דיש גדולים סוברים שלא אמר לו רק הך מילתא לחוד ומש"ה נהגו כשמתירין נדר אין צ"ל אלא מתחרט אתה והוא אומר כן ומתירין לו ומקשה שם על זה דהא רב אסי ס"ל אין פותחין בחרטה ע"כ פי' דכבר אמר ליה אותו שבא לשאול על נדרו הפתח וטעם החרטה אלא שאח"כ שאלו כדו תהית כלומר הזהר שלא תשקר ובודאי אתה מתחרט ע"כ כתב שם שאין די בחרטה לחוד אלא צריך לחזור אחר טעם החרטה וכתב שם במרדכי בשם מהר"מ שאף על פי שרבותיו נהגו להתיר בחרטה לחוד וכן הוא אחריהם מ"מ אחר כך חזר בו לנהוג דוקא כרבי יוסף שלא להתיר שום נדר בלא פתח וחרטה ע"כ. וכיון שמהר"ם החמיר מי יקל בדבר ומה שמביא רמ"א כאן נהגו להחמיר לעשות מהחרטה פתח הוא נכון מצד שאין לחוש שמא עכשיו הוא מתחרט אבל לא מתחרט מעיקרא ע"כ מהני פתח שאומר שאילו יודע דבר זה לא היה נודר כלל אבל לא יצאנו בזה מהחומרא דרבינו יוסף דלעיל שצריך דוקא לידע טעם החזרה וכל הרוצה לעשות מן המובחר יעשה כן:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>An opening and also regret. </b>The Beit Yosef cites from the Mordechai in the name of Rabbeinu Yosef Bechor Shor regarding the incident in the fourth chapter of Nedarim where a person came before R’ Assi, who asked him, “Do you now regret it?” He answered, “No,” according to one explanation as a question, and R’ Assi released him. Some great authorities understood that R’ Assi asked only this and therefore practiced that when releasing a vow one need ask only, “Do you regret it?” and when he answers yes they release him. The Mordechai objects that R’ Assi holds that one may not open with regret. It therefore explains that the person had already stated the opening and reason for his regret, and only afterward R’ Assi asked, “Do you truly regret it?” meaning, be careful not to lie. The Mordechai therefore writes that regret alone is insufficient and one must investigate the reason for the regret. It cites Maharam that although his teachers practiced release through regret alone, and he initially followed them, he later retracted and practiced like Rabbeinu Yosef, never releasing a vow without both an opening and regret. Since Maharam was stringent, who may be lenient? The Rama’s statement that the custom is to be stringent by turning the regret into an opening is correct, because otherwise perhaps he regrets only now but did not regret from the outset. An opening is effective when he says that had he known this matter he would never have vowed. Nevertheless, this does not fully satisfy Rabbeinu Yosef’s stringency, which requires knowing the reason for his change of heart. Whoever seeks to act in the best manner should do so.

<b>But one does not open with the honor of Heaven, etc. </b>The Tur writes that one should not say, “Had you known that you were committing a transgression through your vows, would you have vowed?” The Beit Yosef asked why this differs from the opening clause concerning violation of “You shall not take revenge,” and therefore altered the wording here to, “that you were treating the honor of the King lightly.” This correction is unnecessary. **** ENGLISH ****
<b>For a person is not so brazen, etc. </b>It appears to me that since the concern is that this is not truly in his heart, this opening is ineffective when others propose it to him, for he is ashamed to answer truthfully. If, however, a person comes on his own and says, “Please release my vow, because I have now learned that making a vow, or failing to release it, involves a transgression and is like building a private altar,” this is a valid opening. There is no concern that he is too embarrassed to say that he would not have refrained, since he could have remained silent altogether. The wording of the Gemara and poskim is precise: “We do not open with the honor of Heaven,” meaning that we do not propose it, but he may raise it himself. The same applies to the ruling in seif 12 that we open only with a foreseeable occurrence. The Rambam writes explicitly in chapter 6 of Hilchos Shevuos: “We do not open with an unforeseen development,” but concludes, “If he himself regretted because of the new development and changed his mind, they may release him.” The Rama likewise writes in seif 13, “But if he regrets from the outset,” etc. This is the same principle.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>For example, if he forbade them from his property, etc. </b>The Gemara explains that since he was brazen once, he will be brazen again. Since he already had the audacity to forbid his father from his property, if he does not truly regret it he will also have the audacity at the time of release to say that he would not have refrained because of his father’s honor. Therefore, if he says that he regrets, we may assume the regret is genuine. The reason resembles what I wrote in seif 10. Concerning a common transgression, we do not say that he is ashamed to admit that he would have vowed despite knowing the prohibition. Here, although casting off the obligation to support one’s parents is uncommon, even without an explicit Torah command reason itself dictates honoring one’s father, as the nations do, and everyone regards failure to do so as sinful. Since this person already broke through the universal standard of conduct, he will not now be ashamed to say that even had he known he was obligated to support them he would have vowed. If he says otherwise, he truly regrets it. My revered father-in-law distinguished that “You shall not take revenge” and honoring parents are explicit in the Torah, and since he had the audacity to transgress an explicit Torah command he will also now speak without shame. His words are difficult, for if he already knew the prohibitions, what opening exists in saying, “Had you known,” etc.?

<b>ונעשה אותו פלוני תלמיד חכם. </b>התוספות פרק אף על פי הקשו הא ר"ע התיר לבן כלבא שבוע נדרו על ידי שאמר אדעתא דגברא רבא מי נדרת והא אין פותחין בנולד ותירצו כיון שהלך רבי עקיבא לבי רב ודרך הוא בהולך לבי רב ללמוד שנעשה אדם גדול ע"כ. ותירוץ זה דוחק דמשמע בסוגיא לכאורה דבן כלבא שבוע לא ידע בשעת נדרו שר' עקיבא הלך לבי רב אבל מדברי הסמ"ג ותשובה להרמב"ן שמביא ב"י יש תירוץ אחר שם שהם מפרשים הא דאין פותחים בנולד פותחין הוא דאין פותחין אבל פותחין בחרטה מחמת נולד וכן הביא רמ"א בסעיף י"ג משום הכי לא קשה מן ר"ע דשם היה חרטה גמורה על הנדר מעיקרא לבן כלבא שבוע מחמת הנולד שנעשה רבי עקיבא תלמיד חכם ובפירוש אמר כן שמתחרט על הנדר מעיקרא מחמת כן שלא אסרו בנולד אלא אם אחרים פותחין לו בזה כדי שיתחרט מחמת הנולד והוא אינו אומר כלום שמתחרט מעיקרא רק שאומר על דעת זה לא נדרתי דבזה אמרינן שאין מתחרט אלא מן הנולד ואילך ולא מעיקרא והוא הדין אם אחר אמירה של אחרים אומר הוא בפירוש שמתחרט מעצמו שעשה נדר מתחילה מועיל וכן מבואר מדברי רמב"ם שמביא ב"י כאן שכתב אבל אם ניחם מעצמו כו' אע"פ שכתב ב"י שמדברי ר' יוסף בכור שור דלעיל לא משמע כן מ"מ יפה פסק רמ"א כאלו הגדולים שהם רבים אבל לע"ד נראה דגם בכור שור מודה בזה כיון שאומר בפירוש שעוקר הנדר מעיקר' ומתחרט מעצמו:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And that person became a Torah scholar. </b>Tosafos in the chapter “Even though” asked how R’ Akiva released Ben Kalba Savua’s vow by asking, “Had you known he would become a great man, would you have vowed?” We do not open with an unforeseen development. Tosafos answered that since R’ Akiva had gone to the study hall, and it is common for one who goes to study to become great, it was foreseeable. This answer is strained, for the sugya appears to imply that Ben Kalba Savua did not know when he vowed that R’ Akiva had gone to study. The Semag and the responsum of the Ramban cited by the Beit Yosef offer another answer. “We do not open with an unforeseen development” means only that others may not propose it as an opening. If he himself regrets from the outset because of the later development, the vow may be released. The Rama cites this in seif 13. There was therefore no difficulty from R’ Akiva, for Ben Kalba Savua completely regretted the original vow once R’ Akiva became a Torah scholar and explicitly said that he regretted it from the outset. The prohibition applies only when others tell him of the new circumstance and he merely says, “I did not vow with that in mind,” without explicitly saying that he now regrets the original vow. In such a case we say that he regrets only from the time of the new event onward. If, however, after hearing it from others he explicitly says that he now regrets having made the vow initially, it is effective. This is also clear from the Rambam cited by the Beit Yosef. Although the Beit Yosef thought Rabbeinu Yosef Bechor Shor disagreed, the Rama correctly ruled like the many great authorities. In my view, Bechor Shor also agrees when he explicitly uproots the vow from its origin and regrets it on his own.

מדבריהם מאחר שב"י ורמ"א כתבו סתמא כוותייהו ונראה משום דרוב הנדרים שבאים לפני החכם להתיר הן שנדר להתענות או שלא לאכול בשר וכיוצא בו לסגף נפשו ידוע מסתמא שעשה להצדיק נפשו ומש"ה אין רגילין לשאול על סבתן בלבו באותה שעה בכל נדרים שבעולם דבזה אין שום מכשול בעולם אף אם לא נדע טעמו כיון דלא תלה נדרו בפירוש בשום דבר כן נראה לי ברור:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>He must specify to them the vow and its cause. </b>Rashi explains that a widow may have vowed to the orphans, “All fruits in the world shall be forbidden to me if I derived any benefit from my kesubah.” If she later comes merely to release her prohibition of fruits and omits the condition, a stumbling block may result. If she states the entire vow, the sage will not release it. From this we learn that one must state before the sage every element of the vow, for perhaps if he states it fully the sage will refuse to release it. The Gemara also gives the reason of a matter involving prohibition, meaning that he may have forbidden all fruits upon himself if he transgresses a habitual sin. If he omits that condition when seeking release and mentions only the fruit prohibition, a stumbling block may result. The Yerushalmi relates that a man came before R’ Yudan and said, “Release my vow.” He asked, “What did you vow?” The man said, “I vowed that I would not profit.” R’ Yudan asked, “Do people act that way?” He answered, “I meant that I would not gamble.” R’ Yudan said, “Blessed is He Who chose the words of the Sages, who said that one must specify the vow, for had you not specified it I would have released it.” The Beit Yosef inferred that it is insufficient to state the words of the vow; one must also explain why he made it. The Rashba likewise wrote that he must specify the cause. It is clear that two matters require caution. First, he may not omit a condition from the vow. Second, even where there was no condition, he may not substitute different wording, as did the gambler who used a cleaner expression so the sage would not understand the matter. This is the Shulchan Aruch’s intent. “Specify the vow” means not to change its language. “And the cause” means not to omit what he stated when vowing, such as, “if I commit such-and-such a transgression.” If he states the vow exactly as made, without alteration or omission, neither the Gemara nor the poskim indicate that the sage must investigate what private reason motivated him. On the contrary, the Yerushalmi proves otherwise. Had R’ Yudan not found the expression strange, he would not have asked further. Tosafos in Arachin 23b states explicitly that one need specify only the language of the vow, but not why he vowed. The Perishah was astonished that even careful rabbis do not investigate the cause and suggested distinctions. According to what I have written, they were never required to ask about an unexpressed reason. They need only ensure that the condition was not shortened and that the wording was not altered. If he states the vow exactly, there is no need to ask what was in his heart.

No prohibition exists until Shavuos, so he may seek release whenever he wishes before then. It is preferable to release it immediately before Shavuos and again on Shavuos itself. According to the Shulchan Aruch’s ruling here that release should occur twice, the same procedure should be followed there, once before Shabbos and again on Shabbos. The same is proper for Shavuos.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Unless he informs him. </b>The Yerushalmi gives the reason of suspicion, meaning that the other person should not suspect him of treating his vow lightly. Another opinion gives the reason of shame, meaning that he should be ashamed before the person for whose benefit he vowed and whom he now asks to release him. The Rivash explains that the practical difference is as follows. According to shame, when the vow was not for the other person’s benefit but was merely made in his presence, it may be released outside his presence. According to suspicion, even then there is concern unless he is informed. According to shame, if the vow was for his benefit it may not be released outside his presence even if he is informed. According to suspicion, informing him suffices. All agree that if the vow was not for his benefit but only made in his presence, it may be released outside his presence provided he is informed, and in his presence even against his will. Thus, when the person who administered the oath benefits from it, his consent is required, which follows the reason of shame. When he derives no benefit, consent is unnecessary and mere knowledge suffices, following the reason of suspicion. Therefore, the phrase here, “unless he informs him,” means that he must consent. The later phrase, “they may release it without his knowledge,” means without his consent, but he must still be informed because of suspicion. Although the Rama presents this later as an alternative opinion, it appears that the Shulchan Aruch itself intended this, for its language follows the Tur, and the Beit Yosef explains that in the first case the Tur requires actual consent.

It resembles one who swore against a rabbinic mitzvah, whose oath takes effect, as ruled in siman 239, seif 6. The Rivash likewise discusses one who swore not to serve as one of the trustees selected by the community for tax matters, after the community had placed them under cherem. An individual may not exempt himself from communal taxation. If he swore beforehand, he is forbidden to sit with them until he seeks release. If no opening can be found, the community’s selection is void because it selected five and now has only four. This appears to conflict with the Mordechai in the name of Rashi, who wrote that one who swears not to obey a forthcoming communal enactment has sworn in vain, because he swore to nullify a mitzvah and is already sworn from Sinai to obey communal regulations. That implies the oath does not take effect at all. The distinction is as follows. If the communal enactment safeguards against transgression, the individual’s oath does not take effect, because the Torah grants the community authority to erect fences around prohibitions, as it states, “You shall guard My safeguards.” If, however, the enactment concerns financial benefit, taxes, or appointments, the oath takes effect, and the communal regulation cannot uproot it without release. Accordingly, when the Rama writes that one who swore not to reveal information must nevertheless testify when placed under cherem, no release is needed if he initially swore to violate the obligation to testify, for he was already obligated by the Torah. Indeed, when formally summoned to testify he must testify even without a cherem, as otherwise he violates, “If he does not testify.” The case must therefore be one where he was not formally summoned. In siman 239, seif 7, the Rama writes similarly concerning one who swore not to reveal information that would save another from loss and states that release is required. There, the obligation is not as absolute as formal testimony, and therefore he was stringent to require release.

<b>And there are those who are stringent. </b>The Rama wrote this based on a responsum of the Rosh, klal 9, siman 4, concerning a community that enacted under cherem that every document must be written only by the official scribe. They later enacted under cherem that two appointed men should write each document and the scribe should write only the final line. The Rosh wrote that a community that enacted a cherem and later wishes to enact a different regulation may do so, provided it first releases the earlier cherem. If it did not mention release, it acted improperly and placed a stumbling block before the community. Nevertheless, documents written under the second enactment are not invalid, and the second enactment stands. This does not resemble Rava’s rule that whenever the Torah says not to do something, if one did it the act is ineffective. That rule concerns mitzvos that cannot be abolished. Here, where the enactment can be abolished, Rava agrees that the act is effective. The Rama understood that the Rosh was stringent and upheld the second cherem, and therefore wrote here, “There are those who are stringent.” In truth, this is very difficult. The second cherem did not take effect at all, as the Rosh himself wrote in that klal, ruling 6. The Rosh referred only to documents made under the second enactment and said that the enactment itself stands, but not its cherem. They had authority to alter the rule, and although they acted in violation of the first cherem, their act remains effective. It resembles one who swore not to sell and then sold, where the sale is valid, as written in Choshen Mishpat, siman 208, unlike the Rama’s ruling later in siman 230. However, a responsum of the Rosh, klal 5, ruling 4, concerns a community that enacted under cherem that no synagogue seat could be sold except with the owner’s consent, not by beit din for a debt. Later they enacted under cherem that beit din could sell seats of anyone who failed to pay an assessment. Many seats were sold under the second enactment, and objections were later raised based on the first. The Rosh wrote that if the second enactment released the first cherem, the sales stand, but if it did not, the sales are void. We must say that when the Rosh wrote the responsum in klal 5 he held that the sale was void because of the rule in Temurah, as the Hagahos Mordechai cited by the Rama in siman 230 likewise holds. In klal 9, however, he retracted and held that this case differs from Rava’s rule, as he wrote explicitly. In Choshen Mishpat, siman 208, I wrote what appears primary. See there. <b>With the permission of one of them. </b>This is from a responsum of the Rashba cited by the Beit Yosef. The Rashba derives it from the chapter “One who borrows,” 94b, according to R’ Yonasan, whose opinion we follow. The verse, “He cursed his father and his mother,” implies both together and also each one separately, unless the Torah explicitly states “together,” as it did concerning an ox and donkey. So too, when he was placed under oath, the intent was that he not lend except with the permission of both or of either one. This is difficult. Since the wording bears two meanings, how do we know that the person administering the oath intended the lenient meaning, that permission of one suffices? We rule in siman 208 that unspecified vows are interpreted stringently. All the more so should we follow the simpler meaning, both together. Rashi likewise explains on the verse, “And Hashem struck every firstborn,” that the conjunction is additive, as when one says, “so-and-so and so-and-so.” The simple implication is conjunction. We also find that the Tur and other poskim write regarding a vow made according to many, “according to so-and-so and so-and-so.” Why do we not say that it means either one and is not according to many? Mahari’s writings, siman 73, imply that where one says “or so-and-so” and then uses collective language, both are required. Where no collective language is used, the obligor might still claim that he bound himself only to what both would say, but not that “and” automatically means “or.” Kiddushin likewise teaches, “Be betrothed to me with this and this and this,” and we do not say that it means either item. It therefore appears that the Rashba did not permit lending with the consent of one merely because the wording can mean “or.” Rather, he teaches that this is at least a possible meaning, with a practical consequence if one of them dies. Then permission of the survivor suffices, because the father never intended the prohibition to last forever if one died. A similar rationale appears in a responsum of the Ramban, siman 249, cited by the Rama in seif 20, that when the person according to whose understanding the vow was made dies, his successors stand in his place if the matter was for their benefit, because the one who vowed did not intend eternal prohibition. Here, if there is no benefit to them, the deceased’s successors are irrelevant. The Rama correctly placed this ruling in siman 216, seif 7, where he wrote that one of them died. Although he explained that whenever “so-and-so and so-and-so” is stated it can mean either one, he means only that such an interpretation is possible. Therefore, after one dies, we may interpret it as “or.” This is the intent of the Rama’s cross-reference here.

<b>Since he was obligated, etc. </b>I do not understand the Rama’s view. The responsum attributed to the Ramban, siman 259, states that one who swore to pay by a specified time, or to bring his daughter to marry a certain man on a specified day, and failed to do so, may remain obligated afterward and violate the oath each day. These are two matters: first, he swore to perform the act without making it dependent upon time; second, he accepted an additional obligation to perform it by a specified time. If, however, he swore to do the act on a particular day and that day passed, the oath no longer obligates him, though he is liable for having violated it. The responsum proves this from one who vowed to fast. If he accepted an unspecified day and ate, he must fast another day. If he accepted this specific day, he merely completes that day. The Rama distinguishes the two clauses by saying that in the first he was already obligated independently, unlike the second. This distinction is exceedingly difficult, for it is nowhere implied. The first clause also speaks of one who swore to perform an act. The Shulchan Aruch’s wording in the latter clause, “the act,” clearly refers to the same act mentioned in the first. If the Rama’s distinction were correct, the proof from fasting would be irrelevant. Moreover, the Shulchan Aruch writes, “There is no further obligation through the oath,” implying that some obligation may remain apart from the oath. The responsum attributed to the Ramban is in fact a responsum of the Rashba, as the Beit Yosef explains in his introduction, and the Rashba states explicitly that the first case creates two distinct obligations: he swore to perform the act and separately fixed a time. Maharam Padua, siman 72, explains that when the vow and time are stated together, they form one matter. Thus, if he first swore to perform an act and then added that it should be completed by a certain time, the oath on the act remains after the time. If he said in one statement, “I will do this act on such-and-such day,” the oath ends when the day passes. The Rosh disagrees regarding payment of an existing debt. Even when he says in one statement, “I swear that I will pay you on such-and-such day,” the oath primarily concerns payment, while the time merely urges prompt payment. The Rosh himself distinguishes this from one who swore to eat a loaf today and did not, for there he had no prior obligation to eat the loaf. Thus, even the Rosh is stringent only where an independent obligation already exists. Regarding something not otherwise obligatory, he agrees with the Rashba. As practical halacha, we should rule stringently like the Rosh concerning an existing obligation, that the oath continues after the time. Where no prior obligation exists, we distinguish between a separate oath to perform the act followed by a time limit, and a single oath to perform it on a specified day. Maharam Padua therefore ruled concerning one who vowed during distress to travel to Eretz Yisrael in Iyar, but war arose then, that after Iyar there was no continuing oath penalty, though he was stringent to require release. This is the proper practice.
