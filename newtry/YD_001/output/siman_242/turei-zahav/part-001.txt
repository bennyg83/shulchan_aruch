**** ENGLISH ****
<b>More than for his father. </b>The Tur concluded that his father brings him to the life of this world and his teacher brings him to the life of the World to Come, and there is no honor greater than the honor of one's teacher and no awe like awe of him. The Sages said: "The awe of your teacher is like the awe of Heaven." The Perishah asked: if so, what does "more than his father" mean? For regarding his father too, the Tur wrote at the beginning of siman 240 that his honor and awe are like the honor and awe of God! He answered that every analogy is not fully equal to its source; but "the awe of your teacher is like the awe of Heaven" was derived from "es Hashem Elokecha tira" — to include one's teacher. Since both types of awe are written in one verse, it tells us they are completely equal. Thus far his words. But this is not compelling. For certainly whatever is analogized to something else has no distinction at all from the source. The Tur did not intend here to prove that the awe of one's teacher is greater than the awe of one's father — for that was already proven by the fact that the teacher brings him to the life of the World to Come. Rather, the difficulty here was regarding the statement that there is no awe like the awe of one's teacher — but the opposite appears to be true, since the awe of one's father was analogized to the awe of God! This would undermine the preeminence already stated regarding the teacher. He answered that regarding the teacher too we find this, as it says "the awe of your teacher," etc. Thus the preeminence of the teacher remains intact, based on what was already stated — that he brings him to the life of the World to Come.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And one who takes leave of his teacher and receives permission, etc. </b>At first glance, the source of this ruling appears unclear. It is based on the Ran’s explanation of the Rif at the beginning of Moed Katan. Scripture states, “On the eighth day he sent the people away.” The Ran explains that the eighth day of the festival was the 22nd of the month, and they received Solomon’s permission because they intended to depart on the 23rd. Scripture then states that on the 23rd of the seventh month he sent the people away. From here we learn that a student who takes leave of his teacher but then remains overnight in the same city must take leave a second time. The Ran writes that because they remained there that day and lodged in the city, they needed to obtain permission again for their departure on the 24th. However, on the 23rd, although they had lodged the night after the 22nd, they did not need to obtain permission again, because when they received permission they intended to depart the next day, the 23rd. There is plainly a scribal error in the Rif, for the verse and Gemara state the 23rd, not the 24th. Nevertheless, the Ran’s words are correct. His difficulty was why they needed permission twice. The permission on the 22nd was for departure on the 23rd, since they could not depart on the 22nd, which was Yom Tov. They later changed their minds on the 23rd and decided to depart on the 24th, and therefore needed permission again on the 23rd for the journey of the 24th. Thus, the Gemara correctly derives that one who remains overnight in the city must take leave again. Yet the lodging after the 22nd did not itself require new permission, since they had originally intended to depart on the 23rd. Only when they decided on the 23rd to remain another night did they require permission again for the 24th. Everything is therefore properly resolved, with God’s help.

Tosafos in Shevuos 36 wrote that the positive commandment to honor Torah may perhaps not apply to the wife of a Torah scholar after his death. Maharam Mintz, however, wrote that if the wife of a Torah scholar marries an ignoramus, one is no longer obligated to honor her as before. This implies that while she remains the widow of a Torah scholar, one is obligated to honor her as before. I also found the following in the words of my revered father-in-law, of blessed memory: When a person’s teacher is mentioned before him as though he were merely his colleague, he need not object and say, “He is not my colleague but my teacher.” The proof is from the beginning of Beitzah, where they said to R’ Eliezer, “Your colleagues have already voted concerning you.” The Gemara asks, “Who are your colleagues?” and answers, “R’ Yochanan ben Zakkai,” who was his teacher. <b>And even if the first authority permitted it and the ruling has already taken effect, etc. </b>The second authority may not issue a contrary ruling even to be stringent. This applies specifically when the first lenient ruling has already taken effect before the second authority disagreed. If it has not yet taken effect, the second authority may disagree and rule stringently. The Rama’s wording is imprecise.
