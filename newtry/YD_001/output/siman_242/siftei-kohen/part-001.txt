<b>אביו כו'. </b>ז"ל ד"מ ולרבו יותר כו' יש ללמוד מזו דמי שאביו הוא רבו קורא אותו רבי ולא אבא מורי וכן מוכח ר"פ הזהב דקרי ליה ר"ש לרבי אביו ר' ונראה לי דהיינו דוקא אם הוא רבו מובהק כו' עכ"ל וצריך עיון שאין נוהגין כן הלכך יש לומר דאע"ג דחייב בכבוד רבו יותר משל אביו מכל מקום יותר יש לקרותו בשם אב המיוחד לו מילדותו וגם מסתמא האב מוחל על כבודו בזה כמו שנתבאר בא"ח סימן תע"ב סעיף ה' דבן אצל אביו אע"פ שהוא רבו מובהק צריך הסיבה אע"ג דתלמיד אצל רבו א"צ הסיבה משום דמסתמא אביו מוחל על כבוד רבו בזה וה"ה הכא ורבי שאני דהא אמרינן מימות משה עד רבי לא מצינו תורה וגדולה במקום אחד:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>His father, etc.</b> The Darkei Moshe writes: From the ruling that one's teacher takes precedence, one may learn that if his father is also his teacher, he should call him 'Rabbi' rather than 'my father and master.' This is also indicated at the beginning of the chapter HaZahav, where Rabbi Shimon called Rabbi, his father, 'Rabbi.' It appears to me that this applies only where his father is his primary teacher. This requires study, since the practice is not so. Rather, although one must honor his teacher more than his father, it is more fitting to call him by the title 'father,' which has been unique to him since childhood. Presumably the father also waives the honor due him as a teacher in this regard, as explained in Orach Chaim, siman 472, seif 5: a son reclining in his father's presence must recline even if his father is his primary teacher, although a student need not recline in his teacher's presence, because the father is presumed to waive his honor as a teacher. The same applies here. Rabbi was exceptional, for the Sages said that from the days of Moshe until Rabbi, Torah and greatness were not found together in one person.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Anyone who establishes a study hall for himself.</b> The Kesef Mishneh writes that this means specifically where he establishes himself to issue halachic rulings. The Bach, however, does not appear to understand it so, for he writes that by establishing a study hall he challenges his teacher's authority. Proof is that even examining a slaughtering knife, which is not a halachic ruling at all, was forbidden in the Talmudic era because of one's teacher's honor. See Responsa Maharik of Krakow, beginning of siman 19.

He writes there that even if one had been his student, if he later became equally learned he may dispute him and rule in his presence, even in practical halachah, as Reish Lakish disputed Rabbi Yochanan. [לא] מקרי רבו וכמ"ש והרי הריב"ש כתב שם דא"צ נטילת רשות רק מרבו הא' וכע"ש וגם הרב בד"מ בשמו אלמא דיש לו הרבה רבותיו מובהקים אלא ודאי כדפירש' וכן נראה דעת המחבר דלקמן סעיף ל' כתב דכל שאין רבו מובהק דהיינו שאין רוב חכמתו ממנו אין חייבים באלו הדברים וכאן כתב אע"פ שנטל רשות מרב א' לא סגי עד שיטול רשות מכל רבותיו המובהקים אלא ודאי כדפי' ודו"ק:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And the term 'primary teachers,' etc., means, etc.</b> The Rama's words appear to mean that according to this view, even if one did not learn most of his wisdom from a teacher, so long as he has not grown to be near his teacher's level he is called his student. Yet in my humble opinion this is incorrect. The Rambam and Maharik do not disagree. Maharik himself writes at the beginning of the responsum that only a primary student, meaning one who learned most of his wisdom from his teacher, is bound by all these laws. One who did not learn most of his wisdom from him owes only the limited honor of standing before him and rending garments for him. Maharik proves from the Rambam that the prohibitions against establishing a study hall, ruling in one's teacher's presence, disputing him, and similar matters apply only to a primary teacher. Even according to those who rule like Rabbi Yosi, that illuminating even one mishnah creates a teacher-student bond, this applies only to standing and rending garments, not to all the other laws. Maharik's later ruling creates a separate principle: even where one learned most of his wisdom from his teacher, if he later became equally learned he is a colleague-student. He proves this from Reish Lakish, who was a full student of Rabbi Yochanan and was ordained by him, yet later disputed and ruled before him. The Darkei Moshe and the Rivash likewise support this. But where one did not learn most of his wisdom from a teacher, he is not called his full student even if he never reached the teacher's level. The Rivash explicitly defines a colleague-student as one who did not learn most of his wisdom from him. The objection that one cannot have several primary teachers is unfounded, for Rashi explains that one may have learned most of his Scripture from one teacher, most of his Mishnah from another, most of his Gemara from another, and similarly Midrash, Aggadah, Kabbalah, and other areas of Torah. Thus one may indeed have several primary teachers. This also accords with the Mechaber below, seif 30, who defines a non-primary teacher as one from whom he did not learn most of his wisdom, while here requiring permission from all his primary teachers.

<b>Some write, etc.</b> The Hagahot Derishah questions this from Eruvin 62b, where Rabbi Yaakov bar Abba asked about ruling in one's teacher's locale even concerning Megillat Taanit, a written and established text. Rav Yosef replied that Rav Chisda asked him even about eggs with kutach throughout Rav Huna's lifetime and did not rule. Rashi explains that no halachic work was written in their days except Megillat Taanit. This implies that even matters written in a book may not be ruled in one's teacher's presence, and even by a colleague-student. One answer is that only directly in his presence is forbidden, whereas outside his presence it is permitted. Another is that Megillat Taanit involved a matter novel to the questioner, whereas Maharam permits a written ruling only where it appears non-novel. Yet this cannot be correct, for Maharam explicitly discusses a matter novel to the questioner and permits reliance on an explicit ruling in the works of the Geonim. The first answer is also difficult, because the Gemara says 'in his locale,' which indicates even outside his presence. It therefore appears that Maharam understood the case of Megillat Taanit as requiring comparison of one case to another, rather than merely reading an explicit ruling.

Their view should therefore be treated stringently.

<b>אבל אם אביו שוכר לו רבי כו'. </b>לשון ספר חסידים ס"ס תקפ"ה אבידת רבו קודם לאבידת אביו דזהו כשאין אביו משכיר לבנו רב אבל כשאביו משכיר לבנו רב והרב לא היה מלמדו בחנם אז אבדת אביו קודמת ואם הרב לא היה רוצה ללמדו אלא בשכירות ויהודי או יהודים נותנים לו השכירות ללמדו אז אבידת בעל הנותן קודם ע"כ:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>But if his father hires a teacher for him, etc.</b> Sefer Chasidim writes that a teacher's lost object precedes the father's only where the father did not hire the teacher. If the father paid the teacher, and the teacher would not have taught without payment, the father's lost object takes precedence. If another Jew or group of Jews paid the teacher to teach him, the lost object of the payer takes precedence.
