**** ENGLISH ****
<b>It is permitted to sell it in this manner.</b> This follows Tur according to Rosh. Rosh explains that prohibited benefit from the sale proceeds occurs where one sells the firstborn donkey to someone who does not know its status and receives payment for its full value. But he may receive the amount by which it is worth more than a small lamb, because the buyer will still need to spend the lamb's value for redemption. Tur understands the core leniency as requiring deduction of the lamb's value. One might ask why the initial prohibition is described as depending on failure to inform the buyer, since even if he informs him, receiving the full value should still be prohibited. The answer is that ordinarily once the buyer is informed he will not pay the full value, knowing he must still provide a lamb. If he nevertheless chooses to pay full value, Tur would still prohibit the seller from accepting it. Beit Yosef, however, explains Rosh as making everything depend on notification. Once the buyer knows the animal is a firstborn donkey, the seller may accept the full price even without deducting the lamb's value, because either the animal is especially dear to the buyer or the excess is treated as a gift. Rama appears to follow Tur. In my opinion, once the seller informs the buyer, both parties necessarily intend the sale only with respect to the permitted value remaining after redemption. Whatever price they agree upon is the agreed value of that permitted interest, even if the market would usually be lower. This resembles the law of household goods where there is no ordinary claim of ona'ah because an owner may sell his possessions only at a high price. The prohibition exists only if the seller expressly states that the price is for the entire animal and that the buyer need not provide a lamb.
