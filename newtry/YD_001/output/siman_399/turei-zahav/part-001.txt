<b>And not merely because it is a festival, and so forth.</b> This is Ramban's position. He proves it from the Gemara's question whether a festival counts toward the thirty days. Regarding the seven days the Gemara does not ask, because the festival plainly cannot count as seven when the core practices of shivah, such as removing shoes and overturning the bed, are not observed. Regarding thirty, however, the question arises because thirty-day restrictions are practiced during the festival. Rashi explains that pressing garments and haircutting are prohibited on the festival as well. Ramban asks: some shivah restrictions also remain during the festival, such as refraining from laundering even with water and receiving consolers. Why then should the seven days not count? He concludes that for the thirty-day count, the full character of the thirty-day mourning must remain, including restrictions that would not otherwise arise merely because of the festival. This is why he rules here that the mourner's restrictions on pressed garments and haircutting remain in force as mourning restrictions themselves, not only as festival prohibitions. Tur understands Rosh differently: even if the mourner is not subject to any additional restriction beyond what the festival itself prohibits, the festival can still count toward thirty because those same prohibited acts are actually being observed. Beit Yosef rules like Ramban because his reasoning is persuasive.

Responsa R. Binyamin disputes Shulchan Aruch and argues that Ramban's proof is weak. He says the essential markers of shivah are the classic practices of head covering, removal of shoes, and similar matters. Laundering in plain water is not one of the principal markers. By contrast, the essential thirty-day restrictions are precisely pressing and haircutting, which remain prohibited during the festival. He further asks who says a mourner buried during a festival may not launder clothing in plain water. In my opinion these objections are unconvincing. The Gemara's list concerning Shabbos does not establish that only those items are principal mourning practices. Laundering simply was not relevant in that particular discussion. The Yerushalmi expressly treats laundering as a mourning restriction. If partial observance were enough for thirty, partial observance should likewise be enough for seven. As for laundering during the festival, it is certainly prohibited as private mourning, and all agree private mourning restrictions remain. Rosh disagrees only about the added public restrictions of haircutting and pressed clothing. Ramban classifies even those as sufficiently private because refraining from wearing a pressed garment does not necessarily publicize mourning, and likewise some haircut situations are not obvious. His reasoning is sound and Beit Yosef ruled correctly.

<br><b>I also saw</b> in that responsum a case of a groom who married on Erev Yom Tov and whose relative died during the festival. Ordinarily, if a groom suffers a bereavement during his seven wedding days, he begins the thirty-day count only after the wedding week, because the wedding celebration is stronger than an ordinary festival and he is allowed pressing and haircutting. In this case, however, the wedding week fell within the festival, so he was in any event prohibited from those acts because of Yom Tov. The responsum therefore rules that according to Ramban the days should not count because the groom is personally exempt from the extra mourning prohibition, while according to Rosh they should count because he is in fact refraining from pressing and haircutting. I find this very surprising. Even according to Rosh, the festival counts only where there is a genuine mourning obligation and the same acts happen also to be forbidden because of the festival. Then the mourner can say he is observing them for mourning as well. A groom during his wedding week has no mourning obligation at all. His abstention comes solely from the festival. How can those days count toward mourning? This would be like a groom whose wedding week happens to coincide with a circumstance making laundering impossible or with a personal oath not to cut his hair. Surely that cannot count toward thirty. Therefore, the rule that a festival counts toward thirty is a specific novelty and should not be extended to wedding days. The groom's wedding week does not count toward the thirty days, according to everyone.

<b>We do not say that Shemini Atzeret cancels it.</b> One might have thought that because Shemini Atzeret is an independent festival, it should cancel mourning just as a festival cancels mourning when some mourning was observed before it. Here too some mourning-related restrictions such as pressing and haircutting were observed during Sukkot. The ruling teaches that Shemini Atzeret has no such independent cancellation power unless the seven-day mourning had already ended before the festival. Hagahot Maimoniyot adds that Shemini Atzeret likewise does not count as seven separate days toward thirty where the burial occurred during the festival. It counts only as one day, unless burial preceded the festival. Tosafot and Mordechai rule similarly, unlike R. Yosef, who holds that Shemini Atzeret, Shavuot, and Yom Kippur each count as seven even if the death and burial occur on that very day.

Maharshal, responsum 5, disagrees and says that even where the death occurs during the festival, Shemini Atzeret counts as seven toward thirty. His proof is only from Tur's wording that Shemini Atzeret does not "cancel," implying that while it does not cancel all remaining mourning, it still has seven-day significance. This is no proof. Tur uses the language of cancellation merely because the preceding law concerned a festival canceling mourning. Rambam also implies the opposite of Maharshal. He writes that one who buries during the festival counts thirty from the day of burial, which implies that even if burial occurs at the end of a festival, the remaining festival days count only as ordinary calendar days, not as seven. The Gemara likewise asks simply whether festival days count toward thirty, meaning whether each day counts as an ordinary day. It never suggests that a festival day itself counts as seven.

R. Zerachyah HaLevi gives further support. Regarding someone who did not hear of a relative's death until after the festival, he says the festival and Shemini Atzeret do not each count as seven to transform the report into a distant report. Such seven-day significance exists only where the festival actually interrupted an existing mourning period. Maharshal mistakenly cites R. Zerachyah as supporting leniency, but his words explicitly support stringency. Responsa R. Binyamin likewise followed Maharshal without examining R. Zerachyah's actual text. My father-in-law too rules against Maharshal. Thus, where death and burial occur during a festival, Shemini Atzeret counts only as one day toward thirty.

<b>And he buried him that same day, he observes it, and so forth.</b> Rambam has an earlier ruling that in lands observing two festival days, one who buries during Yom Tov counts the seven beginning from the final second day of Yom Tov. Even though mourning is not actually observed that day, because the day is rabbinic it counts and he completes only six more days afterward. Shulchan Aruch omits it here because it is stated in Orach Chayyim siman 548.

<br><b>Maharil writes</b> that he would not rule that the second day of Rosh Hashanah counts toward shivah and said this was the implication of Maimonides. With other festivals he agrees that the second day counts. Maharshal objects that Hagahot Maimoniyot itself distinguishes cases. It reports a practical ruling where R. Yehudah's mother died on the first day of Sukkot, was buried on the second day, and after Sukkot he observed only six additional days of mourning. Raah similarly rules that when burial occurs on the final day of Yom Tov or the second day of Shavuot, six days remain afterward, because the day of burial counts even though no mourning is observed, as the day is rabbinic. Nevertheless, we follow Shulchan Aruch in Orach Chayyim that the second day of Yom Tov in the Diaspora counts even if death and burial occurred on the first day through gentiles.

Maharil was stringent specifically with the second day of Rosh Hashanah because the two days are considered one long day. Therefore, if death and burial occurred on the first day, just as the first day does not count, the second is drawn after it and likewise does not count. But if burial itself occurs on the second day of Rosh Hashanah, it appears obvious that the count begins then. The second day is at least rabbinic, and for purposes requiring a count of actual days Rosh Hashanah is counted as two. Only where burial occurred on the first day does the concept of one long day pull the second day after the first.

<br><b>I have seen</b> in some places a grave error. When a deceased person is buried on the first day of Yom Tov through gentiles, Jews themselves come and return the earth into the grave until it is completely filled. This is a full melachah, involving building-like activity, and is plainly prohibited to Jews. Care must be taken that all such work be done by gentiles, may we know no such trouble.
