<b>ומ"מ כל סתם תבואה כו'. </b>כלומר סתם תבואה שאינו ידוע אם נשרשה קודם לעומר אבל אם ידוע שרוב מין אותה תבואה לא נשרשה קודם לעומר או נזרע אחר העומר אע"פ שהמיעוט נשרשה קודם לעומר אסור ולא הוי ס"ס כן משמע בטור מת' הרא"ש ובת"ה סי' קצ"א:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Nevertheless, ordinary grain, etc.</b> Meaning ordinary grain whose status is unknown, whether it took root before the omer. If, however, it is known that most grain of that species did not take root before the omer, or that it was sown after the omer, then even if a minority took root before the omer it is forbidden, and this does not constitute a double doubt. This is implied by Tur from Rosh’s responsum and by Terumat HaDeshen, siman 191.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>For it is preferable that they remain inadvertent, etc.</b> If most drinking in the country is wine, and barley and oats are needed only occasionally as food, so that there is no public hardship in separating from the prohibition of chadash, then where there is only one doubt, whether the grain is from last year or from this year, it is a mitzvah to protest among the populace and teach them that it is forbidden. There is no double doubt by saying that perhaps it is leftover grain from last year, and if it is from this year, perhaps it came from the minority sown sufficiently before the omer. Since that possibility is only a minority, it does not create a double doubt, for a minority in relation to a majority is regarded as nonexistent, as Tosafot state explicitly in the sugya of the open entrance in the first chapter of Ketubot. In Lechem Mishneh at the end of tractate Terumot, he writes at length to permit beer made from chadash barley on the ground that the liquid extracted from the barley is permitted. The matter requires consideration, for Rosh’s responsum at the beginning of klal 2, the cited Terumat HaDeshen, and the other poskim imply that it is forbidden. Bach writes at length to prove that the prohibition of chadash does not apply to produce belonging to non-Jews. All his words are astonishing. The view of Tosafot and those who agree with them is correct, and their proofs are clear, that chadash applies to produce belonging to non-Jews. In particular, Bach’s proof from the Gemara in the first chapter of Rosh Hashanah 13a is not valid. There, were it not for the inference that they ate from the morrow after Pesach, but did not eat beforehand, one could have said that they ate old grain. Alternatively, this follows the tanna in the first chapter of Kiddushin who holds that “in all your dwelling places” teaches that the prohibition did not begin until after conquest and settlement. The Gemara there states that this tanna does not accept the derivation that they ate after Pesach but not before, whereas Rabbi Eliezer, who holds that “in all your dwelling places” means wherever you dwell, does accept that derivation. Thus, the answer in Rosh Hashanah, “This cannot enter your mind, for it is written, ‘On the morrow after Pesach,’” necessarily follows Rabbi Eliezer. Without that inference, one could have said that they did not bring the offering and that chadash did not apply until after conquest and settlement. On the contrary, this provides a clear proof that chadash applies to produce belonging to non-Jews. Since the Gemara asks from where they brought the offering, it assumes that all Eretz Yisrael had belonged to non-Jews. Nevertheless, when it infers that they ate after Pesach but not beforehand, its only difficulty is from where they brought the offering. The fact that they had not eaten beforehand is fully understandable, demonstrating that chadash applies to produce belonging to non-Jews. I also see a clear proof from the Yerushalmi at the beginning of the second chapter of Challah, concerning moist and dry standing grain. Its meaning appears to be as follows: if they found moist standing grain, is it prohibited because of chadash? Perhaps since it is ultimately destined to be harvested, it is considered chadash belonging to a Jew. Or perhaps, since it came into Jewish possession from non-Jews, it is permitted. He asked him why he did not ask concerning dry standing grain. He replied that even dry grain is included, for there is no distinction, and chadash applies to produce belonging to non-Jews. Rav Shimshon of Sens, in his commentary on the Mishnah, explains moist as new grain and dry as old grain from the previous year, and writes that it is a great wonder why the omer had not permitted last year’s grain. I do not understand his view, for the matter is clear as I have explained.

<b>ולא התחילה עדיין לשרש. </b>מבעיא בש"ס אם מותר ללקוט התבואה המונחת בקרקע בשעת עומר ולאכלה או נימא דדמיין כמונחין בכלי ול"ד ללא השרישה קודם העומר בסעיף ג' דאסור משום הגדולים מה שאין כן הכא או נימא כיון דמונחים בקרקע כגדולים דמי ואסור מספק ודוקא בנזרע אחר העומר הראשון אבל נזרע קודם העומר כיון דנעשית ישן אם כן פשוט דמותר ללקטן דנהי דאין העומר מתירן כיון דמונחים בקרקע מכל מקום כבר מותרים:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And it had not yet begun to take root.</b> The Gemara raises the question whether one may gather grain lying in the ground at the time of the omer and eat it. Perhaps it is regarded as though it were lying in a vessel, unlike grain that failed to take root before the omer in seif 3, which is forbidden because of its later growth, a concern that does not apply here. Or perhaps, since it lies in the ground, it is regarded as growing and is forbidden because of the doubt. This applies specifically where it was sown after the first omer. If it was sown before that omer, then once it became old grain, it is plainly permitted to gather it. Although the omer does not permit it while it lies in the ground, it was already permitted beforehand.
