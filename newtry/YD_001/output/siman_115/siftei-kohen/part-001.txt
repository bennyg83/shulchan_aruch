["However b'dieved one need not be concerned for all this." Meaning that one need not be concerned that all this applies — it suffices if he was there at the beginning of the milking, even if he did not see the vessel; and this is according to what Darkei Moshe and Turei Chayim wrote there in the name of Issur VeHeter — that b'dieved if he did not see the vessel but only saw the beginning of the milking it is permitted, end of his words; and likewise in the simanim there. But there is difficulty — for in Issur VeHeter beginning of general rule 45 it appears explicitly the opposite, and these are his words: the Jew must be there when the gentile begins to milk and see that there is no milk in the vessel at first and that there is no impure matter in the flock; and for this reason they practice in many places, and it is a kosher custom, not to let the gentile milk in a vessel called "gallipot" in foreign language, even though it is designated for milking and is a cold thing and not sharp at all — for one must be concerned lest there be a little impure milk in it at first and the Jew will not notice; but b'dieved nevertheless the milk is permitted even if the gentile milked in his own vessel, provided the Jew saw at first in the vessel, and afterward even if he goes out and comes in, end of his words — evidently that even b'dieved we require that the Jew saw at first in the vessel; and so it appears in Semak (daf 112 side 2) and other poskim explicitly, see there. And likewise in the law — since milk that a gentile milked is forbidden because lest he mixed impure milk into it — if so, what benefit is there that he saw the beginning of the milking if he did not see the vessel, lest there be impure milk in the vessel before the beginning of the milking that will become mixed afterward in the rest of the milk? And it appears that also the Tur who wrote "a Jew sits outside" — their intent is regarding a case where there is no concern that impure matter will be mixed in at first, and that is that he saw at first in the vessel; and what Issur VeHeter wrote that going out and coming in afterward suffices — so it is in Semak there; and it appears that even when the gentile milked for his own need, going out and coming in suffices; for the reason of going out and coming in is not because of awe alone but because since he knows there is no impure matter in the flock, even though he cannot see him at the time he milks, there is no concern for any adulteration except that the gentile will leave the flock and adulterate; and since he goes out and comes in and sees that the gentile does not leave the flock, it suffices. And so it is proved in the Gemara in Avodah Zarah (daf 39 side 2) — where there is no impure matter in the flock and the Jew sits outside, even if he cannot see him at all at the time he milks, it is permitted — for the reason that since he sits outside, behold he sees that it is impossible to adulterate; and so it appears from the words of the Tur, and not like Perishah 62 who wrote that the reason we require a Jew sitting outside is because of awe:]

["And if a gentile milked, etc." I do not know from where the Rav took this and I did not understand his reasoning — if he wants to rule like Maharai who wrote that now impure matter is not relevant at all and they forbade only milk that a gentile milked from his own animals and a Jew does not see, and the Rav deals with a Jew's animal — if so, even if the Jew did not come until after all were milked it should be permitted — why? And if he holds like Issur VeHeter that even in our time it is forbidden even b'dieved whenever a gentile's house separates — if so, even if some of the animals were milked it is forbidden. And it appears that what compelled the Rav to distinguish between some of the animals and all of them is from what Shaarei Dura siman 82 wrote — that Rabbeinu Tam permitted R' Eliezer Münz who had ewes milking in his pen behind his house and his maidservant would go and milk without a Jew seeing, end of his words; and on this Maharai wrote his above words that such is the custom according to Rabbeinu Tam; and in siman 81 Shaarei Dura wrote that Rabbeinu Tam was strict with one whose ewes were with the gentile and he sent his daughter there every morning to see the milking and she did not manage to come until most or all were milked, end of his words; and likewise Semag in the name of Rabbeinu Tam, and Beit Yosef brings him — from this the Rav inferred that Rabbeinu Tam was strict only when most or all were milked but not some of them. But in truth it is not reasonable at all to distinguish between all and some; rather it appears that in that case Rabbeinu Tam was strict if his daughter did not come until after some were milked, and the incident was thus; and so it is in responsum of Maharam of Rothenburg siman 215 and 216 (Prague edition) — that Rabbeinu Tam was strict even if some were milked since he was not there from the beginning of the milking, see there. And that which he permitted R' Eliezer Münz in a Jew's pen — that is different, for the gentile is afraid; and on this Maharai came to say that even though Shaarei Dura wrote on Rabbeinu Tam's words that it is unseemly to do so l'chatchila (and likewise in Issur VeHeter end of general rule 45), nevertheless such is the custom since impure matter is not common; and he says that with gentile animals, even in a Jew's pen, it is forbidden; and so it appears explicitly from Maharshal's words there — that for Maharai we require both his animal and a Jew's pen, see there. And also Turei Chayim wrote there that in a Jew's house it is permitted to milk his produce even when no Jew sees; and if the pen is behind the house or a gentile's house separates — forbidden l'chatchila and b'dieved permitted in every case, end of his words — and he does not distinguish between all were milked or some; and perhaps a textual error fell into his words here in the hagahah.
