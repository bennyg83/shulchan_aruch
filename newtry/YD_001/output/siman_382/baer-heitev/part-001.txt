**** ENGLISH ****
<b>I saw.</b> This practice arose because of the wickedness of the gentiles. Where there is no such concern, the mourner should remove his shoes immediately in the cemetery, as explained above at the beginning of siman 375 and in siman 376. Shach. Taz cites Mordechai that if one must appear before an official who objects if he comes without shoes, he should place earth in his shoes and go. The same applies in a dangerous place or anywhere else where he must wear shoes. Taz further writes, citing his brother R. Yitzchak, that a woman may wash in preparation for wearing white garments even within the seven days. Rama's statement that within seven days one may not wash in preparation for wearing white garments is surprising, because this is not bathing for pleasure. Immersion bathing itself is forbidden during mourning because it serves no purpose when marital relations remain forbidden, but washing in order to begin the clean-day process should not be forbidden. At minimum, even Rama can mean only that she should not wash where she can wear clean garments without washing at all, as stated in siman 196, seif 3, in cases of pressing need. Responsa Masat Binyamin permits even actual washing within seven days for the purpose of wearing white garments and disagrees with Rama. Taz concludes that in practice she should wash only the lower part of the body and wear a clean garment, though not necessarily a white one. Those who are stringent enough to postpone wearing white garments entirely are acting astonishingly. Maharshal writes that Maharam served as sandak within thirty days of his father's death and bathed the night before the circumcision, explaining that for him it was like a festival and that the prohibition against bathing was only a custom of stringency adopted by earlier generations.
