**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And even if, etc.; but, etc.</b> So write the Hagahot Maimoniyot, cited by the Darkei Moshe, seif katan 18. See Mordechai, siman 456, who writes that when a non-Jew borrows money from a Jew, even if the lender knows that the non-Jew is borrowing for another Jew's benefit, he may collect principal and interest, because a non-Jew is not subject to agency. He then cites a great authority who disagrees with Rabbeinu Tam, proving his view from the Tosefta: if a Jew appointed a non-Jew as steward or administrator, it is forbidden to borrow from him with interest. Mordechai answers that a steward or administrator is publicly known to be lending on the Jew's behalf. This is the meaning here of "because it appears," etc. All the other poskim, however, explain the prohibition because the responsibility rests upon the Jew, as the Tosefta states, "This is the rule," etc., and as explained below in seif 24. <br><b>(Likkut) And even if, etc.</b> The Hagahot Maimoniyot write this because the Tosefta posed a difficulty to Rabbeinu Tam's ruling. It teaches: if a Jew said to a non-Jew, "Go lend my money with interest," it is forbidden. They distinguish between a case with collateral and one without collateral. See there, siman 33, and Mordechai, siman 447. The poskim, however, distinguish according to whether responsibility rests upon the Jew or upon the non-Jew.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Even though he gave, etc.</b> There the Gemara states, "Granted, the latter clause," etc. See Tosafot there, s.v. "Such as," and Rashi there, s.v. "with that understanding." This is the meaning of "and some," etc., "but," etc. Rabbeinu Tam's proof is from the Tosefta and Yerushalmi cited by Tosafot there: if a Jew appointed a non-Jew as administrator, etc.; money belonging to a Jew that is deposited, etc. The latter case is permitted because responsibility rests upon the non-Jew, unlike an administrator or steward, as the Tosefta states, "This is the rule," etc. Likewise here, even though the non-Jew placed the second Jew in direct relation with the first, so long as he did not tell the first Jew, "You are discharged," the money remains under the non-Jew's responsibility and should be permitted. The other authorities rejected this proof, because placing the parties in direct relation is different. The deposited money discussed there was accepted under responsibility specifically so that the non-Jew would borrow it as a borrower, as explained in Sefer HaTerumah. See the Shach, seif katan 68.

<b>A non-Jew who took collateral, etc.</b> The Semak, cited by the Tur, writes that even according to Rabbeinu Yitzchak, who permits a case where the Jew gave his collateral to a non-Jew to borrow upon it because he transfers ownership to the non-Jew, that reasoning does not apply where the non-Jew seized the collateral against the Jew's will. This follows the Baal HaItur cited by the Rosh in the second chapter of Pesachim. According to the Rosh, however, who disputes the Baal HaItur, it is permitted, as explained below in seif 20. The Tur likewise writes here that the later words of his father, the Rosh, indicate that this too is permitted. The Rosh explains in Pesachim that the prohibition of interest applies between borrower and lender. Here the Jew lent nothing to another Jew, but only to the non-Jew. The dispute of Rabbeinu Yitzchak and the Ramban does not apply, because there the Jew sends the non-Jew to borrow from a Jew and permission therefore requires the presumption that he transferred ownership. Where the non-Jew borrowed for his own need, the Rosh permits it. Sefer HaTerumah, folio 65d, makes the same distinction. The Semak nevertheless holds that since the non-Jew took collateral from one Jew and immediately pledged it to another Jew, it appears that he borrowed for the first Jew's benefit, as the Beit Yosef explains, folio 130d, s.v. It is forbidden specifically when the Jew himself redeems the collateral, because this makes it appear that the non-Jew was his agent from the outset. This is the meaning of "If the Jew," etc. Below, however, in seif 20, the ruling is that even if the Jew redeems it, it is permitted, following the Rosh's view. The alternative explanation offered by the Beit Yosef there is difficult, because the Baal HaItur's reason is that a non-Jew does not acquire collateral from a Jew. The Rosh should therefore have answered that the Jew actually transferred ownership to him, whereas instead he challenged the Baal HaItur from the nature of the borrower and lender relationship. Furthermore, did the Jew know from the outset that the non-Jew would need to borrow upon the collateral? If one says that the Jew presumably intended to transfer it, the same should apply regarding chametz. All this follows Rashi's view in seif 6, which the poskim accepted, as explained above.

<b>A non-Jew, etc.</b> The Tosefta, chapter 5, teaches: if a Jew said to a non-Jew, "Here is your wage; go lend my money with interest," it is forbidden. Money belonging to a Jew and deposited with a non-Jew may be lent with interest; money belonging to a non-Jew and deposited with a Jew may not be lent with interest. If a Jew became an administrator or steward for a non-Jew, it is permitted to borrow from him with interest. If a non-Jew became an administrator or steward for a Jew, it is forbidden to borrow from him with interest. This is the rule: whenever responsibility rests upon the Jew, it is forbidden; whenever responsibility rests upon the non-Jew, it is permitted. The same appears in the Yerushalmi. In the first and last cases, involving wages, administrator, and steward, responsibility remains upon the owner. In the middle cases of deposited money, responsibility rests upon the custodian.
