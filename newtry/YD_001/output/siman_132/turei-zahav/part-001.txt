<b>It is permitted to take its value from that non-Jew. </b>For this is not a sale. **** ENGLISH ****
<b>And if the non-Jew first took the wine, etc. </b>So too wrote the Tur. The source is the statement at the end of the chapter HaSocher, 62b, that the household of R' Yannai lent produce of Shemittah to poor people, and they repaid them in the eighth year. We do not say that the poor people committed a prohibition, because when the repayment was received the forbidden produce was no longer in existence. This resembles what we learned concerning the fee of a harlot: if he gave it after relations, the fee is permitted for an offering, because it is then merely a gift. Tosafos derived there that the same applies to a sale to a non-Jew on credit. Once the non-Jew performs meshichah, he acquires the item immediately, and the money he later gives is merely a gift. The Rosh, however, wrote there that the money is permitted only to another person, but not to the seller himself, because he benefits from money received for idolatry. He derived this from the law of one who sold orlah and betrothed a woman with its proceeds: she is betrothed, but the money is forbidden to the seller himself. Thus, the permission for others is an explicit Talmudic ruling from the case of Shemittah produce mentioned above. Only the prohibition for the seller himself is derived from the sale of orlah. The Beit Yosef overlooked here the source permitting the money to others and derived it instead by his own reasoning from the law that a woman betrothed with the proceeds of orlah is betrothed. He therefore asked that perhaps orlah is different because its prohibition does not transfer to its proceeds. <i data-commentator="Nekudot HaKesef" data-label="♯" data-order="1"></i>Accordingly, he wrote here, There are those who say, etc., that it is permitted to others, referring to the view of Rabbeinu Yerucham cited by the Beit Yosef. This is imprecise, for the permission to others is an explicit Talmudic ruling, as stated. The phrase, There are those who say, should instead have been written concerning the prohibition to the seller himself, which is the view of Rabbeinu Yerucham cited by the Beit Yosef and also the view of the Rosh mentioned above.

<b>For as soon as it enters the airspace of the vessel, etc. </b>The Gemara states concerning this that were we to hold that nitzok constitutes a connection, we would say that while the wine is still in the airspace of the vessel it becomes forbidden through nitzok, because the stream touches at its lower end the residual wine below, and the entire stream becomes forbidden. According to us, since we indeed rule that nitzok constitutes a connection, as written in siman 126, in a case involving only a minor loss we should rule stringently. It appears to me that according to our ruling that when forbidden wine becomes mixed with other wine one may sell the entire mixture except for the value of the forbidden wine within it, one may be lenient here concerning nitzok even l'chatchilah. <b>And likewise if the non-Jew measured it, etc. </b>So too wrote the Tur. His words are to be explained as follows. The Mishnah states: If he fixed the price and then measured, it is permitted; if he measured before fixing the price, it is forbidden. The first clause has two explanations. Rashi explains that the Jew measured the wine into the non-Jew's vessel, as the Tur discusses at length in setting out these distinctions. Rabbeinu Chananel and Rabbeinu Tam explain that even if the non-Jew measured it, it is permitted, because the case is one in which the non-Jew had already performed meshichah and acquired it before measuring. Therefore, this too is permitted. The Tur accordingly records this explanation as well, that whenever the non-Jew first acquired the wine, it is permitted even if he measured it. Nevertheless, the Tur apparently reasoned on his own that l'chatchilah it is forbidden, because the non-Jew is likely to touch the wine while occupied with its measurement. The Tur had to state this here, although elsewhere he does not state an initial prohibition merely because an act is performed through the force of a non-Jew. Elsewhere the wine is in any event forbidden for drinking, and therefore one certainly would not arrange such a procedure l'chatchilah. Here, however, the wine already belongs to the non-Jew. The Tur therefore teaches that even so one should not do this l'chatchilah, because the non-Jew is likely to touch it. Otherwise, one may end up receiving payment for yayin nesech, since the non-Jew did not first perform a valid meshichah. I have not found a source for this ruling, and it therefore appears that the Tur wrote it from his own reasoning. The Tur then explains the latter clause of the Mishnah, that if he measured before fixing the price it is forbidden. This applies whether the Jew measured it or the non-Jew measured it and performed meshichah. In either case, it has not yet left the Jew's ownership, because the price was not fixed and the purchaser does not yet rely upon their reaching agreement. It is therefore likely that the non-Jew touched the wine. This is what the Tur means when he writes that although there is here only an admixture of ordinary non-Jewish wine, it is forbidden. One might otherwise have said that where residual wine remained at the bottom of the non-Jew's vessel, that which was permitted in the first clause is forbidden here because he had not yet acquired the wine, since the price was not fixed first, but that where no residual wine remained at all it would be permitted here as well. This cannot be correct. Why, then, does the latter clause of the Mishnah state without qualification that its proceeds are forbidden? The prohibition caused by residual wine requires only that one sell the mixture except for the value of the yayin nesech within it. Why would the Mishnah say that all the proceeds are forbidden? Furthermore, the word forbidden implies even b'dieved, whereas because of residual wine it would be permitted b'dieved, as stated above. The Tur therefore explains that the prohibition is because the non-Jew may touch the wine after meshichah. Accordingly, it is forbidden even if no residual wine remained at all. I have seen one who forced an unnecessary explanation here. One might ask that in the first clause as well, where the non-Jew had not lifted the wine beforehand to acquire it, the Tur writes that he is likely to touch it, yet he forbids it only l'chatchilah. It appears that in the first clause the non-Jew did not perform a proper meshichah, and merely measuring the wine does not make him as accustomed to handling it as in the latter clause, where he drew it in order to acquire it. There he is more accustomed to it and is more likely to touch it, and therefore it is forbidden even b'dieved. This appears to me to be the correct explanation of the Tur, unlike the Beit Yosef, who explains that the case of measuring before fixing the price refers specifically to a non-Jew who measured it. The Darkei Moshe already challenged this at length and wrote that whether the non-Jew measured it or the Jew measured it, it is forbidden, because the primary concern is that the non-Jew may touch the wine after acquiring it through meshichah. When the Tur wrote above that nevertheless it is permitted b'dieved, that was because the non-Jew did not acquire it through meshichah, but only through the Jew granting him use of the domain or saying to him, Let your vessel acquire it for you. He is therefore not as accustomed to handling the wine as when he acquired it through meshichah. The Beit Yosef asked that at the beginning of the siman the Tur wrote that one may take payment from the very non-Jew who rendered the wine forbidden. This is no difficulty. <i data-commentator="Nekudot HaKesef" data-label="♯" data-order="2"></i>Here the case is different, because the Jew gave him permission to touch it by delivering it into his hand, unlike the case above.

<b>ודוקא כשאמר לו יהא דינר זה בידך כו'. </b>הרשב"א בתה"ה הוכיח זה דהוקשה לו כי הקדים לו דינר מאי הוה הא מדיהבי ליה קנייה עובד כוכבים דסתמא דמילתא נתנו לו שיוציאו ובשעת נתינת המעות ליכא איסורא דלא שקילי יין נסך וכי שקלי פועלים בתר הכי לא הוה הדינר של ישראל וה"ל כנתן טלה באתנן ואח"כ בא עליה דאתננה מותר ע"כ פי' דמיירי כמ"ש כאן ודוקא כו' והטור כתב על זה ואינו נראה דכיון שהקדים לו הדינר מיד נקנה לו היין ובשליחת' נתנו להם ע"כ וכתב ב"י שאין ספק שלא ראה הטור דברי תה"ה אלא הקצר ראה ולפיכך נחלק אבל אי ראה הארוך לא נחלק דמאי שנא מאתנן שמביא הרשב"א) ולעד"נ דברי הטור נכונים דאע"ג דמעות אינן קונות היינו בעובד כוכבים לדעת הטור לעיל בסימן זה אבל ישראל מישראל מעות קונות דבר תורה כדאיתא פרק הזהב ואם כן ישראל מעובד כוכבים כ"ש דקונה במעות כדאיתא בח"מ סימן קכ"ו דאם ישראל מישראל מפקיע במעמד שלשתן ק"ו מעובד כוכבים הכא נמי כן הוא וכיון שהקדים לו דינר על היין נקנה לו במעותיו יין כנגדו וכ"ת מאי איכפת לן בהאי קנין הא אין הישראל נהנה ממנו לזה אמר שהעובד כוכבים נותנו בשליחות ישראל בעל היין לפועלים שלו וזה אין שייך באתנן לומר שקנה שום דבר באתנן ההוא נמצא דאין לאתנן שייכות להביאה שאחר כך ולפיכך שרי התם כן נראה לע"ד ליישב דברי הטור אבל הרשב"א לא חשיב זה לקונה ממנו יין נסך דהא סתם קאמר שיתן להם אכילה ושתייה ולא זכר יין נסך:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Specifically when he said to him: Let this dinar remain in your possession, etc. </b>The Rashba proves this in Toras HaBayis HaAruch. He asks: What difference does it make that the Jew advanced him a dinar? Once the Jew gave it to him, the non-Jew acquired it, for ordinarily money is given to be spent. At the time the money was given there was no prohibition, since the workers had not yet received yayin nesech. When they later received it, the dinar was no longer the Jew's. This should resemble one who gave a lamb as a harlot's fee and only afterward had relations with her, in which case the fee is permitted. The Rashba therefore explains that the case is specifically as stated here, that the Jew said the dinar should remain in the non-Jew's possession. The Tur writes concerning this: This does not appear correct, for once he advanced the dinar, the wine was immediately acquired for him, and the non-Jew gave it to the workers as his agent, end quote. The Beit Yosef writes that the Tur undoubtedly did not see the Rashba's words in Toras HaBayis HaAruch, but only the abbreviated version, and therefore disagreed. Had he seen the longer work, he would not have disagreed, for how is this different from the case of the harlot's fee cited by the Rashba? In my humble opinion, however, the Tur's words are correct. Although money does not effect acquisition, that applies to a non-Jew according to the Tur's ruling earlier in this siman. Money paid by one Jew to another effects acquisition by Torah law, as stated in the chapter HaZahav. If so, when a Jew buys from a non-Jew, all the more so he acquires with money, as stated in Choshen Mishpat, siman 126, that if a Jew can transfer a debt owed by another Jew through the presence of all three parties, he can certainly do so when the debtor is a non-Jew. The same applies here. Since he advanced the dinar for wine, he acquired with his money the corresponding amount of wine. One might ask why this acquisition matters, since the Jew does not personally benefit from the wine. The answer is that the non-Jew gives the wine to the Jew's workers as the agent of the Jewish owner. This does not apply to a harlot's fee, where one cannot say that the giver acquired anything through the fee. The fee therefore has no connection to the later relations, and it is permitted there. This appears to me to resolve the Tur's words. The Rashba, however, did not consider the Jew to have purchased yayin nesech from the non-Jew, because the Jew merely stated generally that he would provide the workers with food and drink, and did not mention yayin nesech.
