**All slaughter, etc.** Taz brought the words of Tur, who cited the baraita that Rabbi taught: “And you shall slaughter,” etc., “as I commanded you”—teaching that Moses was commanded regarding two [simanim] in an animal and one in a bird, and most of one like him. Taz challenged: generally we derive on what is stated in the verse “as He commanded you”—commanded at Marah; why is this “as I commanded you” different from “as He commanded”? Answer: to distinguish Torah from Mishnah—see there; and in any case there is room to say that at Marah they were commanded only regarding mitzvot that would be practiced immediately from then on; and how many mitzvot the Holy One blessed be He preceded giving them at Marah until giving the entire Torah; but a mitzvah that would not apply to them immediately, only after some time from receiving the Torah—He gave only at giving the Torah, when all of them came—whether practiced immediately or after time. And in the desert we hold like R' Akiva that they did not require shechitah in Chullin—only nehirah meat was permitted them; and likewise Rambam explicitly. If so, how could it enter one's mind that they were commanded at Marah regarding a mitzvah that would not apply to them immediately until entering the Land—and what was its nature at Marah? Perforce “as I commanded you” refers to Moses, who was commanded regarding entering the Land—and straightforward. With this one may resolve the words of Rambam in chapter 1 of Hilchot Shechitah, who wrote: learning bird shechitah from Torah—for regarding bird it says “whoever hunts animal or bird and pours its blood,” teaching that pouring blood of a bird is like blood of an animal—end quote. Maggid Mishneh wondered: in the Gemara we derive from this verse that there is no shechitah for a bird min haTorah—for it is written “and pour its blood”—mere pouring; and it challenges “say animal”; and answers it is equated to disqualifications of kodshim, etc.; and we say “who is Chanina who disagrees?” and we say “it is Rabbi,” who taught “and you shall slaughter,” etc., “as I commanded you,” and we learn Moses was commanded regarding two in animal and one in bird—end quote the Gemara. If so, on the contrary, from this verse “and pour” it should enter one's mind to declare bird kosher without shechitah—and Rambam learns from it that shechitah is required. Maggid Mishneh answered: now that we hold like Rabbi—from “as I commanded you,” etc., that he was commanded regarding bird—the verse “and pour” goes for pouring. And still explanation is needed—for nevertheless the Gemara did not mention we derive from it; on the contrary it should be inferred explicitly the opposite; and many certainly infer from “and you shall slaughter from your cattle and flock as I commanded you” the command regarding animal—but regarding bird we did not hear from where is an allusion (see Tosafot who expounded Rabbeinu Tam, see there). And it appears that certainly in Scripture it is written “for whoever hunts animal or bird”—it opens in the plural and concludes in the singular “and he shall pour its blood,” not “their blood”—plural language (see Chullin 87 s.v. lechalek, see there); and it must be said “and pour” means mere pouring without shechitah—and this is only regarding bird; therefore it wrote “its blood.” Or, conversely, Scripture spoke in singular language to teach that animal and bird are as one species for shechitah, and both require shechitah. And if we say like R' Akiva that in the desert nehirah was permitted—why the first explanation that it comes to teach mere pouring suffices, for then in the desert obviously everything was by pouring and pouring is not a precise matter at all; perforce the second sign is needed to teach they are equal forever, whether in the desert or afterward—either both by pouring or both by shechitah without difference. And this is the intent of the Gemara of Rabbi, who said “as I commanded you” teaches Moses was commanded, etc.; for at Marah, where he holds nehirah was permitted in the desert, there is no need for “and pour” to teach bird suffices by pouring; perforce it must teach one law with animal, and it is proven that also for bird shechitah is required like animal—and these are the words of Rambam; think.

**Even women.** Tosafot and Rosh wrote this—to exclude the laws of Eretz Yisrael, which wrote women do not slaughter l'chatchila because their minds are light-minded; Tosafot challenged him from the beginning of chapter Kol HaPesulim: the Gemara challenges “all invalid persons who slaughtered”—b'dieved yes, l'chatchila no; yet slaughter by a zar is kosher l'chatchila, and it answers because it teaches “slaughter is kosher with women, slaves,” etc., and tamei—l'chatchila no, lest he touch meat—and it does not answer “because of women,” etc.; rather learn that women are kosher l'chatchila. In Tosafot, Ran, and Rosh it mentions anonymously “because their minds are light”; Hagahot Maimoniyot chapter 4 of Hilchot Shechitah wrote their minds are light lest they faint—therefore one may reject their proof, or certainly if one stands al gavav and sees she did not faint it is kosher. In kodshim slaughter it is uncommon for a woman to slaughter without a grown man al gavav, for a kohen must receive the blood—from reception onward it is a mitzvah of kehunah. It is strained to say she slaughtered with a long knife outside the azarah—for in any case a woman's way is not in the azarah, as Tosafot wrote in several places, and the kohen stands in the azarah and receives blood—like tamei in kodshim, which also deals with kehunah—and if so he does not see the slaughter; this is strained. And he holds regarding slaughter in Eretz Yisrael that with one al gavav a woman is permitted l'chatchila even though some say we are concerned for fainting—we do not permit l'chatchila with one al gavav for a woman, but certainly we permit—it is only a general stringency. Perhaps Tosafot go according to their view in Chullin 12b s.v. katan, etc., that a minor with one al gavav has da'at—and if so in kodshim too one may say his slaughter helps; if so those in the Mishnah deal with one al gavav—why does it teach “slaughter is kosher with women,” etc.? It should teach a greater novelty that it is kosher with minors when one stands al gavav—but this is not enough to attack the laws of Eretz Yisrael. Perhaps Tosafot come against the author of laws of Eretz Yisrael in both ways: according to his view those Amoraim who are not concerned for the question “and all who slaughtered,” etc., should explain the Mishnah with that resolution—“all slaughter” means all men slaughter, but women do not slaughter l'chatchila, and if they slaughtered their slaughter is kosher—tzarich iyun; and one must say the Gemara holds “and if they slaughtered” is redundant, and if to permit b'dieved—it already teaches in Zevachim that slaughter is kosher with women, as the Gemara challenged regarding tamei in kodshim; and the Gemara answers there the main topic is kodshim, etc.—this does not apply to disqualification of women, which is mainly in chullin, and one should not make a distinction between kodshim and chullin—two unnecessary teachings fit well. However per this one must say that which does not deal with one al gavav—for otherwise it teaches too much that even without one al gavav it is kosher b'dieved—and Tosafot's question stands well in both ways. In the body of the question itself it should have established the Mishnah thus: “all slaughter”—men, etc.—this question can be asked in several ways; the correct view is as Taz wrote, but not for his reasons—for one may only say that certainly by way of parable: if it teaches “women do not slaughter l'chatchila,” it implies l'chatchila forbidden and b'dieved permitted—and then it should be difficult that it further teaches “and if he slaughtered his slaughter is kosher b'dieved, permitted,” for this is already implied from the precision of the reisha. However for every resolution in the Gemara this question applies: Rabah bar Avuha establishes it regarding tamei in kodshim—do not slaughter l'chatchila; this is if he did not say “it is clear to me I did not touch,”—even b'dieved invalid; only if he says “it is clear to me” then b'dieved permitted—and behold here is another mode not heard from the precision of the reisha. Likewise Abaye and Rava establish Kuti—do not slaughter unless he cuts an olive-size of meat and eats; even b'dieved invalid unless he cuts and eats—then b'dieved permitted and his slaughter is kosher. Likewise R' Ashi regarding apostate—certainly if he does not check his knife afterward b'dieved forbidden; only kosher b'dieved when he checked the knife. But regarding the challenge to the Geonim at the beginning—it is not resolved with the answer I wrote, that there is no case at all of l'chatchila do not slaughter until examined—for if he slaughtered it is permitted; if so the siyafa is redundant, for it explains “all slaughter” on the basis of examination afterward, and if he did not examine b'dieved permitted—and there is no redundant precision at all; think. Nevertheless there is a resolution for laws of Eretz Yisrael—for they certainly stand according to what we hold we are not concerned for fainting, but a woman stands in presumption of fainting; however for one version in the Gemara in the words of Ravina—in every person we are concerned for fainting and require presumption he will not faint; if so for women too—if no presumption, obviously forbidden; if there is presumption, from Torah to forbid. If so there in Zevachim the Gemara sought to answer for all, even for that resolution of Ravina—if so unnecessary “because of women” that women differ from men—even kohanim may not slaughter l'chatchila without presumption, and likewise women; there is no difference between men and women, and therefore it had to say “because of tamei.” According to the conclusion that we are not concerned for fainting—so much the more laws of Eretz Yisrael. The upshot: the words of the author of laws of Eretz Yisrael may not be pushed aside; Rama wrote well that women should not be left to slaughter—do not heed Peri Chadash's testimony that he saw women slaughtering, river by river, and it spread.

**That we say “now he became defective.”** Agudah learned thus from that which we say: he slaughtered with a knife and it was found defective—R' Chanina declares kosher, and the Gemara explains: what is different from tevel on which something interposing was found and immersion did not take effect—the Gemara answers: a knife became defective but an animal did not become defective; Agudah wrote: likewise here—the slaughterer became defective but the animal did not become defective. Taz wondered: this is for R' Chanina, but we hold like Rav Huna that even if he broke bones in it every day it is forbidden—learn we do not say “the knife became defective”; if so, likewise regarding a slaughterer one should forbid retroactively. Tosafot challenged: what is different from a mikveh that was measured and found lacking—they render impure retroactively and we do not say “the place became defective but the person did not”; Tosafot answered several answers, and one answer that there was a safek the defect was afterward and we thought it was beforehand—perhaps he did not slaughter with that defect. If so, in this case too the safek applies: perhaps now he forgot and we thought beforehand that perhaps nevertheless he slaughtered properly—this is all for R' Chanina; but since we hold like Rav Huna, learn we do not say so—and likewise regarding a slaughterer there is room to forbid. Peri Chadash agreed and brought proof from Rashba who holds regarding cheeses: if you must say it did not become treifah now but only before, we say establish retroactively. If so here too one cannot say that at this moment he forgot—establish retroactively. These words do not help and do not avail—for when Rashba spoke he certainly did not say except for a safek where we say “perhaps it was before”; we too concede in this case there is a safek—only it is a safek as Taz wrote.
