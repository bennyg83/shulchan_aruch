**** ENGLISH ****
(Siman 84 seif 4) And some forbid even if it died inside the fruit and separated, etc. That which Peri Megadim in Shaftei Da'at challenged — that if it separated dead it is rabbinic, as Kereti and Peleti wrote, why did they rule stringently. One could say that in a teiku we are stringent regarding rabbinic matters, as Maharshal wrote, brought by Knesset HaGedolah at the beginning of chapter 1 of Beitza. And Peri Megadim himself also wrote thus in Orach Chayyim siman 244. And it requires study, for in siman 462 there he wrote the opposite — that without teiku it is a safek chisaron yeda.

**** ENGLISH ****
(There seif 13) Honey into which ants fell — one should heat it, etc. And this is not nullifying forbidden matter ab initio for eating; but when one intends to permit the permitted and separate the forbidden from it — it is permitted. So too Rashba in responsum siman 494 and Ran chapter 2 of Avodah Zarah regarding hag'alah. And in Sha'ar HaMelekh, Laws of Forbidden Foods, he challenged — for from the words of Tosafot and Ran in Pesachim (daf 30) it appears that it is forbidden even though one does not intend to nullify the forbidden in order to benefit; and according to what Peri Chadash wrote in Orach Chayyim siman 453, there is room to distinguish — for hag'alah is different, since one's intention is to separate the forbidden from the permitted, and in such a case everyone permits:

(There seif 17) A burnt sheretz is permitted to eat for medicinal purposes. It implies that without medicinal purpose it is forbidden. And Minchat Yitzchak general rule 46 s.k. 9 challenged — whence did he learn this, for in the Gemara and poskim it appears that ab initio it is permitted, for 'all that are burned, their ashes are permitted,' and a chick from a nevelah egg is permitted — why? For from when is it accepted? When it spoils it is mere dust. And the case under discussion is not comparable to the proof, for there it changed into something permitted — and similarly he himself wrote in Orach Chayyim Laws of Pesach. And further, regarding a chick one does not come to forbid it as after the fact, unlike a sheretz. And further, from the words of Rosh in chapter 2 of Pesachim that chametz charred before its time is forbidden in eating even though there was no prohibition upon it beforehand — and Shulchan Aruch ruled thus — it also implies prohibition; and the reason is because we say 'since it is fit for eating, he has established it.' And from the language of Rambam chapter 14 of Laws of Forbidden Foods, law 11, it also appears thus — for he wrote: but one who eats forbidden food after it spoiled and became foul and is no longer human food — he is exempt. And it implies that there is nevertheless a prohibition, as Perishah deduced in Orach Chayyim siman 442 and ruled to forbid in every prohibition upon which there was a prohibition from the outset and afterward became unfit for human food — except chametz. And it appears that this is for that reason that we say 'since it is fit for eating, he has established it.' And so one may deduce from the words of Rambam in chapter 4 of Laws of Chametz and Matzah, and Taz brought it in Orach Chayyim there, where he wrote: the bread itself that molded, etc. — he is not liable to destroy it. And this means it molded before its time of prohibition, as Hagahot Maimoniyot wrote; and nevertheless he wrote only that he is not liable to destroy it — implying that in eating it is nevertheless forbidden (and see Laws of Shevuot). And although regarding chametz some permit even ab initio, as Rosh wrote in Pesachim — nevertheless in other prohibitions one certainly forbids rabbinically. And if so, here too if not for medicinal purpose one certainly should not permit a burnt sheretz in eating even though it is unfit for human food, because we say 'since it is fit for eating, he has established it' — only when one eats it for medicinal purpose it is permitted, for then it is not applicable to say 'since it is fit for eating, he has established it,' for his illness proves that he is not eating it except for medicinal purpose. And if so, all the more so on Yom Kippur it is permitted to eat foods unfit for eating for medicinal purpose — for he does not reveal his intent thus, as Taz wrote in his book 212. And not like Sha'agat Aryeh wrote in siman 75 — and that which 'all that are burned, their ashes are permitted' does not refer to eating but to prohibitions of benefit, and refers to benefiting. And from this that it is permitted without checking in seif 8 — it is no proof that there are good doubts there, as I wrote. And independently it is possible it is forbidden. And if so, whence do we permit eating ab initio — behold all prohibitions, even though one is not lashed except through their normal manner of benefit, there is nevertheless a prohibition, as Tosafot wrote in Kiddushin and in Raavad on Niddah. And if because we say 'nevelah unfit for a ger is not called nevelah' — one could also say it is forbidden rabbinically, as is implied from the words of Rambam. And so Peri Chadash wrote in Orach Chayyim there: And know that Noda B'Yehuda deduced in Yoreh De'ah part 1 siman 26 from the words of Rambam that specifically in other prohibitions one who eats after it spoiled and is no longer human food is exempt — but in basar b'chalav and the like he is liable. From this he rules like R' Meir — that what is not spoiled from the outset does not require fit for a ger, and he exempts only in other prohibitions because it is not in its normal manner of benefit — but nevertheless the name of prohibition remains upon it. And his deduction is not necessary — on the contrary, from that which he concludes regarding basar b'chalav and the like that he is liable when a bitter thing was mixed in, we learn that if they spoiled he is also exempt in them. And as Chavat Da'at wrote siman 150 — that the prohibition there flew away even though they fixed it until it was fit for eating:
