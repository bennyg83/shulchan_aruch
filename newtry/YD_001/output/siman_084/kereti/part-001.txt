**** ENGLISH ****
After twelve months. Within twelve months, soaking in cold water is ineffective. Hot water is also ineffective, because a worm remains forbidden after death just as before, as explained above. Moreover, at times a worm naturally remains fixed to the fruit without moving, adhering to it very tightly, and cold water will not cause it to crawl. Such a worm is itself forbidden even if it never emerged outside, since it developed while attached to the fruit. After twelve months, however, if the worm's nature is not to move from the fruit, it is plainly permitted, because no separation occurred. If its nature is to move, and we are concerned that it may separate into the cooked food, then it will also separate when exposed to cold water. Pouring hot water cannot be relied upon when there are many worms. It may be relied upon only when the cold water has already reduced their number, for there is then a double doubt: perhaps nothing remained after the cold-water treatment, and even if something remained, perhaps it died in the hot water. The possibility that there were no worms at all does not count as a doubt, because these species are certainly infested.

**** ENGLISH ****
A woman who found, etc. Maharshal maintains that one should not rely upon women for these inspections. The Rama writes that the custom is to rely upon them. In truth, nowadays it is more reasonable to rely upon the inspection of women than upon that of men, for men are often hurried and occupied with business and do not pay sufficient attention, as experience demonstrates. Nevertheless, when one wishes to eat raw vegetables, such as salad and the like, there is a doubt concerning a Torah prohibition. Nullification is not applicable, because one can inspect the vegetables two or three times until the worm is found. It is therefore a doubt concerning a Torah prohibition, and Heaven forbid one should be lenient. It is difficult for me to rely upon women, or upon men who hurry through their work, in such a case. With cooked vegetables, however, one may rely upon their inspection. At the very least, their inspection removes the matter from the status of a certainty that creeping creatures are present and leaves only a doubt. There is then a double doubt: perhaps nothing remained after the inspection, and even if something remained, perhaps it dissolved during cooking. Furthermore, after cooking it is impossible to identify or remove the worm. According to the Torah it is nullified, and it is only by rabbinic law that a whole creature is not nullified. One may therefore be lenient regarding this rabbinic concern. This has been my practice since I reached maturity, not to eat raw salad vegetables and the like in reliance upon such inspection. Every Torah scholar should act likewise, for this is a very common stumbling block.
