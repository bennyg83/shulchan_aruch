<b>For whatever purpose they wish.</b> This applies even to a non-mitzvah purpose. Maharik, at the end of responsum 128, writes similarly in the name of many authorities and concurs. This is specifically where the collection is a fixed one, so that if funds are lacking they will collect again. But where an unforeseen need arose and they collected for particular poor people, such as for clothing, or many poor people arrived and money was collected for them by name, the funds may not be redirected to another purpose, not even to other poor people. So rule the Rosh and Tur, and this also appears to be the view of Mahari Weil, responsum 26. See also Responsa of Rabbi Eliyahu Mizrachi, responsum 9, and Responsa Maharam Alashkar, responsum 10. It appears that for redeeming captives they may redirect the funds even in such a case, as stated above at the beginning of siman 252. The Beit Yosef writes that the Rosh appears to understand the Yerushalmi in Shekalim as permitting a change only to another need of the poor, but not to a non-mitzvah purpose, whether by the trustees or by the community. The Darkhei Moshe questions this, because the Tur writes that even redirecting the funds to other poor people is forbidden. There is no difficulty. The Rosh explicitly cites the Yerushalmi, 'surplus funds collected for particular poor people may be used for other poor people,' and explains that the trustees are not restrained if they see a genuine need to redirect them. Yet the Rosh himself explains that the case concerns an unforeseen collection for particular poor people. The Yerushalmi therefore means that when the trustees discern a genuine need, they may redirect the funds for a mitzvah purpose, even though an unforeseen collection may ordinarily not be redirected, even to another mitzvah. The Beit Yosef accordingly explains that the Yerushalmi permits the trustees to redirect only for another need of the poor, but not for a non-mitzvah purpose. This is clear.

<b>וה"ה לגבאי הממונה כו'. </b>אין לשון הרב מתוקן דמשמע דגבאי שוה לכל דבר לציבור ואינו כן כשהציבור יכולין לשנות אפי' לדבר הרשות כמו שהסכימו הרבה פוסקים וכתבו בסמ"ק דכן עמא דבר ולדבריהם הסכים מהרי"ק שורש ה' וקכ"ח אבל הגבאי אינו יכול לשנות אם לא לדבר מצוה כדאיתא באשר"י פ"ק דב"ב וכן כתב מהרי"ק שורש ה' בשם הפוסקים והכי אמרינן בפ"ק דערכין משבא ליד גבאי אסור לשנותה ול"ד לחבר עיר דרשאי לשנות לדבר הרשות כמו הציבור כדמשמע מדברי הרמב"ם וט"ו דחבר עיר היינו חכם גדול שהכל גובין על דעתו והכל סומכין לעשות כמו שירצה וכן כתב הב"ח דדווקא חבר עיר אבל גבאי אין רשאי לשנות למה שירצה כי לא נתמנו בסתם להיות הרשות בידם לעשות מה שירצה אם לא שנהגו בכך מקודם על פי תקנותם עכ"ל ואולי הרב מיירי בגבאי דומיא דחבר עיר והיינו במקום שהמנהג כן שהגבאי עושה מה שירצה ועיין על זה במהרי"ק וב"י כמה חילוקי דינים בזה:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>The same applies to an appointed charity collector, etc.</b> The Rama's wording is imprecise, for it implies that a charity collector has the same authority as the community in every respect. This is not so. The community may redirect funds even to a non-mitzvah purpose, as many authorities agree, and the Semak records that this is the common practice. Maharik concurs in responsa 5 and 128. A charity collector, however, may redirect funds only for a mitzvah purpose, as stated by the Rosh in the first chapter of Bava Batra and by Maharik in responsum 5 in the name of the authorities. So too the Gemara states in the first chapter of Arakhin that once funds have reached the charity collector, they may not be redirected. A city sage is different, for he may redirect them even to a non-mitzvah purpose, like the community itself. As is evident from the Rambam, Tur, and Shulchan Aruch, a city sage is a preeminent scholar on whose judgment everyone contributes and whom everyone relies upon to act as he sees fit. The Bach likewise writes that this applies specifically to a city sage, but an ordinary charity collector may not redirect funds as he wishes, since he was not appointed without limitation to exercise such discretion, unless prior custom established this authority under communal enactments. Perhaps the Rama refers to a charity collector comparable to a city sage, namely, where local custom empowers the collector to act as he sees fit. See Maharik and Beit Yosef for the various distinctions in these laws.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>This applies specifically to charity that they would not otherwise have given, etc.</b> The Mordekhai writes in the name of the Or Zarua: This applies specifically where a fast was decreed because of drought, an outbreak of wild animals, or another calamity, and the residents undertook charity and likewise required visiting townspeople to contribute, in order to annul the decree through repentance, prayer, and charity. In such a case the visitors must give to the local charity collectors because of suspicion, and they must also give through the city sage where there is one. But charity customarily given at all times and fixed in every place, which they would not have refrained from giving had they remained in their own town, need not be given to the local collectors or city sage merely because they are presently elsewhere. They may take it home with them. Accordingly, villagers who come to larger communities for Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur, recite memorial prayers, and pledge charity are not subject to suspicion, for they will certainly give it. Nor must they give it to the local city sage, because this charity does not arise from the local community but from established law and custom. They may therefore take the charity back to their villages and use it as they see fit. This is quoted by the Beit Yosef and Darkhei Moshe. It nevertheless appears that if they purchased honors in the synagogue, such as mitzvah honors or gelilah, they must pay the local community and may not retract and take the money home. They purchased those honors in this synagogue, and otherwise the local charity would suffer a loss because local residents would have purchased them. This is the accepted practice. I later found the same ruling in Responsa Mabit, part 1, responsum 60. The Kol Bo, siman 116, records an ancient enactment that villagers without a minyan who come to established communities and bring wax candles lit on Yom Kippur must leave the unused portion in the synagogue where they were lit. If they prepared two candles, one should remain where it was lit, and the other may be lit in their own town where they pray privately.

<b>מי שיש לו מעות אחרים כו'. </b>לשון הר"ר טוביה שבהגמ"ר א אינך מושבע עליו אחרי אשר אין לך חלק בשכר ואפי' קדמה תקנת מקומך לתקנת מקומו הייתי אומר לך לשלחו לו והוא יביאהו אל בית האוצר אם לא פירשו בתקנתן שיפרישו מעשר מכל מה שירויחו בין יבא לידם בין יבא ליד אחרים והכי נמי נראה לי במלוה מעות למחצית השכר שיש לו לשלם חצי המעשר לבעל המעות והוא חייב להביאו אל בית האוצר הואיל ומחצית השכר שלו עכ"ל ומביאו בית יוסף בסימן רנ"ח והרב כתב דבריו על פי הבנתו ואחריו נמשך העט"ז אבל באמת אין הדבר כן והדבר פשוט דמה שכתב ואם לא פירשו בתקנתן וכו' רצה לומר ואף אם לא פירשו בתקנתם של מקום המלוה וכן פירש מהר"מ מטיקטי"ן בגליון המרדכי פרק הגוזל בתרא וכ"כ בספר גדולת מרדכי שם שבס"א כתב בהדיא אפי' אם לא פירשו כו'. א"כ העולה מהמרדכי והגמ"ר הוא דמי שנתעסק עם מעות אחרים אם כל הריוח למלוה ואין לו חלק בשכר ישלח הכל ליד המלוה והוא יתן שם המעשר אפי' קדמו תקנת מקום המתעסק לתקנת מקום המלוה ואפי' לא פירשו במקום המלוה שיפרשו מכל מה שירויחו אחרים מממונם ואם מלוה למחצית ישלם החצי שכר למלוה ומחצית שכר יתן המתעסק בעירו ודו"ק:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>One who has money belonging to others, etc.</b> Rabbi Toviah writes in the glosses to Mordekhai: You are not bound by the local oath concerning money from whose profit you receive no share. Even if the enactment of your place preceded that of the owner's place, I would instruct you to send the money to him, and he should bring the tithe into his local treasury, unless their enactment explicitly requires tithing all profit generated by their money, whether it comes into their own hands or into the hands of others. The same appears to me regarding money lent under a half-profit arrangement: the agent should pay half the tithe to the owner of the money, and the owner must bring it into the treasury, since half the profit belongs to him. The Beit Yosef cites this in siman 258. The Rama understood this passage differently, followed by the Levush. In truth, however, the words 'unless they explicitly stipulated in their enactment' refer even to the enactment of the lender's place. Maharam Tiktin explains this way in his gloss to Mordekhai, chapter HaGozel Batra, and the work Gedulat Mordekhai notes that another version states explicitly, 'even if they did not stipulate.' The resulting law is this: one who conducts business with another person's money, where all profit belongs to the owner and the agent has no share, must send everything to the owner, who gives the tithe in his own locality. This applies even if the enactment of the agent's locality preceded that of the owner's locality, and even if the owner's locality did not explicitly require tithing profits earned by others through local residents' money. Under a half-profit arrangement, the agent pays the owner his half of the profit, while the agent gives the tithe from his own half in his own city. Consider this carefully.
