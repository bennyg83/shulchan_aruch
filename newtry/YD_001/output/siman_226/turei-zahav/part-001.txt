<b>And all the more so if one of them, etc. </b>The Tur writes here: “unless it is for the need of one of the partners in the courtyard.” The Beit Yosef expressed astonishment at this, for since the courtyard is subject to division, the vow takes effect, and what difference does it make that he enters for the need of one of the partners? In any event, he benefits from walking through it, as we say regarding one who is prohibited from benefiting from another and enters to visit him. This requires study, end quote. For this reason, the Shulchan Aruch rules here without qualification that it is forbidden, meaning even when he enters for the need of one of the partners. Regarding the latter case, where the courtyard is not subject to division, the Tur writes: “The vow does not take effect, whether they prohibited one another or one of them prohibited an outsider from benefiting from his property,” end quote. The Beit Yosef writes: “Perhaps our master confused one place with another. Here he should have written that it is permitted specifically when he enters for the need of one of the partners, for so the Ran wrote in the name of the Rashba,” end quote. He therefore ruled in the latter case that it is permitted specifically for the partners’ needs. In my opinion, however, the words of our master the Tur are to be understood in their plain sense, and they follow the words of his father, the Rosh, who wrote: “Rabbeinu Shimshon wrote in the name of the Ri that the halacha is that in Torah matters there is bereirah, as is proven here, etc. According to this ruling, partners who prohibited one another from walking through the courtyard may enter it. If one of them prohibited an outsider, that outsider may not enter the courtyard, for ordinary concessions are forbidden. Bereirah cannot permit the partners when the courtyard is subject to division, for R’ Eliezer ben Yaakov permits on the basis of bereirah only when no remedy exists through division. All the more so may an unrelated person not enter, for why should he enter the courtyard? It appears to me that when R’ Eliezer ben Yaakov permits an outsider in the Mishnah, the case is where he enters for the need of one of the members of the courtyard,” end quote. His words clearly indicate that only where the courtyard is subject to division must the outsider enter for the need of one of the partners, since he wrote this specifically regarding that law. It follows automatically that where the courtyard is not subject to division, the outsider may enter even for his own need. What the Rosh wrote first, “If one prohibited an outsider, he may not enter,” does not refer to the partners, but to an unrelated person. He intended to exclude Rabbeinu Tam’s explanation, which he cited immediately beforehand, that the ruling is that if the partners prohibited one another from walking through the courtyard, they may not enter, whereas if the owner prohibited an outsider, the outsider may enter because ordinary concessions are permitted. The Rosh therefore wrote that according to Rabbeinu Shimshon’s ruling both matters are the reverse. This is clear to one who studies the passage. The same appears in the Tur’s abridged rulings: “Partners who vowed not to benefit from one another may enter the courtyard, for we rule that there is bereirah. This applies specifically when it is not subject to division, but not when it is subject to division. All the more so, if an outsider was prohibited by them, he may not enter except for the need of one of the members of the courtyard,” end quote. Thus, he placed the prohibition upon an outsider after discussing a courtyard that is subject to division. One cannot say that it also refers to the first case, for how could he say “all the more so”? What kal vachomer would there be? It therefore certainly refers to the latter case, and this is also the meaning of his words here in the Tur. The reason for these laws appears to be as follows. Whenever the courtyard is not subject to division, the vow does not take full effect upon the courtyard, even when one partner prohibited an outsider, because the outsider can say, “I am not entering your portion, but your fellow’s.” The Rambam likewise writes this in chapter 4 of Hilchos Nedarim, and these are the words of R’ Eliezer ben Yaakov in the Mishnah, which concerns a courtyard not subject to division. In such a case we apply bereirah. When it is subject to division, however, he cannot say, “I am entering your fellow’s portion,” for since division is possible, their domains are not viewed as intermingled. This is straightforward. Nevertheless, when he enters for the need of one of the partners, he may enter for another reason mentioned by the Ran: “The Rashba wrote that this applies specifically where he enters for the need of the partner, whether he needs the partner or the partner needs him, because the courtyard is acquired to each partner and to anyone who needs them. An outsider, however, may not enter it for his own purposes, for although the partners granted one another use of the courtyard for walking and for all their needs, they did not grant it to the entire world,” end quote. The Rosh also cited part of this in the name of R’ Shimshon ben Avraham of France, who is the Rashba. This demonstrates that whenever a person needs the courtyard, even for his own benefit through the partner, he is permitted, because the courtyard is granted to him for that purpose. If so, even where it is subject to division, this permission applies, for he may say, “I am entering my own,” meaning that he has a right in the other partner’s share. We are stringent in a courtyard subject to division only because a remedy through division is possible, and therefore we do not apply bereirah. This applies specifically to the partners themselves who vowed not to benefit from one another, since each can compel the other to divide. An outsider, however, is not a party to the division with the one who prohibited him, and bereirah therefore assists him even in such a courtyard. Accordingly, what difference is there whether the courtyard is subject to division or not? This is clear to anyone who understands. We can now resolve an apparent difficulty in the Rosh’s words cited above. He asked how R’ Eliezer ben Yaakov could permit an outsider in the Mishnah. He could seemingly have answered that R’ Eliezer ben Yaakov speaks of a courtyard not subject to division and permits the outsider there, just as the opening case of partners who vowed not to benefit from one another concerns a courtyard not subject to division, as I wrote in siman 224. In truth, there is no difficulty. The Rosh wished to teach that the Mishnah’s case of an outsider prohibited from benefiting from one of the partners, which R’ Eliezer ben Yaakov permits, applies to every courtyard, even one subject to division, whenever he enters for the need of one partner. In that circumstance, a courtyard subject to division has the same law as one not subject to division, for the reason is the same: the outsider has no power to compel division, and the courtyard is granted to him for that use. The Tur’s words are therefore exact. Where the courtyard is subject to division, an outsider may not enter for his own needs, because the courtyard was not granted to him for that purpose and he cannot say, “I am entering your fellow’s portion.” When he enters for his own need, each partner has the legal right to prevent him, and each therefore also has the power to prohibit him. He cannot claim, “I am entering my own.” When, however, he enters for the need of one partner, the other partner has no right to prevent him and therefore cannot prohibit him, for it is as though the outsider says, “I am entering my own.” Thus, entering for the need of one partner in a courtyard subject to division is equivalent to entering for one’s own need in a courtyard not subject to division. Both are permitted, because he can say, “I am entering your fellow’s portion,” as explained. The Beit Yosef’s difficulty from the case of entering to visit one who is ill is thereby removed, for here the permission rests upon the claim, “I am entering your fellow’s portion.” The Rashba, however, certainly holds that even when the courtyard is not subject to division, permission exists only when the outsider needs one of the partners, as is clear from Responsa of the Ramban, siman 242, which are the Rashba’s words, as the Beit Yosef wrote in the introduction to his work. The Shulchan Aruch therefore correctly ruled here that when the courtyard is not subject to division, it is permitted specifically when the outsider enters for the need of one of the partners. We must still clarify this permission for an outsider who needs one of the partners. It appears clear that he must need a member of the courtyard other than the one who made the vow. If he needs the one who prohibited him, it is certainly entirely forbidden. This is also implied by the Rashba’s wording cited above, where he says, “he needs the partner,” meaning the second partner. The rationale itself requires this, for the Rashba’s principal reason is that the courtyard is granted to anyone who needs the partner on that partner’s account. This cannot apply to the person who made the vow. Even if the courtyard was granted to the outsider for the sake of that partner, the partner may still prohibit his own property to him. I saw that my revered father-in-law, of blessed memory, wrote that if he enters for the need of the one who prohibited him, it is permitted, for that person surely did not prohibit him from serving his own needs, etc. I do not understand this at all. One who prohibits another from benefiting from his property certainly prohibits him even when he will later need him, as is clear from siman 221, seif 4: “If his son became ill, he may not enter at all,” etc. There is no greater need than this. Only regarding his own livelihood do we say there that he did not prohibit him, but not regarding other needs. My revered father-in-law also wrote at length to explain the Tur’s ruling that when the courtyard is not subject to division, an outsider prohibited by one partner is permitted, as meaning specifically when he needs one of the partners. This is not implied by the language at all, for the Tur writes that “the vow did not take effect.” According to his explanation, the vow did take effect with respect to an outsider entering for no partner’s need. Although the Shulchan Aruch also writes here that “the vow did not take effect,” this proves nothing, for it followed the Tur’s wording and then added its qualification. I have written what appears clear to me in the words of the Rosh and Tur. Regarding the Shulchan Aruch’s initial ruling that where the courtyard is subject to division entry is forbidden without qualification and even need of a partner does not help, one may rely upon the lenient view when he enters for the need of one of the partners, since the Rosh and Tur wrote this explicitly. <i data-commentator="Nekudot HaKesef" data-label="♯" data-order="1"></i>It is an extraordinary wonder that the Beit Yosef did not see these words of the Rosh. Had he seen them, he would not have written that our master confused one place with another. So it appears to me, and my revered father-in-law, of blessed memory, likewise ruled this way as practical halacha.

<b>וכן הדין בשנים כו'. </b>סדר הג"ה הזאת אינה מדוקדקת דדין זה הוא מתשובת הרשב"א שהביא בית יוסף סוף סימן רכ"א ושם כתב דבשותפות מקומות אלו אין בו חלוקה וכיון שכן אינו יכול לאסור כלל על חבירו אלא דבמקום שאדם יכול לאסור על חבירו יש חילוק בין אומר הקדש לך או לאו וכמו שכתב כאן רמ"א ואם כן צריך לומר דרמ"א מיירי כאן במקומות שיש בהם דין חלוקה וכל זמן שלא חלקו ואחד מהם הקדיש הוה חילוק בין לך או לא ואם כן מה לו לתלות חילוק זה בשנים שיש להם בשותפות כו' אלא היה לו לכתוב חילוק זה סתם במי שאוסר מקומו על אחד מן השוק ושותפות מאי בעי הכא:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>The same law applies to two people, etc. </b>The arrangement of this gloss is imprecise. This ruling comes from a responsum of the Rashba cited by the Beit Yosef at the end of siman 221. There he wrote that jointly owned synagogue seats are not subject to division, and therefore one partner cannot prohibit the other from using them at all. Only where a person has the power to prohibit another is there a distinction between saying, “It shall be hekdesh to you,” and not saying so, as the Rama writes here. The Rama must therefore be discussing seats that are subject to division, before they have actually divided them. If one partner consecrates his share, the law depends upon whether he says, “to you.” If so, why did the Rama connect this distinction specifically to two people who jointly own, etc.? He should simply have stated the distinction regarding anyone who prohibits his place to an outsider. What relevance does joint ownership have here?

<b>לא כוון אלא שיהא הקדש לעניים. </b>שם בתשובה דמ"מ אחר פיו אנו הולכין אם אומר מה היה כוונתו דסתם נדרים להחמיר ופירושן להקל ולהחמיר עד כאן ונראה דהיינו בלא אומר לך אבל באומר לך לא מועיל מה שמפרש להקל דלשון לך הוי כמפרש להקדש ממש כמבואר שם:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>He intended only that it should be consecrated for the poor. </b>In that responsum it is stated that, nevertheless, we follow his own explanation if he states what he intended, because unspecified vows are interpreted stringently, while their explanations may produce either leniency or stringency, end quote. It appears that this applies when he did not say, “to you.” If he said, “to you,” however, his later explanation cannot create a leniency, for the phrase “to you” itself is equivalent to an explicit statement that it is actual hekdesh, as explained there.

<b>והיו כופין את הנודר כו'. </b>דחיישינן שמא יכשל חבירו על ידי נדרו ובטור כתב בזה ב' דעות הרמב"ן סבירא ליה דלא חיישינן למכשול חבירו אלא למכשול הנודר ורמב"ם סבירא ליה אדרבה הנודר יזכור שנדר ויזהר משא"כ במודר שלא הוציא נדר מפיו אפשר שישכח לפיכך חייב למכור:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>They would compel the one who made the vow, etc. </b>We are concerned that his fellow may stumble because of his vow. The Tur cites two opinions. The Ramban holds that we are concerned only that the person who made the vow may stumble, not that the person prohibited by it may do so. The Rambam holds the opposite: the one who made the vow will remember his vow and be careful, whereas the person prohibited by it, who did not utter the vow himself, may forget. Therefore, the one who made the vow must sell his share.
