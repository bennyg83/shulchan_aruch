**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Take precedence.</b> The customs state that even if the mourner within seven days is a guest, he takes precedence over a local resident who is within thirty days or observing a yahrzeit. This applies specifically on the Shabbos during the seven-day period, because during the weekdays he does not go to the synagogue. If the mourner within seven days is a minor who is not yet bar mitzvah and does go to the synagogue during all seven days to recite Kaddish, or if someone loses a father or mother during those seven days, he recites the orphan's Kaddish once. Shach writes that if a person's mourning was canceled by a festival, such as where one hour before the festival canceled the decree of seven or thirty days, nevertheless the status of seven or thirty still applies to him even during the weekday for these priority rules, because he is legally permitted to go to the synagogue. Responsa Masat Binyamin asks that the custom sources appear contradictory. Initially they say that a resident observing a yahrzeit takes precedence over a resident within thirty days for the entire yahrzeit day, including Maariv, Shacharit, Minchah, and the Kaddishim said at the amud, implying that yahrzeit takes precedence. Later they say that an out-of-town mourner within twelve months or observing a yahrzeit receives only one Kaddish, whereas an out-of-town mourner within thirty days shares all Kaddishim and prayers with the resident, implying that thirty days takes precedence. He answers that the first case is where there are many yahrzeits and insufficient Kaddishim for everyone. Then the mourner within thirty days is displaced by the yahrzeit, because if the yahrzeit misses Kaddish that day, the opportunity passes, whereas the mourner within thirty days can say Kaddish the next day. But where there is ample opportunity for Kaddishim, such as on Shabbos, the mourner within thirty days takes precedence and receives two Kaddishim while the yahrzeit receives one. Taz accepts this distinction as correct. Shach disagrees and reads the customs literally: yahrzeit takes precedence over thirty days. The later rule, limiting an out-of-town yahrzeit to one Kaddish while allowing an out-of-town mourner within thirty days to share with a local resident within twelve months, concerns the preference of a resident over a guest. The resident can tell the visiting yahrzeit, "Your date was fixed and known to you a full year in advance, yet you entered my jurisdiction; one Kaddish is enough for you." This argument does not apply to a mourner within thirty days. According to our practice, however, where thirty days takes precedence, if a local mourner within thirty days coincides with a minor within seven days, the minor receives one Kaddish as well. Shach. Responsa Masat Binyamin further writes that even if one mourner within seven days is an adult local resident and the other is a minor guest, they divide equally. Taz writes that if someone dies on Shabbos or Yom Tov and cannot be buried that day, Kaddish should nevertheless be recited immediately after death because Kaddish is not dependent on mourning. On a weekday, however, this should not be done because while he is an onen he is exempt from prayer. Nekudot HaKesef disagrees: since the reason for Kaddish is to redeem the parent from Gehinnom, before burial there is no judgment of Gehinnom, and he believes the custom is therefore not to recite it before burial.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>The custom.</b> Some communities practice that a local resident takes precedence over a guest, while in other places they are equal. Even where the local resident takes precedence, the guest is initially entitled to lead the prayers once and to recite the orphan's Kaddish once. The customs state this. Shach writes that the guest is thereby treated like a local mourner within twelve months. Accordingly, if the resident himself has no Kaddish, such as where a mourner within thirty days is present and takes precedence, the guest likewise receives nothing. The customs further state that if the deceased has sons in another city who come to the city where their father was buried, they do not acquire resident status merely because their father is buried there. Only those actually residing within the community are residents. This includes taxpayers, communal servants, and even the city's poor who have a fixed residence there. A person becomes a resident from the time he becomes obligated to share communal burdens, not merely from the time he settles his tax arrangement with the ruler, even though he may already be obligated to pay the ruler. Conversely, one who moves away ceases to be a resident once he is no longer obligated to share communal burdens, even if he still owes tax to the ruler. A householder's hired teacher or attendant, if single and hired for a term, is also considered a resident. If he has a wife and children elsewhere, he is treated as an outsider. An orphan being raised in a household, even for payment, is considered a resident if he has no father or mother elsewhere. If he has a parent elsewhere, he is not a resident even if supported as charity, and certainly not if he is there for wages. Shach adds that youths and unmarried men studying in a yeshivah have resident status even if they have parents elsewhere. Even a married householder studying in the yeshivah, though his wife and children remain elsewhere and he pays taxes there, is treated as a resident where he studies. This is the practice. The customs further state that where both parties are within thirty days, both are observing yahrzeit, or both are within twelve months, the guest is entirely displaced from leading the prayers and reciting Kaddish at the amud. But for Barechu and Kaddish said at the doorway, they are equal and divide by lot. If both guest and resident are within seven days, they also divide by lot, because for the seven-day period there is no preference of resident over guest.
