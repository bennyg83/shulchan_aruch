**** ENGLISH ****
Or to separate challah. Maharshal wrote: therefore they are accustomed to say terumah challah, both of them, end of his words. (1) And I am astonished: for we should still say perhaps challah is the main thing and not terumah, and if so the word terumah would be an interruption between the berachah and the word challah. And the Semak explicitly wrote that there is no insistence here, and whichever one wants, one may say. And those women who change on Pesach regarding challah from the matzot from what they say all year — it is obvious that it is a mistake in their hands, for what change is there in this regarding the berachah. And it is implied here that they need not say from the dough, but it suffices when they say to separate challah; and why then did they become accustomed to say from the dough? And even though the law is so, to exclude separating challah from flour, nevertheless there is no need of it for the berachah; and do they need to mention the details of the laws in the berachah? And above, siman 28, regarding the berachah of covering that they say with earth, we wrote there the reason why they say the particular; and here I do not know a need for it.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And likewise a maidservant, and so forth.</b> Terumat HaDeshen, siman 188, was asked about a maidservant who kneaded dough for the housewife, but the housewife left and the dough had fully risen. Further delay would spoil it. The maidservant wished to separate challah without express permission. He permits this because preventing spoilage is a benefit to the owner, and one may acquire a benefit for another in his absence. The maidservant is therefore treated as an agent even without explicit appointment. The printed text later says, "and even where there is only a concern of spoilage," but this is difficult because the opening already dealt with spoilage. Nor does it fit the subsequent reasoning that housewives are accustomed sometimes to authorize servants to separate challah. In my opinion the text should read, "and even where there is no concern of spoilage," meaning that regular prior authorization can itself establish presumed consent. Rama appears to have had this reading, for he writes simply that a maidservant may separate, even without mentioning spoilage. My father-in-law disagrees and reads Terumat HaDeshen as permitting only where there is at least some concern of spoilage. I have shown why that reading is difficult. Still, it appears that Terumat HaDeshen himself did not rely entirely on presumed authorization where there is no spoilage, because both his question and final conclusion return to the concern of spoilage. Semak provides additional support. He writes that if the kneader separates without permission, the challah is valid, comparing it to workers in a winepress who may separate terumah once they have gone back and forth in the press because otherwise the owners, who are amei ha'aretz, may touch and defile the produce. Beit Yosef rejects the comparison, arguing that those workers were hired specifically to preserve purity. But if that were literally their explicit agency, the Mishnah would teach nothing. Rather, the point is that once the owner has shown that he wants the worker to manage the process properly, we infer consent to whatever separation is normally needed, even without a fresh instruction. Accordingly, on Erev Shabbos, when women are particular to separate challah themselves and consider it part of preparing bread for Shabbos, a maidservant should not separate without the owner's command unless spoilage is genuinely threatened. On ordinary weekdays, however, where the housewife regularly lets the maidservant or household members separate, they may do so without a fresh instruction because she is not particular.
