**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And it became mixed.</b> Responsa Chatam Sofer, siman 307, discusses a firstborn that became mixed into a flock. The Jewish owner had marked the firstborn's wool so it would not be confused and entrusted it to a gentile shepherd, informing him that this was a sacred animal forbidden in shearing and work. When shearing season came, the shepherd sheared the entire flock including the firstborn, destroying the mark, and also castrated many animals, leaving uncertainty whether the firstborn was among the castrated or unblemished animals. Several issues arise. First, each unblemished animal is now a doubtful unblemished firstborn and ordinarily all must graze until they develop blemishes. One might seek to permit through separation from the fixed mixture, but the dispute of Rabbenu Tam and Ri is well known, and with sacred property all agree that one separated animal is not permitted because of the decree lest one intentionally take from the fixed group. Second, the castrated animals have blemishes that, if inflicted by the shepherd under the relevant circumstances, are sufficient to permit the actual firstborn after the fact. Since each of those animals is only a doubtful firstborn, they may be eaten by Israelites because the kohen must prove which one is the firstborn, except that one animal is given to the kohen to avoid theft from the priestly tribe. Regarding shearing and work, however, each remains a doubtful firstborn indefinitely. Chatam Sofer ultimately finds leniency because it was never established by valid testimony that the firstborn was actually mixed into the flock. No Jew saw the act; only the gentile shepherd said so, and his words are inherently surprising because they assert he violated the owner's explicit instructions. A gentile is not believed either to permit or to prohibit. Even though the number of shorn animals matches the expected flock including the firstborn, perhaps another animal was present. This does not create a fully reversible double doubt, because we know a firstborn certainly existed and cannot begin by doubting whether one ever existed. Still, enough uncertainty remains to permit the blemished animals for shearing and work through one of two methods. First, give one of the blemished animals to a kohen. The kohen should slaughter and eat it, because there is a mitzvah to eat his firstborn promptly. Once one member of the mixture has been consumed in the ordinary course of the mitzvah, the others become permitted, as in siman 110, seif 7, because no one acted expressly in order to nullify the prohibition. The remaining animals should nevertheless be shorn two at a time rather than singly, as explained there. Second, one can rely on Rama in siman 110, seif 6, to move all the animals away from their original fixed place simultaneously so that none remain in the original kavu'a location. The separated animals may then be shorn even one by one, even if they later regroup, as Shach explains. The final two would normally remain prohibited, but one can combine the first method by slaughtering one of the separated animals and attributing the firstborn identity to it, thereby permitting the final two as well. Regarding the remaining unblemished animals, there is further room for leniency. If they are a minority and most of the flock was castrated, one can attribute the prohibition to the majority of castrated animals and permit all the unblemished ones, especially because the castration occurred before the mixture became known, which resembles separation before the doubt arose and is not subject to the decree lest one intentionally take from the fixed mixture. Even if the unblemished animals are the majority or exactly half, so this route is unavailable, moving all of them away from their fixed location can be used as described above. As an added precaution, after they separate one by one it is preferable, where feasible, to cause an unmistakable blemish through a gentile and then eat them after the blemish. For the definition of an unmistakable blemish, see siman 309. Each blemish should be inspected by three ordinary synagogue members, not by a single individual. Chatam Sofer's discussion of the mixed wool is cited above in siman 308, seif 8.
