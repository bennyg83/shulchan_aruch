**** ENGLISH ****
<b>An androgynos and one who, etc.</b> Shabbat 138b: “Is that so? But,” etc.; “this depends upon that,” according to the tannaitic dispute, etc. The Rosh wrote that Behag was uncertain whom the halachah follows; therefore such a child is circumcised on the eighth day, but Shabbat is not desecrated for him. Beit Yosef wrote that the same applies to an androgynos. His words are puzzling, because according to all opinions an androgynos is circumcised on the eighth day. The principle that “this depends upon that” does not apply to him, since Shabbat is not desecrated only because of the uncertainty, whereas on the eighth day he is certainly circumcised, lest he be male. It is possible that Beit Yosef wrote this according to Tosafot and the Rosh at the end of chapter 8 of Yevamot, who hold that an androgynos is certainly male. See Tosafot to Shabbat there, s.v. “and not a doubt,” etc.

<b>Small stars.</b> Specifically small stars. See Shach.<br><b>(Likkut) If he was born, etc.</b> We are not expert in determining the proper measure of the stars. See Tosafot to Shabbat 35a, s.v. “two,” etc., who write, “but it is difficult,” etc., and “now they disagree,” etc. Therefore the principal criterion depends upon the darkening of the sky. Even if the firmament was still bright, etc., the matter does not depend upon the brightness of the firmament but upon the stars. At dawn the reverse is true: day begins at dawn, when the firmament begins to brighten, as stated in the Yerushalmi at the beginning of Berakhot concerning “two rays of light,” etc., even though all the stars are still present. The Yerushalmi there states: Rabbi Chanina, colleague of the Sages, asked: just as you say that in the evening three stars become visible even though the sun is still within the thickness of the firmament, perhaps the same should be said at dawn. Rabbi Abba said: Scripture states, “The sun had risen upon the earth,” and it also states, “The sun comes, and he is purified.” The Torah compares its emergence to its setting: just as at its setting it is hidden from human sight, so at its emergence it must become perceptible to human sight. Rabbi Abba further said: Scripture states, “The morning was light”; the Torah calls light “morning.” Rabbi Yishmael taught: “morning by morning,” to establish a boundary for the earliest morning, etc. Rabbi Chiya said: this may be learned from ordinary conduct. When a king begins to emerge, even before he has actually emerged, people say, “The king is emerging,” etc. Rashba, at the end of chapter 2 of Shabbat, was troubled by this Yerushalmi because he understood it as referring to the beginning of sunset, and therefore wrote that the matter requires study. This is incorrect. It refers to the criterion explained above. This also resolves Tosafot’s difficulty in Shabbat there, s.v. “two thirds of a mil,” where they ask that here, etc., and Rabbeinu Tam answers that there the discussion concerns, etc.; but they further object, etc., and answer that perhaps the later statement, etc. According to Rabbeinu Tam’s view, night does not begin until four mil after sunset. They therefore ask further that we should simply observe the time taken by an average person, etc. Ramban and Ran likewise write that from the beginning of sunset until twilight is three and one quarter mil, as stated in Orach Chaim 261. According to this, the interval from dawn until sunrise would equal the interval from sunset until the appearance of the stars. This is not so, as is evident from the Yerushalmi cited above and from ordinary observation, for the interval from dawn until sunrise is far longer than the interval from sunset until the appearance of the stars. In northern countries, dawn begins in summer around midnight, whereas there is then no true appearance of all the stars at all. I have already explained that the two intervals are not equal, because at dawn the stars remain visible for a long time. Certainly the appearance and disappearance of the stars correspond to one another, and the arrival and departure of light correspond to one another. Thus, in northern countries the light never fully departs in summer, although stars remain visible. The “appearance of the stars” mentioned in Pesachim, however, means the visibility of all the stars. All the stars are not visible until it is fully night and the light has entirely departed, because of the minuteness of the smallest stars. This is the meaning of Shabbat 35b: not small stars, which are visible only at night, meaning after the light has disappeared. Those stars are not visible at all in the northern summer. By contrast, the appearance of stars mentioned throughout the Talmud as defining night means only three stars, and specifically medium-sized ones, as stated in Shabbat. These are two separate distinctions. Such stars become visible when the upper portion of the sky has darkened and become equal to the lower portion. Since great expertise is required to identify exactly three stars and to know that they are medium-sized, as Tosafot state there, the Sages instead gave the measure of the darkening of the sky, etc. This is also the basis of the statements of Abaye and Rava there. Their final difficulty, that we should observe the time taken by an average person, is likewise resolved. Moreover, the measure of four mil is not uniform at every season and in every location, as is well known. Their answer that perhaps the matter remains uncertain is also difficult, since the interval is already measured in Pesachim. According to what has been explained, however, everything is clear. There is no need to posit two sunsets. All references are to one sunset, and the interval from sunset until night is only three quarters of a mil. In our countries it is longer, because horizons and seasons differ. The measurements of four mil and of three quarters of a mil are stated for the horizon of Eretz Yisrael and Babylonia. Here the interval is longer. The seasonal measurement applies to the equal days around the equinoxes of Nisan and Tishrei, when such matters may be measured uniformly. This also removes the apparent contradiction with Rambam and Ibn Ezra, who write that twilight spans twenty degrees, approximately one hour and one third, whereas according to the Gemara it is one and one half hours, or one and one fifth hours, as discussed in Orach Chaim 459. Heaven forbid that the Gemara should contradict observation so plainly that, according to it, there would be no night for four months in our countries. Rambam and Ibn Ezra speak of the equator, while the Gemara speaks of their own horizon. According to what has been explained, twilight begins immediately at sunset. It is called twilight because it lies between the setting of the sun and the beginning of the arrival of darkness, namely the point at which the sky has darkened and its red glow has ceased. Thus sacrificial blood becomes invalid at sunset, as stated in Zevachim 56a and elsewhere. Likewise one may no longer pray Minchah then, as stated in Berakhot 29b: “In the West they cursed one who prayed at the dimming of the sun.” According to Tosafot, however, it would still be broad daylight. Shabbat likewise begins then, as the Mishnah in Shabbat 17b states: Beit Hillel permit work until the sun sets. Tosafot to Shabbat are not referring to an additional period added to Shabbat, as Tosafot to Rosh Hashanah 9a, s.v. “and Rabbi Akiva,” explain. End of Likkut. See also the Gaon’s comments in Orach Chaim 261.
