<b>And they do not rend.</b> See Baer Heitev, which notes Ramban and Tur's view that mourners do rend for one who took his own life, and according to them they also observe mourning, though no eulogy is given. Responsa Chatam Sofer, siman 326, argues that Ramban's position is fundamentally correct and rejects Lechem Mishneh's proof at the end of Hilchot Avel chapter 1. Nevertheless, for practical halachah he will not oppose Beit Yosef and Shulchan Aruch, who rule like Rambam, especially because we follow the lenient view in mourning. He adds, however, that where the honor of a distinguished family is at stake and they would suffer enduring shame because one member acted improperly, while permitting them to mourn would lead people to assume the rabbis investigated and concluded that he did not have the formal halachic status of one who intentionally took his own life, a rav may permit mourning even if he personally knows the strict status actually applied. Ramban is in any case the primary position, and although mourning itself follows the lenient view, regarding family disgrace we do not minimize the honor of descendants of Avraham, Yitzchak, and Yaakov. See Orach Chayyim siman 139, seif 3 in the gloss. See also Responsa Lachmei Todah of Mahari Basan, siman 18. Chatam Sofer further writes that although the common practice is not to recite Kaddish for one who intentionally took his own life, he knows no compelling reason for the practice. Why should such a sinner be denied every effort to rescue his soul from destruction? Avshalom also failed to act as a proper member of Israel, yet spiritual rescue was still sought for him. The custom developed because other mourners refuse to surrender their share of Kaddish, arguing that the deceased brought this situation upon himself and should not impose the cost of his soul's rectification upon others. He might otherwise have lived for years and died at another time or place. Therefore, if the son assembles a private minyan at home, causing no loss to other mourners, he may recite Kaddish and should do so for the full twelve months. The usual reason for stopping after eleven months is to avoid classifying one's father as wicked, but this person already rendered himself wicked and would prefer the atonement. In a synagogue where there are other mourners, however, if the sons know with certainty that their father deliberately took his own life, reciting Kaddish at the expense of other mourners would wrong the deceased righteous people represented by those mourners. Family honor does not justify taking another deceased person's right. If there is any genuine doubt whether the strict status applies, then even if burial was conducted stringently and the deceased was treated with disgrace, the sons retain the right to recite Kaddish. Members of a community share these rights, and the sons acquired their share immediately upon the father's death. Those who seek to exclude them must prove that the death occurred in a manner that forfeited it.

<b>One who deliberately takes his own life.</b> Responsa Even Shoham, siman 44, discusses a man who lived uprightly and practiced fasting and immersion, and later was found dead under circumstances suggesting self-inflicted death. He had gone to immerse, told the woman living there that if people looked for him they would find him at the immersion place, later returned home, and the next day was found lying in blood with a knife in his hand. Some days earlier he had sharpened the knife, saying he wanted it for slaughter, and a handwritten note on the wall asked his students to recite Kaddish after him. Even Shoham ruled that none of the customary honors should be withheld. First, no one saw him perform the fatal act, and circumstantial evidence alone is insufficient. His request for Kaddish can be explained by weakness and fear that he might die. Second, perhaps confusion or mental disturbance overtook him, and the strict status applies only to an act done with clear, settled intent. Third, perhaps he acted out of some distorted notion of repentance. Chatam Sofer, siman 326, analyzes the baraita in Evel Rabbati chapter 2 and concludes that even if a person announces, "I will go up to the roof and throw myself down," and is seen going up, but is later merely found below dead, we still say perhaps another cause made him fall. If witnesses actually see him fall from the very height he said he intended to use, then even if they cannot prove he pushed himself, the circumstances create a presumption that he acted intentionally. If no one saw the fall itself, finding him dead at the location is not enough. Maharshal's statement that if he was not seen going up the strict status does not apply is therefore unnecessary: even if he was seen going up, without seeing the fall it still does not apply. Chatam Sofer further explains Mechaber's case of one found hanged or fallen on his sword as requiring circumstances showing that he himself produced the act, such as being found hanging in a room locked from within. Even then, unless his settled intent had been established beforehand, we can attribute the act to sudden terror, mental disturbance, or another transient cause. But if his conduct, anger, distress, and preparations clearly showed that he was deliberately preparing for death, and he was then found dead in a manner clearly self-inflicted, that strong circumstantial proof is equivalent to an express declaration. See Rosh, end of Perek Elu Metziot, siman 141. Responsa Besamim Rosh, siman 345, writes that one reduced to complete poverty who declared before two witnesses that he despised life and then killed himself is not treated under the strict rule when he acted from overwhelming suffering, anxiety, pain, or extreme poverty. Har Avel, branch 11, questions this ruling and notes that it does not bear the authentic stamp of Rosh. Chatam Sofer likewise writes that the case of R. Chanina ben Teradyon in Avodah Zarah proves that even extreme suffering does not remove the formal classification, thereby rejecting Besamim Rosh on this point. See Beit Ephraim, Yoreh Deah siman 76.
