**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Even his sons, and so forth.</b> This follows Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel, who says even his son and daughter are believed, unlike R. Yose. Although Rava rules like R. Yose and the Gemara later says that if all the owners stand outside and the firstborn enters whole and emerges injured there is no concern, Tur cites this statement. Beit Yosef asks that Rava said it according to his own view, whereas we rule like Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel, and Rambam therefore omitted it. In my opinion, the case still teaches a practical novelty even according to Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel. His leniency regarding sons and household members applies where no obvious indication of trickery exists. But if all household members leave and at exactly that moment the firstborn enters and comes out injured, then if we knew one household member remained inside we would certainly suspect deliberate manipulation. The novelty is only that we do not suspect someone secretly remained hidden there. This remains relevant even according to Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel.

<b>I showed this blemish, and so forth.</b> The Mishnah says that a kohen is believed to say, "I showed this firstborn to a sage and it is a permanent blemish." Rav Yehudah further says a kohen is believed to say, "An Israelite gave me this firstborn already blemished." Rosh holds that in both cases the kohen must identify the sage or donor by name, so that fear of verification creates credibility. Rambam appears to distinguish. In the first case he says that if one witness testifies the blemish arose on its own and the kohen says, "I showed it to an expert and he permitted it," the kohen is believed because we do not suspect him of slaughtering sacred property outside the Temple, a grave offense carrying karet. In the second case, where the kohen says an Israelite gave it to him already blemished, Rambam explains that he is believed because the matter can be checked and he fears that the owners will be asked and will say the animal was unblemished when given. This implies that the identity of the Israelite is already known in the second case. The real question is only whether the animal was blemished when transferred. In the first case, however, Rambam does not rely on fear of verification. He relies on the severity of the offense and therefore still requires testimony that the blemish arose on its own. This explains why even identifying the expert would not eliminate the need for such testimony, because perhaps the expert only ruled that the defect was a permanent blemish provided it arose naturally. The kohen could still be suspected of having created it. Thus Rambam's ruling is consistent with Rashi.
