כמו דפסק בסימן י"ז דאי מהימן ליה העובד כוכבים אסור הכי נמי יש לדון בזה אפילו באינו מסיח לפי תומו:
**** ENGLISH ****Therefore if he transgressed and used. Explanation: permitted matter that is not sharp; but if it is a sharp thing, certainly forbidden, as above seif 3; and the reason we establish gentile vessels as not ben yomo — the Tur wrote it is because of a double doubt: doubt whether they used it today, and even if they used it today, perhaps they used it in something that spoils; and it is written in the Tur: and according to this the same applies to our vessels — it is a double doubt, we rely to be lenient — end of his words; and so it is written in Shulchan Aruch nearby seif 7; and in Darkei Moshe in Hagahat Shach it is written: regarding a Jew's cooking there is remedy through asking, and if he does not know he is penalized — end of his words. And it seems to me that penalty applies only to that Jew whose pot it is and he should have known; but regarding another, penalty does not apply, as above siman 99 — end of Darkei Moshe. And Rashal wrote in chapter Kol HaBasar siman 65: it seems to me that even though stam gentile vessels are not ben yomo and also presumably one need not ask whether it is ben yomo or what they cooked in it if it is something not spoiled; nevertheless if he asked them and they told him it is ben yomo, he should also ask what they cooked in it; and if it is something not spoiled, or he is in a situation where he cannot know from them what they cooked in it — it is not fit to permit, since there is no double doubt here; and even though we are not concerned for a gentile's words — neither to forbid nor to permit — that is regarding something that was already in presumption of kashrut; but here the presumption that stam gentile vessels are absorbed with prohibition has worsened, only through double doubt it comes to permit; therefore we rely on his word. And furthermore that which we do not rely on a gentile's words — this is where he comes to improve his merchandise, saying they are orlah, as stated in the Gemara — end of his words. And this does not seem so to my humble opinion: what he wrote that after he asked and he told him it is ben yomo there is no double doubt here — behold certainly there is a double doubt here: doubt perhaps he lies in saying he used it today, for stam a gentile is presumed to lie, as stated in chapter Chazakat HaBatim regarding one compelled for his donkey; and from scripture: whose mouth speaks falsehood, etc.; and furthermore what he wrote that here presumption of kashrut worsened regarding the vessel — what do you see that we follow the vessel's presumption, follow the presumption of the dish cooked in it that has presumption of kashrut; and this is exactly like the sugya in first chapter of Chullin: establish the knife on its presumption and say it was not spoiled; on the contrary establish the animal on its presumption and say it was not slaughtered; and we answer: the knife was impaired, the animal was not impaired; and here likewise exactly so regarding the dish there is no adverse factor. And what he wrote that we do not rely on a gentile's words — specifically when he intends to improve his merchandise — it is true that at the end of Yevamot it is so: a gentile who brings fruits to market and says these fruits are orlah, etc. — he is not believed neither to forbid nor to permit, except he intended to improve his merchandise; the Gemara needed this lest you say since he harms himself and says they are orlah, certainly he spoke truth — to this it said on the contrary that he intends to improve his merchandise; but otherwise certainly a gentile is not believed at all, even to forbid, even where improving merchandise does not apply he says so, for even a Jew is not believed to forbid his fellow's property unless it is in his hand — and so it is stated in Mordechai chapter Rabbi Yishmael regarding a maidservant who said the wine found is what she drew; and he wrote: and if because of the maidservant's statement there is no concern, for even a Jew is not believed regarding something not in his hand now — end of his words. However it seems to me: if the gentile says thus innocently by himself without any question, and the matters appear he did not intend regarding a Jew — then one should be stringent and forbid the dish of a Jew who cooks in it; and proof for this from what is written in siman 113 seif 2, when the gentile says unprompted that a Jew commanded him to cut the blemish that is forbidden. And one should still discuss this, as ruled in siman 17: if the gentile is believed to him it is forbidden — likewise one should discuss this even when he does not speak unprompted.

**** ENGLISH ****But for an hour one need not be concerned. This is from Mordechai's words that we will cite nearby, who wrote thus regarding knives: specifically if he left them with the gentile half a day or more; but for an hour — no — end of his words. And it is difficult, for in siman 136 the Tur wrote: even if they stood there only one hour they require kashering; and in Issur VeHeter general rule 33 he distinguishes: if he sends from outside the city — then the gentile is not afraid, forbidden even for an hour; but from house to house he is afraid for an hour and it is permitted even l'chatchila, unless it stood there half a day. And Maharach wrote: there is a dispute here — one teaches this, one does not teach that; and in my humble opinion it appears that is it written in the Gemara that half a day or for an hour are distinguished? Rather the essential matter is everything according to its context: regarding what is its way to use frequently, even incidentally, such as a wine vessel that a person is apt to drink at every hour — there is concern that in the short time it was with the gentile he drank wine in it; therefore they were stringent in siman 136 even for an hour; not so a vessel in which they cook or an eating knife, whose way is not to use except at mealtime, and a person's way is not to eat except a fixed measure per day — therefore one should not be stringent except when it stood like half a day; and some say half a day, but a large measure one can estimate that in that time he used it for eating; and this is a matter learned from its context — the Tur did not write to be stringent for an hour except in siman 136 that deals with wine vessels; so it seems to my humble opinion.

**** ENGLISH ****And all this when he wishes to use, etc. This is from Mordechai's words in chapter Ein Ma'amidin: stam vessels of gentiles are not bat yoma; and I said to him: behold this is a Torah doubt; one may say it is a double doubt — doubt whether they used it today; and if you say they used, perhaps they used in something that spoils; and according to this, when they bring knives to sharpen in the craftsman's house and return them the same day — this reason of double doubt does not apply, because the same day they returned them and there is no room to say doubt because he did not use at all, for certainly he is accustomed to use since no Jew stands over him; therefore he must kasher it; and so Rabbenu Tam, who requires that the Jew go to sharpen it, and he was more stringent with them than gentile vessels themselves; and specifically when he left them half a day in the gentile's house or more; but for an hour — no; however those knives that gentile servants carry in a Jew's house here and there — those one should permit from double doubt like gentile vessels; and it is fit to be stringent. They asked Rabbi Shalom of Bunburk only regarding a Jew who baked in a gentile's house and forgot the pot that they heated water in it in the gentile's house until the next day; and he answered: it seems to me that even though we taught, etc., and all the more so with the gentile — we may say he used them; however one may say establish the pot on its presumption and say prohibition was not used in it; and even if he used, it is spoiled that is not bat yoma; and stam gentile vessels we establish thus, as Rabbenu Tam's words — end of his words. And Issur VeHeter general rule 33 wrote: these Mordechai words at the beginning regarding knives, more stringent than gentile cooking — presumably he speaks b'dieved, that if he cut boiling meat with that knife it is as if it is a gentile's ben yomo vessel; for otherwise what distinction is there — behold even a gentile's vessel requires hagalah l'chatchila; and if he forgot an earthenware bowl at home he should break it — end of his words; it implies that even with double doubt in a Jew's vessel left in a gentile's house it is forbidden even b'dieved; and so Mordechai's implication from saying however those knives, etc., should be permitted from double doubt — implies above from it double doubt does not help for Rabbenu Tam's stringency; and it is difficult why Rabbenu Tam in truth was stringent with double doubt, for behold throughout the Torah double doubt helps; and in Torat Chayim general rule 59 he wrote that Rabbenu Tam was stringent l'chatchila in this more than l'chatchila with a gentile's vessel, but b'dieved permitted; and we do not see this at all, since both are forbidden l'chatchila — what stringency in one more than the other? And furthermore we already proved from Mordechai that in Rabbenu Tam's stringency double doubt does not help at all even b'dieved, except he permitted b'dieved only regarding gentile servants in a Jew's house specifically. And it seems to my humble opinion: every double doubt that is permitted b'dieved is after the double doubt occurred; but this Jew who intentionally leaves his knives in a gentile's house and relies on there being double doubt — it is fit to be stringent; and according to this, if he forgot his knife in a gentile's house it is permitted where there is double doubt; and on this basis Mordechai's words afterward in the name of Rabbi Shalom of Bunburk to permit the pot from double doubt are resolved; and at first glance it contradicts what Mordechai first said in the name of Rabbenu Tam to be stringent even with double doubt; and Beit Yosef end of this siman wrote that Rabbi Shalom permits since he returned them the next day — there is double doubt; and above he returned them the same day and there is only one doubt — and this is impossible to say, since we already proved that even with double doubt Rabbenu Tam is stringent. And Maharach wrote that Rabbi Shalom disagrees with that above and we do not hold thus; and Mordechai's language does not imply so, for he wrote the matters without dispute; rather according to what I wrote it is reconciled: Rabbenu Tam was stringent only when leaving intentionally; but when forgetting it is like a gentile's vessel and permitted with double doubt; and Rabbi Shalom deals with forgetting, not as Issur VeHeter wrote to be stringent even when forgetting; except nevertheless one may say that even Rabbi Shalom did not permit the pot except if he cooked in it b'dieved afterward — end of my humble opinion; that even with double doubt l'chatchila kashering is required since it is possible in this; but in earthenware that requires breaking, l'chatchila is also permitted, for it is considered b'dieved; and we find such a distinction in chapter Chomer BaKodesh (22b), distinguishing between earthenware and other vessels; and even though this is according to Beit Shammai and Beit Hillel, who are lenient in all vessels — nevertheless Beit Shammai's reasoning is true where one must be stringent, such as here; and we say we do not be stringent entirely; and further proof from what the Tur wrote siman 142: a new oven that they heated with forbidden wood — permitted l'chatchila to bake in it, etc., for it is considered b'dieved since it requires demolition — end of his words; and so too here, so it seems to my humble opinion. And all the above is with double doubt; but where there is no double doubt, such as if the gentile returned them the same day — forbidden by law, for there is only one doubt, as Mordechai wrote: there is no room to say perhaps he did not use; and if you say he used, perhaps in something spoiled — for every vessel, stam they use it, and this is a regular doubt and is not considered a doubt at all, as Torat Chayim siman 171 wrote regarding wormy wheat that every regular doubt is not considered a doubt at all; and even though in siman 84 seif 9 I wrote that even a regular doubt is considered there for double doubt — nevertheless here it is more regular since Mordechai did not wish to count it as double doubt; and if so it is forbidden here b'dieved when returning them the same day; and Beit Yosef in this siman wrote in the name of Orach Chaim in the name of Ra'ah: our bowls that stood in a gentile's house without a sign — leave it overnight and it is permitted — end of his words; from that he does not distinguish how long they stood in the gentile's house and when he returned them — it implies in every manner he says leave and it is permitted; and he disagrees with Mordechai above; and it appears we establish like Mordechai: one, that Mordechai is a famous later decisor and in most of his matters we practice accordingly; and furthermore Ra'ah's words are astonishing, for behold in a gentile's vessel as well it is permitted b'dieved specifically, and we do not say leave them overnight from a decree lest he use today — here likewise similarly; and in Torat Chayim general rule 59 he wrote that Ra'ah deals where he did not return them the same day — there is double doubt, then standing helps; and it appears nevertheless Ra'ah's words are not for halachah: if he deals with a matter where Rabbenu Tam's stringency applies, who was stringent even with double doubt in a Jew's vessel left with a gentile — certainly Ra'ah's words are void against Rabbenu Tam's words; and if he deals with a matter where Rabbenu Tam's stringency does not apply — namely like the incident of Rabbi Shalom that we recalled — then standing is not needed and the ruling is like Rabbi Shalom; and one should not permit based on what Beit Yosef brings in siman 136 in the name of Ran in the name of Ra'ah and his teacher Ramban regarding wine vessels, that b'dieved a Jew's vessel that was in a gentile's house is permitted as long as there is doubt whether he used it or not, from the reason that even if he certainly used it, it only forbids because of a decree — on the contrary, from there is proof to forbid, for there since the prohibition is only from a decree it is like double doubt here automatically; where there is only one doubt it is forbidden even b'dieved; and in Torat Chayim there he rules it appears to him for law b'dieved permitted, for there is no verification that a gentile used it, even though regular use does not escape doubt — and this is not reasoning at all, as I brought proof above from Torat Chayim that it is not considered a doubt at all; and there is no power to distinguish from his own reasoning to be lenient in what Mordechai explicitly forbids; also what Rama wrote here there is only one doubt — perhaps he did not use it, etc. — he should not have written thus, for this is not a doubt at all as Mordechai wrote; rather he should have written: perhaps he used in something spoiled — this doubt Mordechai mentioned there and in the Tur in this siman regarding gentile vessels — end; it appears there is no leniency b'dieved even for great need, not as Rama wrote nearby and in a place of need one may be lenient b'dieved; rather even b'dieved forbidden. General conclusion: where there is double doubt, such as if they did not return the same day — if left intentionally the vessel should be forbidden and requires hagalah, and earthenware requires breaking; if forgotten the vessel should be permitted, only if it can be kashered he should kasher it, and earthenware is permitted; and likewise for wine vessels in siman 136; but where there is no double doubt, such as if returned the same day — forbid even b'dieved by law; namely if it is an eating vessel, the prohibition is only that it should stand with the gentile part of the day — a large measure that according to estimation of the mind he used it for forbidden eating; and if it is a drinking vessel like the wine vessel case siman 136 — it is forbidden l'chatchila even for an hour — so it seems to my humble opinion; and Maharach elaborated on this law and raised what he raised; and what seemed to my humble opinion I wrote.
