<b>And if they are not.</b> This follows R. Eliezer, whose opinion is established as halachah in Pesachim 48b and in the first chapter of Niddah. Although the Yerushalmi there cites R. Yona and R. Chiyya in the name of R. Yochanan, and R. Yose son of R. Imi in the name of R. Yochanan, that this applies only where the loaves adhered, and records one version that a basket combines but an oven does not and another that an oven combines but a basket does not, with R. Yochanan saying that if they adhered then each combines but if not then neither combines, the Bavli in Pesachim does not conclude this way. Rav Yosef and Abaye follow the Bavli's conclusion, and we rule accordingly. See Tosafot there and Rash. According to this, even R. Chanina's view cannot rest on a different textual tradition. Rather, he holds that a basket combines even without actual adhesion. From Rambam, Tur, and the other poskim, who also rule like the first Tanna, it appears that their text reads "even one who removes," so R. Eliezer does not disagree with the first Tanna. His requirement of adhesion refers specifically to loaves in a basket. Thus the first Tanna requires the doughs to adhere while still dough, while R. Eliezer adds that even afterward there can be combination, and R. Chanina says no adhesion is needed in that case. Nevertheless, actual touching is still required even in a basket according to R. Eliezer, as Semag and Rosh state explicitly, and Rash likewise writes. Although Rash questions this from the wording "the basket combines them" rather than "connects them," as in Menachot 24a, he himself rejects that objection. According to R. Yehoshua ben Levi even adhesion is required and the Gemara still uses the term "combines." R. Chanina disagrees only about adhesion. If even touching were unnecessary, what would be the meaning of saying that even ring-shaped loaves combine without touching, when adhesion is inherently impossible there? This also explains why the Mishnah at the beginning of chapter 4 says "and they touched one another." Mere touching alone is otherwise insufficient for the first Tanna, so the phrase is needed also for R. Eliezer. Likewise in chapter 2 the Mishnah's wording concerning doughs that touched is not superfluous; it teaches R. Eliezer's view, since he disputes only the requirement of actual adhesion. Regarding an oven, Rambam rules that it does not combine, unlike R. Yochanan. This also appears from the fact that the Gemara says the halachah follows R. Eliezer but does not separately say it follows R. Yochanan. Although Rashba disagrees, Rambam's view is primary. The Yerushalmi's statement that when they adhered both basket and oven combine merely explains the competing views there. Since we rule only like R. Eliezer, even adhesion in an oven does not create combination.

<b>If there is.</b> Rosh and Tur write this. Great authorities became entangled in this law. See Tosafot, Niddah 7a, Rash on the Mishnah there, Tosafot Pesachim 46b, Rosh and Mordechai there, and Sefer HaTerumah, who discusses the matter at great length. Nevertheless, Rosh's position is primary, and the Mishnah in chapter 3 of Ma'aser Sheni supports him, where it distinguishes before and after sealing the containers. Rash likewise writes so. The statement at the end of chapter 2 that one places less than an egg's bulk in order to take from that which is mukaf is explained by the Yerushalmi as a case where one is particular that the substances not mix, as will be explained below. The cases in the last chapter of Tevul Yom and first chapter of Niddah concerning making things mukaf are likewise due to that consideration, unlike Tosafot. Rash, Rosh, and Sefer HaTerumah write so. There are three laws here. If produce is not in any vessel at all, even touching is unnecessary, and even if the piles are distant from one another the house combines them. If they are in open containers, they require hakafah, meaning proximity with the vessels touching one another. This is the meaning of taking from that which is mukaf, as when one may not tithe from one barrel for another, as Rashi explains. If the containers are sealed, even touching does not help, as the Mishnah in chapter 3 of Ma'aser Sheni teaches. Rambam and Shulchan Aruch rule likewise in siman 331, seif 26. The Tosefta in chapter 3 of Terumot follows the same structure: scattered produce can combine because it is not in vessels; sacks of grain, because they are vessels, combine when within one hakafah, meaning when touching one another; barrels are different because even when touching they retain an airspace of a tefach above, and there are further restrictions on Yom Tov. Similarly, unsealed barrels arranged in a cellar are considered within one hakafah until they are closed. This explains the rule there in seif 25 that produce in another house is not combined, whereas otherwise a single house can combine it. The Yerushalmi at the end of chapter 3 of Ma'aser Sheni says that Beit Shammai dispute even the unsealed-container rule and require opening them. R. Chanina challenges Beit Shammai from five sacks on a threshing floor, where surely one may separate terumah and ma'aser from one for another. At the end of chapter 2 of Challah, regarding placing less than an egg's bulk, the Yerushalmi asks why the house does not combine them and answers that where a person is particular that items not mix, the house does not combine them, while where he is not particular, it does. Pure dough and impure dough are treated as something one is particular not to mix. Sefer HaTerumah unnecessarily emends "house" to "vessel." The same principle appears in the Yerushalmi in Pesachim concerning throwing something into the airspace of a house: the house airspace combines, while the floor itself does not, because something one is particular not to mix is not combined by the house. We derive further that just as things one is particular not to mix do not combine for challah, so too one may not separate from one for the other without actual touching, as stated at the end of chapter 2. There, mukaf means actual contact, though this stricter requirement is needed only in such a case. The Yerushalmi in chapter 2 of Terumot further discusses a single winepress or threshing floor as one unit, provided the threshing area is held together as one continuous space. Even straw or chaff between them does not necessarily interrupt. When they asked R. Yose about impurity or a cloth between the piles, he challenged whether that truly creates a single mass. The correct reading appears to be that five sacks on a threshing floor may indeed be tithed from one for another, as in the Tosefta and the parallel challenge in Ma'aser Sheni, proving that the sacks do not interrupt. The Yerushalmi's requirement of proximity without a vessel means only that there must not be an intervening different species breaking the continuity. This is the meaning of being within one tefisah, comparable to the usage in Chullin 107b. Even within a single container, an intervening species can interrupt. Thus, if a basket contains cabbage above and below with another species in the middle, one may not separate from the upper cabbage for the lower unless they are within one hakafah, while if there is open air between them they may combine. The Tosefta in chapter 3 of Terumot says similarly that R. Yose regards one threshing complex as one unit. One press with two pits, two presses with one pit, or two presses with two pits can all be tithed from one another when within one continuous unit, but not otherwise. If one gathers vegetables and places them in the garden, one may separate from one for all; if another species is placed between them, one separates from each separately. Where many species are in one basket, with cabbage above and below and another species between, one should not separate from the upper for the lower unless they are within one hakafah. Sefer HaTerumah's explanation that "airspace of the house" means literally suspended in the air has no basis. The intent is rather to exclude produce placed in a depression in the floor, where it is not treated as situated in the house's combining airspace.
