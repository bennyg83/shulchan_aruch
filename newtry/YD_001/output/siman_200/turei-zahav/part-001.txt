<b>She muddies them with her feet. </b>This requires further study, for in Orach Chaim siman 74 the Shulchan Aruch rules that women may pray while wearing a robe even though there is no separation below the heart. It will be explained below that even clear water is like wearing a robe.

<b>She covers herself, etc. </b>The Derishah discusses this law at length and writes that our women, who recite the blessing over immersion while they are still naked in the water and do not muddy the water, do not act properly, even though they clasp their arms around their bodies. For according to what Maharam Yaffe wrote, the reason the water must be muddy is so that their hearts should not see their ervah. Clasping their arms around themselves would therefore be effective to create a separation between the heart and the ervah. Yet regarding a woman there is no concern that her heart sees her ervah, since her ervah is situated very low, as the Beit Yosef wrote in the name of the Orchos Chaim in Orach Chaim siman 74. This is his language: “It appears that women may recite blessings and pray while wearing their robes even though there is no separation below the chest, because their ervah is situated very low. The requirement that their faces be pressed to the ground applies only when they are naked, so that their ervah will be covered,” end quote. This implies that regarding women the prohibition is that they stand naked without their ervah being covered, and it is improper conduct to do so because of the honor of the Shechinah. If so, what is accomplished by clasping the arms? If the concern is that the heart sees the ervah, a woman does not require a separation. Perforce, the concern is the honor of the Shechinah, that she should not stand naked. What, then, is accomplished by a separation between the heart and the ervah? This is the substance of his words there.

It appears to me, however, that women who do this by clasping their arms act properly even l'chatchila. Even without clasping their arms, they have an opinion upon which to rely. His statement that there is a prohibition against standing naked because of the honor of the Shechinah is astonishing. Why must he seek a reason based on the honor of the Shechinah? There is an explicit Torah prohibition because of “so that no nakedness shall be seen among you.” As written regarding separating challah in Yoreh De'ah siman 328, a man is forbidden to separate challah and recite the blessing because his ervah is exposed. Likewise, wherever it is forbidden to pray facing another person’s ervah, the same applies to facing one’s own ervah when it is exposed, even if one’s heart is covered. This is obvious even to young schoolchildren.

Rather, it appears clear and is proven that when a person stands in water, even if the water is clear, there is no concern of exposed ervah. This is unlike the view of the Derishah, who holds that clear water is considered as though she were standing naked. There is an explicit proof from the end of the chapter Mi SheMeiso. The Mishnah teaches, “He shall cover himself in water and recite Shema.” The Gemara asks, “But his heart sees his ervah,” and answers that the Mishnah speaks of muddy water, which is like solid ground. Were clear water considered exposed ervah, why did the questioner specifically mention his heart? Whether the heart may see the ervah is itself a dispute in that chapter. The Gemara should instead have asked that he transgresses “so that no nakedness shall be seen among you,” meaning the prohibition of exposed ervah, even if his heart is covered.

It is therefore clear that whenever his ervah is covered by water, even clear water, it is not considered exposed. Although one who intentionally looks through the water toward his ervah can see it, nevertheless, when one does not intentionally look toward that place through the water, there is no concern of exposed ervah. We find a precise parallel in Tosafos to Nedarim 30a, which I cited in siman 217, seif 36. Fish in the water see the sun, but the sun is not considered to see them because they are covered by the water. The same applies here. So too the Beit Yosef wrote in Orach Chaim siman 206 in the name of the Rashba: “But surely his heart sees the ervah, meaning that his heart and his ervah are together within the water. There is no concern that his eyes see the ervah, because his eyes are outside the water and he looks outward, so he does not see the ervah,” end quote. Thus, even the concern that the heart sees the ervah applies only when the heart and the ervah are both in the water, as implied by the Mishnah’s words, “He shall cover himself in water.” If the heart is outside the water, there is no concern.

This is also proven from the words of Rabbeinu Tam cited by the Beit Yosef in Orach Chaim siman 74. When he bathed in clear hot water and wished to drink, he covered himself with a garment from below his heart so that his heart would not see the ervah, end quote. If standing naked in clear water constituted exposed ervah, what did he accomplish by covering his heart? The Derishah writes that Rabbeinu Tam was seated inside a vessel and therefore there was no concern of exposed ervah. This is entirely incorrect. Even one seated in a vessel with his ervah exposed certainly violates a complete prohibition when there is no water, for he himself sees his ervah.

A proof may be brought from the first chapter of Sukkah, at the end of 10b. Shmuel said that one who sleeps naked beneath a canopy that is ten handbreadths high may put his head outside the canopy and recite Shema, because he holds that it is permitted for the heart to see the ervah. So wrote Tosafos. According to our ruling, then, it is forbidden, and that case is exactly like the vessel in which Rabbeinu Tam bathed. Rather, it is clear that the prohibition of “so that no nakedness shall be seen among you” applies only when one’s ervah is uncovered outside the water. In that case it is forbidden to recite facing it unless one turns one’s face away, as explained in the Shulchan Aruch, Orach Chaim siman 75. When one is in the water, however, there is no concern of exposed ervah, and it is as though one turned one’s face away. There remains only the prohibition that one’s heart sees the ervah, comparable to one who covered himself with a garment but did not create a separation between his heart and his ervah.

Accordingly, a woman who immerses and stands in the water after immersion certainly benefits from the separation she creates with her hands between her heart and her ervah, as the Shulchan Aruch rules in Orach Chaim siman 74. This is completely permitted. At first glance, it would be preferable for her heart not to enter the water, so that her heart and her ervah would not both be within the water, similar to what I cited in the name of the Rashba. Nevertheless, it is more modest for her entire body to be covered by water up to her neck, as the Sha'arei Dura writes in the laws of niddah. She should then clasp her arms around herself to create a separation, since the Shulchan Aruch already ruled in Orach Chaim siman 74, seif 36, that this constitutes a separation.

Those women who stand in the water while reciting the blessing and do not separate between their hearts and their ervah with their hands also have an opinion upon which to rely. This is the Orchos Chaim cited by the Beit Yosef, whom I mentioned above, who holds that regarding a woman there is no concept at all of her heart seeing her ervah, since her ervah is situated very low. She may therefore recite a blessing while wearing a covering garment even when there is no separation beneath the garment between her heart and her ervah. He explains the Gemara’s statement that when separating challah her face must be pressed to the ground as referring to exposed ervah when she is naked. It is obvious that although there is no concern for a woman that her heart sees her ervah, even while she is standing, there is nevertheless a concern of exposed ervah while she stands naked.

This is stated in the first chapter of Sotah 8a: according to R' Yehudah, a woman is stoned while covered in front and behind because her entire body is ervah. Rashi explains that her private area can be seen from both the front and the back. Therefore, at the end of the chapter Mi SheMeiso the Gemara requires a woman who separates challah to be seated. Even sitting is not sufficient because her buttocks are exposed, and therefore the Sages required that her face be pressed to the ground, meaning pressed very closely to the ground so that even her buttocks will be covered.

Accordingly, when a woman is standing and wears a garment that covers her ervah and her buttocks, there is no prohibition according to the view of the Orchos Chaim cited by the Beit Yosef, even though there is no separation between her heart and her ervah. My father-in-law, of blessed memory, understood that according to this Orchos Chaim there is no concern of exposed ervah at all while she is standing, and that the requirement that her face be pressed to the ground serves only to cover her buttocks. He therefore found difficulty with the Gemara’s initial assumption in the chapter Mi SheMeiso that the buttocks do not constitute ervah. Why, then, must she be seated? She should be permitted even while standing, since her heart does not see her ervah. He therefore rejected the view of the Orchos Chaim from the halacha.

He was not precise in this. There is certainly a prohibition of exposed ervah even while she is standing, as we explained. Rather, the Orchos Chaim teaches that whenever the ervah and the buttocks are covered by a garment, even though there is no separation between the heart and the ervah, it is nevertheless permitted, just as it is permitted when she is seated with her face pressed to the ground. Since her ervah is situated very low, there is no prohibition of her heart seeing the ervah.

It appears clear that the Rosh also agrees with the Orchos Chaim, for the Tur writes in this siman in his name: “So too wrote my father and teacher, the Rosh, of blessed memory: When she removes her garments, while she is still standing in her robe she shall recite the blessing, then remove the robe and immerse,” end quote. He was concerned only with exposed ervah. When she is wearing a robe, that is sufficient even though there is no separation between her heart and her ervah, for one who wears a robe ordinarily has no belt creating a separation. He did not require her to wear a belt. It is therefore clear that he agrees with the Orchos Chaim cited above that regarding a woman there is no concern of her heart seeing her ervah.

This also resolves the wording of the Tur. He elaborated when citing the words of the Rosh and did not write briefly, “So too wrote my father and teacher, the Rosh, of blessed memory,” as is his usual manner. The matter is clear. Immediately beforehand, the Tur cited the Ra'avad, who holds that she should recite the blessing before immersion, and that if she did not recite it before immersion she must muddy the water. This must be because of the concern that her heart sees her ervah, for the Ra'avad does not agree with the Orchos Chaim cited above. The Tur then writes that the Rosh likewise holds that she should recite the blessing before immersion, agreeing in this respect with the Ra'avad and disagreeing with Rabbeinu Yitzchak and Halachos Gedolos, who wrote that she should recite the blessing after immersion. However, the Rosh does not agree with the Ra'avad’s statement that she must muddy the water. Rather, it is sufficient that she recite the blessing while she is still wearing her robe, and we are not concerned that her heart sees her ervah.

Since the Orchos Chaim and the Rosh both hold that the concern of her heart seeing her ervah does not apply to a woman, and the Shulchan Aruch has already ruled accordingly in Orach Chaim siman 74, who can object to a woman who relies upon this? Whenever she is in water, even clear water, her ervah and buttocks are automatically covered as though she were wearing a garment covering her in front and behind. There is no concern of exposed ervah, as we proved above, and we need not be concerned that her heart sees her ervah. Nevertheless, as the preferable practice, she should clasp her arms around herself to create a separation between her heart and her ervah while she is in the water. She will then have fulfilled her obligation according to all opinions.

Although the Rama writes here that the custom is to cover herself with her garment or robe, perhaps that was the custom in his time. Today, however, this is not the custom, as the Derishah testified, though he wrote that the women do not act properly. We too have heard that women are not careful even to clasp their arms around themselves to create a separation between the heart and the ervah. It appears that this too accords with the halacha. Nevertheless, l'chatchila she should clasp her arms around herself as we explained, for there is no basis to question this practice. So it appears to me.
