But if a night passed, it is permitted. Beit Yosef writes this because many authorities hold that an overnight stay impairs absorbed flavor, and Semak also holds that one need not be stringent regarding water heated in a utensil that had absorbed actual prohibition. By combining these two grounds for leniency, one may permit it. Shach and Pri Chadash write that the night must pass between cooking the prohibited food and heating the water. If the water was heated before that night elapsed, then even if a night later passed after the water was heated, it remains prohibited, unlike Torat Chattat. Their reasoning requires explanation. Ultimately there appear to be two possible grounds for leniency regarding the later cooked food: either Semak is correct, or Rashi and Rambam are correct that an overnight stay itself renders the absorbed flavor detrimental. Why should it matter when the water was heated? The answer appears to be that, as regards the food presently under discussion, these are not truly two independent uncertainties if both amount only to a single question whether the taste in this food is detrimental or beneficial. That would be one category of uncertainty. The distinction is that under the overnight-imparts-detriment theory, the heated water itself would already have been permitted, whereas under Semak's approach one begins counting from the original prohibited cooking even though the water itself may have been prohibited at the moment it was heated. Because the two possibilities have different legal consequences, they can combine as two doubts. But if the water was heated immediately, before the night passed, it was certainly prohibited at that time. Later, after a night passes, the only two supposed doubts concerning the new food reduce to the single question whether the taste is now detrimental. This explains their position clearly.

I found support for being stringent regarding water heated in such a utensil from the law of the utensils of Midian. The Torah prohibited only a utensil that was ben yomo. It is difficult to imagine that the war against Midian, the return to the camp, and the command concerning purging utensils all occurred within one day. If the Midianite camp had been so close, why were twelve thousand soldiers specially dispatched and why does Scripture describe them as going out to war? Rather, the command to purge was a newly stated ruling transmitted by Elazar in Moshe's name. Before that ruling was taught, Jews had presumably cooked and heated water in the captured Midianite utensils. That renewed the favorable flavor and kept the utensils in a ben yomo state. Therefore purging was required. Even according to the view concerning secondary imparted taste, this is still a secondary imparted taste originating in prohibition, as Shach explains above, and the Torah required purging. According to Issur VeHeter, which holds that when the prohibition had not yet become known we do not apply the rule that the entire mixture becomes forbidden with regard to ordinary prohibitions, this also answers Rabbenu Tam's difficulty regarding purging very large cauldrons. The water did not become entirely forbidden because the law was not yet known, so only the actual proportion of prohibited absorbed flavor had to be calculated, and even in very large cauldrons the amount originally absorbed was not necessarily great.

Semak's leniency is explained by saying that the secondary absorption should not be treated more stringently than the original prohibition itself, and that one counts from the time the prohibited food was cooked. At first glance this is difficult: once the water becomes entirely prohibited, if that water stood for many days it would remain prohibited. Why should its subsequent absorption into the utensil be dated back to the original cooking? One must say that because the rule of chatichah na'aseit neveilah with ordinary prohibitions is rabbinic, Chazal did not impose the stringency so far as to make the secondary prohibition last longer than the original prohibition. Yet Beit Yosef does not appear to accept this reasoning universally, since regarding meat and milk, which he treats as a Torah prohibition, he does not say that material already prohibited as meat and milk thereafter becomes like an ordinary prohibition. How then can he combine Semak's view as a valid lenient factor? It is also unclear where Semak derived such a distinction. It therefore seems more likely that the lenient authorities hold that with liquid mixed into liquid we do not say chatichah na'aseit neveilah, as explained at length above in siman 92. If so, there is a genuine double doubt in every form of the case, including Rama's case: perhaps liquid does not become entirely prohibited, in which case the water prohibits only according to the actual absorbed proportion once the original taste is impaired, and perhaps an overnight stay itself impairs the taste.

Regarding the principle that an overnight stay impairs absorbed taste, Tosafot and especially Rosh at the end of Avodah Zarah cite Rashi and Rabbenu Tam that an overnight stay is detrimental. Rabbenu Tam was uncertain whether the relevant point is the beginning of the night, the end of the night, or an entire night without cooking. He sought proof from the law that sacred utensils require breaking, scouring, and rinsing. If the beginning of the night alone impaired the taste, why would those procedures be necessary? When morning comes and the sacrificial residue becomes notar, the absorbed taste should already be detrimental. This would imply that the entire night must pass without cooking. Rosh rejects the proof, saying the requirement there may be a Scriptural decree. Ran similarly answers that although the flavor expelled from the utensil is impaired, the absorbed remnant within the utensil itself remains prohibited, and the Torah required purging so that no notar taste remain. Re'em in Parashat Tzav gives another difficult answer, that because the status of notar preceded the deterioration by a brief moment, the obligation of scouring and rinsing already took effect.

I find a difficulty with all these answers from Avodah Zarah, where the Gemara asks why spits and grills of sacred property are purged with boiling water whereas the utensils of gentiles require heating in fire. If the sacred utensils contained only detrimental taste and required purging merely by Scriptural decree, what is the question? One could answer that the Gemara did not yet know the requirement was only a decree, but that remains forced because the verse says "scour," perhaps meaning complete removal of all absorbed substance. More seriously, in Zevachim the Gemara says according to Rabbi that "scouring and rinsing" refers to cold washing, because hot purging needs no verse and follows from the laws of other prohibitions. Yet ordinary prohibitions are permitted when their taste is detrimental. Here, according to these answers, sacred utensils require treatment despite detrimental taste because of a special decree or because the absorbed notar itself remains. How then can Rabbi derive the hot purging requirement from ordinary prohibitions? The same problem applies to Ran and Re'em: the fact that the expelled flavor does not improve food would ordinarily eliminate the need to purge, and their novel rule about absorbed notar cannot be learned from other prohibitions.

There is another difficulty in Zevachim. The Gemara asks whether a sacrifice cooked while suspended in the airspace of an oven, without touching and absorbing into the oven, requires the oven to be broken, and leaves the matter unresolved. How could it even be assumed that cooking without absorption imposes a Torah requirement? According to Rabbi, the verse itself concerns cold washing and the hot purging rule is learned from ordinary prohibitions, where no such principle exists. Rabbi and the Sages would not appear to disagree about that. This may explain Rambam's ruling, which records the unresolved question only regarding breaking an earthenware vessel. Lechem Mishneh asks why Rambam did not also record it regarding scouring and rinsing of metal vessels. Perhaps Rambam understood that, according to Rabbi, there is no source for such a rule regarding metal utensils, while the special Torah requirement to break an earthenware sacrificial utensil may extend even to cooking without absorption.

Indeed, I am inclined to say that the concern about cooking without absorption is only rabbinic, lest actual absorption occur. This explains why Rambam records it as an unresolved question rather than automatically ruling stringently as a Torah doubt. Kesef Mishneh asks why he did not rule stringently. On this explanation, the question is rabbinic, and Rambam was uncertain whether the rabbinic doubt should be treated leniently or stringently in this context. One could perhaps say instead that the question was first formulated only after the destruction, as a theoretical inquiry, whereas in the Temple era it could have been experimentally clarified because broken sacred earthenware utensils were miraculously absorbed into the ground. If an oven in which cooking occurred without contact was broken and its shards were absorbed, that would prove breaking was required; if not, it was unnecessary. Hashem does not perform a miracle in support of falsehood. Rambam therefore states the unresolved law for our era but does not formulate it as a practical Torah doubt in Temple times.

This also resolves a difficulty from Taanit. Choni HaMe'agel said, "Go and bring in the ovens for the Korban Pesach so that they not dissolve in the rain." Rashi explains they were earthenware ovens. How could they use earthenware ovens if both kodshei kodashim and kodashim kalim cooked in earthenware require the vessel to be broken? There would not be enough earthenware in the world to continually replace so many ovens. If one says the Korban Pesach was roasted suspended without touching the oven wall, that itself was the unresolved question whether cooking without absorption requires breaking. The Yerushalmi implies the incident occurred already on the tenth of Adar, so these were apparently ovens from the prior year, not newly made immediately before Pesach. The Temple's ordinary oven was made of metal precisely so it would not require breaking. According to what I have written, however, cooking without absorption required breaking only by rabbinic decree lest actual absorption occur. With the Korban Pesach there was no such concern, because if the meat touched the earthenware wall that portion itself became prohibited. The decree was therefore unnecessary.

Returning to Rosh's difficulty: how can Rabbi derive purging sacred utensils from ordinary prohibitions if ordinary detrimental flavor is permitted? There is still another difficulty. Zevachim 96 derives from the word "it" that terumah utensils are excluded from the special law of scouring and rinsing, and the Gemara asks from the Mishnah that if terumah was cooked in a pot and afterward chullin was cooked there, the law depends on whether flavor is imparted. What is the question? Sacred utensils require treatment even when the absorbed taste is already detrimental, while terumah should be permitted in such a case according to the rule of noten ta'am lifgam. This question applies to all the earlier answers.

One strained answer is that these passages follow the authorities who hold that where even the smallest amount prohibits, detrimental taste remains prohibited because some prohibited substance is still present. Thus, according to the view that min bemino is never nullified, cooking a food of the same type in a utensil containing detrimental absorbed taste remains prohibited. Rabbi holds in Chullin 109 that min bemino is not nullified, and R. Yehudah likewise holds so. According to them, the Torah's requirement of scouring and rinsing need not be a special decree at all; it follows ordinary prohibition because the utensil could be used again with the same type of sacrificial meat. Accordingly, when the Gemara asks why "it" is needed to exclude terumah, the question concerns the same-type case. If the exclusion concerned different types, there would be no need for a verse because detrimental flavor is permitted even in sacred food. If it concerns the same type, however, terumah too should prohibit. This also answers the problem that, according to Rashi and Rambam, taste of the essence is not itself Torah law in all contexts, because min bemino according to R. Yehudah is certainly a Torah prohibition. Still, this answer is strained. Rosh himself does not accept the rule that a minute amount remains prohibited despite detrimental flavor, and I have elsewhere shown from Tosafot that even authorities who prohibit the smallest amount do not necessarily classify detrimental same-type flavor this way. The matter therefore remains difficult.

If, however, the beginning of the night alone creates deterioration, the earlier difficulties largely disappear because the Torah's requirement would address sacrifices cooked during the night before that point. Yet Tosafot and Rosh sought to prove from sacred utensils that the entire night must pass. How is that proof established? Zevachim states in the Mishnah that both kodshei kodashim and kodashim kalim require scouring and rinsing. This includes shelamim, which are the standard example of kodashim kalim and may be eaten for two days and one night. At the beginning of the second night, the absorbed flavor of the first day's shelamim would become notar. If the beginning of the night also immediately rendered it detrimental, one could no longer cook anything else in the utensil that night, and there would be no need for scouring. The Mishnah's general rule therefore proves that the beginning of the night alone is insufficient. But if a full night is required, the law is understandable. If shelamim were cooked shortly before the end of their second day and became notar at night, and another sacrifice still permitted that night was to be cooked in the vessel, purging would be required because a full night had not yet passed. That is the force of the proof.

I can add another proof against saying that only the end of the night causes deterioration while scouring is a special decree. Avodah Zarah says that sacred utensils do not require the stronger heating in fire because each day's cooking acts as purging for the prior day's cooking. The Gemara explains that after a chattat one cooks shelamim and so forth, such that each use purges the previous absorption. R. Tarfon likewise says that a pot first used at the beginning of a festival may be used throughout the festival because each day's use purges the last. But every festival includes Shabbos. What happens from Friday to Sunday? Shelamim slaughtered on Friday remain permitted through Motzaei Shabbos, when they become notar. Nothing can be cooked in the utensil on Shabbos that would purge them, and after they become notar one could not cook another sacrifice in the pot without first purging it. If, however, after the entire Shabbos day and the following night the absorbed flavor is already detrimental, Sunday morning presents no problem. On ordinary weekdays some sacred utensils can still contain improving flavor and therefore require scouring as a general rule, but on Sunday morning every vessel unused throughout Shabbos and the night has detrimental taste. In that case no scouring is required under ordinary law. Thus the statement that each day's cooking purges the prior day's use remains workable. This strongly supports the concept that overnight deterioration is the relevant mechanism and that the Torah did not create a unique requirement to purge detrimental flavor in every case.

I also found support for those who hold that a single overnight stay is enough from Zevachim 95b. The Gemara asks: if actual absorption is required, why was the Temple oven made of metal? Let it be made of earthenware, because if the offering does not touch it there is no absorption. The Gemara answers that the remnants of meal offerings were baked in it, creating absorption from the oil. One can ask what the original question was. Baked meal offerings themselves contain oil. Even the daily offering of the kohen gadol is mixed with oil according to some views. Rambam, who holds that the earthenware breaking requirement is specifically connected to a chattat, may avoid the issue because the poor person's chattat meal offering and the jealousy offering contain no oil, and the rabbinic prohibition against adding oil there would not have been the source of the ancient Temple oven's construction. But according to the other authorities, the question remains. The answer is that if an overnight stay impairs taste, ordinary meal offerings are baked by day and cannot be baked at night because they would become invalid through overnight remaining. Therefore, by morning the absorbed taste would already be impaired and the oven would not require breaking. The Gemara instead answers from the remnants of meal offerings eaten by kohanim, which could need to be baked at night after the kometz was offered when daytime was short. In that case the following morning might arrive before a full period of impairment. This gives those authorities a strong basis.

This also helps explain Rambam. Orach Chayyim discusses a baker's peel and implies that even dry chametz can impart an absorbed layer. If so, why does Zevachim need to mention oil in the meal-offering remnants? Even without oil, baking should create at least a surface absorption, and an earthenware utensil that absorbed even through pouring can require breaking. One can say Rashi and Tosafot either do not accept that law concerning the baker's peel, or hold such minimal absorption does not trigger the breaking requirement. Rambam, however, accepts that a baker's peel absorbs and also that pouring affects only a surface layer. He may therefore understand the Gemara's answer "remnants of meal offerings" without making oil the critical point. The Gemara mentions remnants rather than the original meal offerings because the original offerings were baked by day and the overnight stay would impair the taste; the remnants could be baked at night. Thus even without oil they can create the relevant absorption. This resolves the difficulty for Rambam.

Why did Tosafot, Rosh, and Ran not answer that the Torah required purging because kohanim might later cook sharp foods in those vessels, since sacred meat could be cooked with vinegar and spices and sharpness can restore impaired taste? Regarding spits and grills, however, roasting does not normally involve a sharp liquid, and certainly not in the Temple oven. More fundamentally, it appears they considered the sharp-food rule a later stringency rather than a Torah-level principle. The same is likely true here.

Accordingly, the doctrine that an overnight stay impairs absorbed flavor has considerable support. It also resolves several difficulties in the Temple-utensil sugyot, and the matter should be considered carefully.
