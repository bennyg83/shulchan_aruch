אך קשה להכ"מ הא הכל ספק א' אי מגופי' או מעלמא או מן עלי' דמ"ש מעלמ' או מעלי' הכל ספק א' אי דם נדה או דם טהור ומה לי דם חוטמה או דם עלי' ועמש"ל ברמ"א בסי' קפ"ז וזה לא שייך כאן וגם מתהפך לא הוי ולכן ברור כמש"ל בסי' קפ"ז דכאן חזקת אשה דהעמיד אשה בחזקתה חזקת טהרה מסייע עד שבעבור זה היא טהורה מהתורה א"כ בכל ס"ס דעלמא אפי' ס"ס כל דהוא מתירין דהא חזקה מסייע לה:
**** ENGLISH ****
That she is tamei. Rambam wrote in ch. 9 Avos HaTumah this impurity is from doubt lest this stain came from the inner chamber; Kessef Mishneh wondered, behold double doubt and we should permit: doubt from her body doubt not from body; if from body doubt from chamber doubt from upper story, tehorah; one may say nevertheless sages were stringent because it is a severe prohibition (and so Gemara 19a implies to be stringent on Torah words per Rashi lest we be lenient on full niddah); explain: since she has nothing to attribute to, they were stringent as if certainly from her body, only one doubt remains, end quote. Taz wrote one may say since majority of blood leaving woman comes from makor this is not considered doubt at all; do not challenge from Rama's double doubt in siman 187 seif 5 for there is wound and perhaps from sides, double doubt refers there; but in my opinion reason for double doubt: majority failed us since majority of women do not see from relations from makor, therefore true double doubt (as explained Ketubot 16 and Bava Batra 99); for this reason considered double doubt here since majority of women feel if they see blood, majority failed; unless for reasons Kessef Mishneh wrote; clear. But Kessef Mishneh is challenged: all is one doubt whether from body or elsewhere or from upper chamber, what difference elsewhere or upper chamber, all one doubt whether niddah blood or tehorah blood; what difference nose blood or upper chamber blood; and what Rama wrote in siman 187 does not apply here nor is it reversible; therefore clear as in siman 187 here woman's chazakah, we establish woman in her chazakah of taharah assists until for this she is tehorah from Torah; thus in every double doubt in the world even slightest double doubt we permit for chazakah assists her.

**** ENGLISH ****
The measure of a lentil is approximately the area occupied by four hairs, meaning two hairs across and two hairs lengthwise. For example, where hairs are arranged in sequence, one should not say that because there are four hairs from the first to the fourth there are two lentil-sized areas. Rather, the measure is determined by the space from which the hairs draw nourishment and grow. The distance from the first hair to the second is one interval. A lentil-sized area therefore consists of two such intervals across. Thus, from the first hair to the third is one lentil-sized area, and from the third to the fifth is a second. From the fifth to the sixth is only half a lentil-sized area. Accordingly, six hairs is not exact. One must add half an interval at one end and half an interval at the other, which together are equivalent to a seventh hair. Only seven hairs encompass three lentil-sized areas. Alternatively, one may describe it as six hairs together with half the space of a hair on the right and half on the left. The Perishah wrote this as well. The Minchas Yaakov elaborated with diagrams but added nothing, for the entire explanation already appears in the Perishah. He merely changed the wording, which made the matter harder to understand than the Perishah's formulation. Nevertheless, the matter remains strained and requires study.

It may be explained that Rambam was troubled by the following. Why did they permit colored garments during the campaign of Vespasian in order to render a woman pure for her husband? This implies that, were it not for that consideration, they would not have been concerned for the resulting neglect of procreation. Why, then, was a decree that otherwise ought to have been enacted set aside because of a limited concern for neglect of procreation? It must be that an earlier enactment already required women to have colored garments. Therefore, even during the campaign of Vespasian, they did not wish to uproot that earlier enactment entirely. It was sufficient to leave the matter optional, so that a woman was not required to wear colored garments. Originally, however, the enactment specifically favored colored garments. They later left the choice to the woman, but they did not wish entirely to abolish their own enactment and prohibit what they had previously instituted. The Gemara therefore asks, 'Who instituted this?' meaning that everyday practice shows women also wearing uncolored garments. The Gemara answers that this was not the later enactment. Rather, after the earlier enactment they no longer prohibited uncolored garments because of stains, although the original enactment had indeed been to wear colored garments specifically. This resolves the matter. See also Mordechai to Yevamos, who infers from the Gemara cited above that nowadays one may not divorce a wife merely because he has no children, even after ten years.

**** ENGLISH ****
If she handled less than a lentil-sized quantity and two lentil-sized areas were found. The Beit Yosef and the later authorities were greatly perplexed concerning the reason for this ruling, since the Gemara concludes that we do not say, 'Take the bird's blood and cast it into the middle.' It appears to me as follows. The added amount in the expression 'a lentil-sized area and more' has no fixed minimum. Even the smallest possible point, like the geometrical point which most authors describe as the final tip of a needle, suffices, for in any event the total is more than a lentil-sized area. All measures fixed by the Sages operate in this manner. Accordingly, if she handled a lentil-sized quantity and two lentil-sized areas plus an additional amount were found upon her, there is only one uncertainty, namely, perhaps the lentil-sized quantity she handled should be placed in the middle. There is no further uncertainty that perhaps two lice were crushed adjacent to one another, despite the statement that no sheet is without several drops of blood, because here there are two lentil-sized areas plus an additional amount. Had two lice been crushed, only two ordinary lentil-sized areas should have been found. By contrast, if she handled less than a lentil-sized quantity and exactly two lentil-sized areas were found upon her, there are two uncertainties. Perhaps the blood she handled should be placed in the middle, and perhaps two lice were crushed immediately adjacent to one another, producing exactly two lentil-sized areas. Since there are two uncertainties, although each possibility is uncommon and individually remote, together they create a single meaningful doubt, and in a rabbinic matter a doubt is resolved leniently. This is the explanation that appears correct to me. Although it is strained, it is preferable to the other explanations offered by the authorities.

She examined her garment, etc. The later authorities noted that the first examination appears unnecessary. Once she examined the garment after the first woman removed it, what difference does it make whether it had been examined before she wore it? The Shach answers in seif katan 47 that the ruling teaches a novelty: even though the garment had initially been examined, the first woman remains pure. Had it not been examined initially, she would in any event be pure because it was not examined before she wore it, as stated in seif 39. This also shows that the Shach understands the term 'examined' in seif 39 to mean a full examination, unlike the Taz, who explains that it merely means that the garment was taken from a protected place, as I wrote above. However, the Shach's answer is insufficient. Even where it was examined initially, what novelty is there in the first woman's purity, since she examined it after removing it and found nothing? The Taz's answer is also difficult, with all due respect. In my humble opinion, the matter requires substantial study. We rule that a single witness is not believed to prohibit another person's property when the matter is not within the witness's control, for the other person may say, 'I do not believe you.' Thus, if one says, 'The pure foods that I prepared with you became impure,' he is not believed when the other does not remain silent, or where silence cannot be treated as admission because the other could not have known. See Rosh, Gittin, chapter HaNizakin, and Yoreh De'ah, siman 127. Accordingly, here the first woman removed her garment, examined it, and lent it to her fellow without telling her that it had been examined, as is evident from Niddah 58a, where the borrower may say regarding the laundering cost, 'I do not believe you.' She plainly was not told of the examination when the garment was lent. Just as she may say this regarding the laundering cost, she may likewise say regarding her own prohibition, 'I do not believe that you examined it. I am pure, and you are impure.' By contrast, if the garment had been examined initially, the second woman gains nothing from this claim. Even if the first woman did not examine it after removing it, both women would be impure, while the first woman is believed regarding herself that she examined it afterward. Only regarding the laundering cost may the second woman still advance that claim. Why, then, may she not also say concerning the initial examination, 'I do not believe you'? One answer is that the case concerns an initial examination that was publicly known, or that the first woman told her of it when lending the garment. Furthermore, regarding the initial examination the first woman's statement renders herself impure, and since she is believed regarding herself she is also believed regarding her fellow, for there is no basis to select one over the other, as is well known. Although she remains pure by saying that she examined it after removing it, her statement that it had been examined initially at least places her within the possibility of liability. At that time the garment was also hers, so she is treated as its owner and is believed. This point, however, requires study. It appears more likely that the case concerns one who immediately stated, when she put it on, that she had examined it, so there is no concern regarding credibility. In any event, the legal principle stated above appears clear to me: she is not believed to render her fellow impure merely by claiming that she examined the garment when removing it, for the fellow may say, 'I do not believe you.' This requires study.
