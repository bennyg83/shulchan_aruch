<b>היתה שופעת כו'. </b>בגמ' דנדה איתא תינוקת שלא הגיע זמנה לראות ושופעת כל שבעה אינה אלא כראיה אחת פוסקת הוה לה כשתי ראיות אמר רב שימי מזלפת אינה כרואה והא קא חזיא אימא אינה כשופעת אלא כפוסקת ופרכינן מכלל דשופעת כי נהרא פרש"י מכלל דשופעת דאמר לעיל אינה אלא ראיה אחת היינו דשפעה כי נהרא בתמיה כיון דמזלפת אינה חשובה שופעת וא"א להיות כמעין כל שבעה וחיה ומשנינן אלא אימא אינה אלא כשופעת. והנה לפי המסקנא הא דאמר תחילה שופעת כל שבעה היינו מזלפת דהא א"א בשופעת כי נהרא וא"כ לכאורה קשה על הטור והש"ע שכתבו היתה שופעת או מזלפת דהא שופעת נמי ע"כ מזלפת ומו"ח ז"ל פירש דשופעת היינו ששופעת בהפסק בין זו לזו ואפ"ה חשבינן לה לראיה אחת וכן מזלפת טיפין טיפין בלא הפסק ומ"ש אחר כך אבל אם פסקה מעט כו' לא קאי רק אמזלפת אבל בשופעת אפי' פסקה מעט וחזרה וראתה והפסיקה כך הרבה פעמים כיון שהיא שופעת הוי ראיה אחת וצ"ע עכ"ל וקשה דהא בהדיא אמר בגמרא דשופעת מותר ופוסקת אסור ש"מ דפוסקת קאי אשופעת ומלבד זה קשה לחלק בהפסקה בין מזלפת טיפין טיפין מהפסקה בין שופעת. ודברי הטור והש"ע הם מתוקנים דודאי שופעת שזכרו הם הוא שופעת ממש כי נהרא בלי הפסק ואע"פ שאמרו בגמ' שהוא א"א היינו כל ז' דלא בחנם כתב רש"י כל ז' אבל הטור והש"ע כתבו כמה ימים דהיינו ב' או ג' ימים וזה ודאי אפשר שתחיה ונמצא שכל שיש הפסק בין הראיות הן בין שופעת הן בין מזלפת טיפין נחשב לשתי ראיות. <i data-commentator="Nekudot HaKesef" data-label="♯" data-order="2"></i>כן נ"ל פשוט:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>If she flowed continuously, etc. </b>The Gemara in Niddah states: A young girl who has not yet reached the age at which she is expected to see blood, and who flows throughout all seven days, has only one sighting; if she stops, it is regarded as two sightings. Rav Shimi said that dripping is not regarded as seeing. The Gemara asks: But she is in fact seeing. Rather, say that dripping is not like flowing continuously, but like stopping. The Gemara asks: Does this imply that “flowing” means flowing like a river? Rashi explains that this is a question, for if dripping is not considered flowing, it would follow that the earlier statement referred to flowing like a river; yet it is impossible for her to flow like a spring for seven days and live. The Gemara therefore concludes: Rather, say that dripping is like flowing continuously. According to the conclusion, the original case of flowing throughout all seven days must mean dripping, since flowing like a river for seven days is impossible. It would therefore seem difficult that the Tur and Shulchan Aruch write, “If she flowed continuously or dripped,” since flowing must itself mean dripping. My father-in-law explained that “flowing” means a discharge with interruptions between one flow and another, yet it is nevertheless regarded as one sighting, whereas “dripping” means drop after drop without interruption. He further explained that the later phrase, “But if she stopped briefly,” refers only to dripping; with flowing, even if she stopped briefly and then saw again, repeatedly, it remains one sighting because each discharge was a flow. This requires further study. It is difficult, for the Gemara explicitly says that flowing is permitted and stopping is forbidden, proving that stopping refers to flowing. Moreover, it is difficult to distinguish between an interruption among drops and an interruption among flows. The words of the Tur and Shulchan Aruch are correct. The flowing they mention means actual continuous flowing like a river without interruption. Although the Gemara says that this is impossible, it means that it is impossible throughout all seven days, as Rashi deliberately wrote “all seven days.” The Tur and Shulchan Aruch, however, write “several days,” meaning two or three days, and it is certainly possible for her to survive that. It follows that whenever there is an interruption between sightings, whether between flows or between drops, they are regarded as two sightings. <i data-commentator="Nekudot HaKesef" data-label="♯" data-order="2"></i>This appears obvious to me.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Or she smelled its odor, etc. </b>Without this, we do not attribute the stain to a bedbug. The Rosh, as cited by the Beit Yosef, writes that ordinarily we do not attribute it to a bedbug because bedbugs are uncommon. This implies that in places where they are common, we do attribute stains to them, just as we do to a louse. The Rashba states this explicitly, as cited by the Beit Yosef, ruling that in such a place she may attribute a stain to a bedbug up to the size of a lupine bean. The Beit Yosef, however, interpreted the Rosh's words differently, explaining that “uncommon” means that it is uncommon for the bedbug to be so near her body without her smelling it. He later wrote that this interpretation can be rejected. He also cited the Maggid Mishneh, who brought the Rashba's words and wrote that the Ramban and Rambam did not distinguish between places where bedbugs are common and places where they are not. It is possible that they hold that even where they are common, we do not attribute the stain to them, since they have an odor, and we do not say that perhaps she smelled it without noticing. As a matter of practical halacha, since the Rashba explicitly rules leniently, the plain meaning of the Rosh agrees, and the Darkei Moshe likewise cites Rabbeinu Yerucham that where bedbugs are common stains are attributed to them, and since in matters of stains we follow the lenient view, it appears that where bedbugs are very common one may attribute stains to them up to the size of a lupine bean. A lupine is a round legume about the width of a small coin. The Beit Yosef explains that where a stain may be attributed to a bedbug, there is no distinction whether its length equals its width.

<b>Two women who handled blood, etc. </b>Tosafos in the chapter HaRo'eh, Berachos 58, write in the name of Rabbeinu Tam that the case is where they handled it one after the other. Had the first woman asked before the second woman's case became known, we would have declared her pure because she handled blood; yet because of the second woman we now declare the first impure, although she was otherwise entirely pure. End quote. My father-in-law copied this and wrote that since they come to ask together, both are impure, and that this is implied by Tosafos. His words imply that if they ask one after the other both are pure. This does not appear correct. Had that been so, the Gemara or the poskim should have stated the distinction explicitly, as they did in the first chapter of Pesachim concerning a mouse with chametz in its mouth, a ruling cited in Orach Chaim in the laws of Pesach. <i data-commentator="Nekudot HaKesef" data-label="♯" data-order="5"></i>Moreover, Tosafos could only have declared the first woman pure if she came before anything was known concerning the second; once both cases are known, both are certainly impure even if they ask one after the other. I found the same in Toras HaBayis HaAroch concerning three women, as cited below in seif 49. The reason is that this case differs from the chametz case. There, when there is doubt whether the mouse entered another house, the house remains permitted; the Sages were stringent only when two houses are involved and one of them must contain chametz, yet they were lenient when the owners ask one after the other because an ordinary doubt is resolved leniently. The same applies to two paths, where each person may be maintained in his original state of purity when he asks separately. Here, however, when a stain cannot be attributed to any cause, the doubt itself renders her impure. The only basis for leniency is a reasonable attribution of the stain to something else. When two women are involved and there is one extra sela-sized amount of blood, there is no greater reason to attribute the impurity to one than to the other, so it is as though there were nothing to which the blood could be attributed. The Derishah also writes that there is no distinction between asking together and asking one after the other, although his reason, that this rule applies only to rabbinic matters whereas this is actual blood and a Torah matter, is incorrect. The principle is used even in Torah matters, as in the cases of two paths and permitting a woman to her husband, because each is maintained in her prior presumption of permission. His further suggestion, that perhaps half a sela of bird blood reached each woman and the remaining half sela came from each woman's source, is also incorrect. If that were so, the Gemara would not have needed to say that there is one extra sela and ask why it should be attributed to one more than the other; it could simply have ruled both impure because each has only half a sela from the bird. Rather, whenever there is a cause before us to which a stain may be attributed, we attribute the entire stain to it and do not say that only part came from that cause and the remainder from the woman. The difficulty therefore returns, why is this unlike chametz? The correct answer is as stated: here it is as though there is no cause of attribution at all. Therefore, even when they ask one after the other, both are impure.

<b>And the same law applies if she examined herself and placed it in a box, etc. </b>These are the Rashba's words. He does not mean to exclude a case where she left it exposed and blood was later found on it, for the Mishnah plainly rules that blood found on her examination cloth after a delay renders her impure because of doubt. Rather, he contrasts this case with the end of the seif, where she placed it beneath a pillow or cushion, or pressed it against her thigh. His meaning is that if blood is found immediately on a checked cloth she is obviously impure; and even if it is not found until the following day, she is likewise impure when the cloth was meanwhile kept in a box and was not pressed against her thigh. The wording of the Tur, however, is difficult. It says, “and the following day she found blood on it, even the smallest amount, or she placed it in a box,” without explaining where the cloth had been kept in the first case. Even with the Beit Yosef's correction, that it had been left exposed and that the Tur proceeds from the lesser to the greater novelty, the wording remains unsettled, since the first clause does not identify its location and the second cannot naturally say “or she placed it in a box.” The Perishah writes that “or even if she placed it in a box” refers to an unexamined cloth, but this cannot be written, for the Gemara explicitly states that even Rebbe, who is most stringent regarding an unexamined cloth and renders her impure as a niddah, agrees that a bean plus a little is required. Were I not afraid to emend the text, I would say that “the following day” in the first clause is a scribal error, and it should read: “She found blood on it, even the smallest amount; or she placed it in a box and the following day...” This would conform precisely to the Rashba. It would also resolve why the Tur, when discussing an unexamined cloth, writes that blood was found on it the following day but does not mention that it had been kept in a box. The Tur relied on the first clause: with a checked cloth, if blood was found the next day after it had meanwhile been in a box she is impure, whereas if the same happened with an unexamined cloth the Rashba declares her pure.

<b>She checked the garment, etc. </b>Why was the first examination necessary, since the garment was found clean after she removed it? The Derishah leaves this requiring study. My father-in-law answers that the first examination is mentioned only by way of inference: if the garment was not found clean afterward, the earlier examination would not help. But the Tur later explicitly writes that whenever the two women are equal, both are impure. The Perishah explains that “she was found pure” refers to the woman rather than to the garment, and that because she initially checked the garment and was later found pure, we maintain her prior presumption; although the second woman was likewise found pure after removing it, we apply “here it was found, here it was.” These words are astonishing, for throughout this siman it is obvious that when they are equal we do not apply that rule. The correct explanation is straightforward. An examination after removing the garment is effective only if performed deliberately to ascertain its condition. A casual glance is ineffective, because a person does not pay attention to a matter that is not relevant to him. If she did not inspect the garment before wearing it, she has no reason to examine it carefully after removing it, for even if she finds a stain she remains pure under the preceding seif. Her later examination will therefore be casual and cannot render the second woman impure. Only when she first checked the garment does she have reason to inspect it carefully after removing it, since a newly found stain would render her impure. One cannot say that she examines it carefully because she wishes to lend it to another woman and thereby protect herself if a stain is later found, for then both women would be pure, since the garment was not examined initially and the stain may already have been there. Thus the Rashba, followed by the Tur, specifically states that she checked it first, ensuring that the later examination was deliberate. This appears entirely clear.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>If all three were awake, etc. </b>Toras HaBayis HaAroch cites this ruling in the name of the Ra'avad: “The Mishnah's ruling that when blood is found beneath one woman, she and the woman next to her are impure, applies specifically when they were sleeping. In their sleep they press against one another and one enters the other's place. The same applies when they are awake but move from their positions and ordinarily enter within one another's area, such as women grinding. But when they sit or lie awake on a bed, there is no concern that they shifted, for each can know her place; and if she says, ‘I am certain that I did not enter here,’ she is pure.” End quote. I have not understood his reasoning. His words imply that she is pure only when she states with certainty that she did not enter the inner woman's place. <i data-commentator="Nekudot HaKesef" data-label="♯" data-order="9"></i>Yet such a statement should not be effective. Choshen Mishpat, siman 79, seif 11, rules that if one says, “I lent you a maneh beside a certain pillar,” and the defendant answers, “I never passed that pillar,” but witnesses testify that he did pass it, though they did not see the loan, he is not established as a liar, because a person may make an inaccurate statement about a matter that was not important to him. Here too, perhaps the woman entered the inner area without noticing, since she had no reason to pay attention. One cannot answer that an awake woman does not ordinarily enter her companion's place, for then why must she say, “I am certain that I did not enter”? She should be permitted even if she does not know. It follows that her statement is the basis for permission, and the difficulty remains. It appears that this case is different because the blood was found shortly after they lay down. Within such a brief interval she can reliably know whether she entered her companion's place, especially since doing so is somewhat unusual. But if a long time elapsed between their lying down and the discovery of the blood, it appears to me that the outer woman is also impure for the reason stated. This appears correct.

<b>Except for stains found on a checked examination cloth, etc. </b>This is the Ra'avad's view. It appears that only concerning a checked cloth, where the Gemara says that she is impure as a niddah, do we regard the finding as an actual sighting for every purpose. But an unexamined cloth, although it also renders her forbidden as stated in seif 36, does not render her a niddah; she is only impure because of doubt, like an ordinary stain. It is therefore not treated as an actual sighting for establishing a fixed period. This also supports what I wrote in seif 38, that blood on an unexamined cloth is not Torah-level impurity even when she wiped herself. The Maharshal copied a responsum of Maharam: “Concerning a woman afflicted with boils, it is obvious that her stains are pure. Even if she did not examine herself at the time of her fixed period and later examined and found stains on her sheet or garment, she is pure; all the more so during her clean days, when we attribute leniently.” End quote. Her status during the first three clean days will be explained, with God's help, in siman 196, seif 10. <br><b><i data-commentator="Nekudot HaKesef" data-label="♯" data-order="10"></i>The Rama wrote </b>in responsum 96 concerning a stain larger than a bean plus a little found on a woman's garment, where she had a possible attribution because she suffered from scabs and blood-filled wounds. At the time she found the stain she commonly discharged permitted white and green appearances, and her garment was soiled with such marks. At the edge of one of them a red appearance surrounded the white mark like moisture spreading through cloth, making it appear that the redness was the border of the white mark and that both formed one stain. The Rama ruled that although stains are generally treated leniently, she was impure, because the appearance indicated that the red came from her together with the white. Terumas HaDeshen, siman 247, states that white and green appearances also come from the source. Once the source had opened to discharge the white, the adjoining red presumably emerged from there as well. Even if it were only doubtful whether they formed one stain, the doubt should be resolved stringently. He supported this from the Gemara's rule that when one staining substance is found on her, she may attribute several colors to it. Rashi explains that if something capable of producing a stain, such as eye salve or resin, was found on her, and she later found a stain on her garment, she may attribute it to that substance even when the colors differ. The Gemara's second version limits this to handling a chicken, which contains several kinds of blood. The Rama reasoned that if different colors are associated leniently, all the more should they be associated stringently; and the source resembles a chicken because many types of blood emerge from it. End of his words in brief. Although the Rama's words are precious to us and we generally drink them thirstily, this ruling is not accepted by us. If the opening of the source for a white appearance established that any red stain came from it, then even red found far from the white should be impure, just as blood from a handled chicken may be attributed wherever it is found on the garment. That is certainly false, for it would mean that whenever a woman discharged a permitted appearance, every later stain would be impure even where there is another valid attribution, a distinction never made by the Gemara or the poskim. The Mishnah concerning three women sleeping in one bed proves the contrary. When blood is found beneath one, all are impure; if one immediately examines herself and is found impure, she alone is impure and the others are pure. Their purity rests on the presumption created by her actual impure finding. According to the Rama, even if she found only a white or green appearance, we should say that her source was open and attribute the bed's blood to her rather than to the others, yet the Mishnah says no such thing. Thus a permitted appearance creates no presumption regarding an impure one. The Mishnah concerning the woman who asked R' Akiva about a stain provides another proof. He asked whether she had a wound that could reopen and bleed, and purified her on that basis. Had a customary white discharge prevented attribution to a wound, he should also have asked whether she saw a white or other permitted appearance. One cannot say that such investigation was unnecessary, for the Rama's logic is that a rule used leniently should all the more be used stringently. Rather, where a stain can be attributed to a wound or boils we rule leniently. Even an actual sighting may be attributed to a wound in that place, and certainly a stain on a garment may be. The Gemara's statement that one may repeat an experiment stringently, cited above in seif 32, applies only where the blood cannot be attributed to anything other than the woman and the sole basis for permission is that she was not known to have passed that place. There the Sages required reenactment because stains were decreed stringently when no attribution exists. Ravina states at the end of the chapter HaRo'eh Kesem that the decree was made to reinforce Torah law, but stains themselves are rabbinic and are treated leniently by attributing them to any plausible cause. For the same reason, even according to Rava's first version, where one found substance allows attribution of several colors, we may not reverse that leniency into a stringency. The Sages specifically established leniency in attribution whenever possible. The Rama's comparison to a chicken is also difficult. A chicken actually contains red slaughter blood, black limb blood, and saffron-colored intestinal blood, as the Tur explains. Therefore one who handled a chicken may attribute all three colors to it. But if she handled only saffron-colored intestinal blood, she certainly could not attribute red blood to it. Why, then, should a woman who discharged a permitted color be presumed also to have discharged an impure color? Were that so, every permitted appearance would itself render her impure, lest an impure drop was covered by it, as we fear after first marital relations that semen may cover a drop of blood. There, however, the Beit Yosef explains that most virgins have virginal blood. No comparable presumption exists here. Opening of the source for a permitted appearance establishes nothing concerning a red appearance. The red therefore remains an ordinary stain and is treated leniently when there is another cause to which it may be attributed. Only in the precise case asked of the Rama, where the eye could see that the red was the edge of the permitted mark, spreading around it like a line, is she certainly impure. Otherwise, if the permitted appearance merely lies over or passes across the red, and the red can be attributed to another cause, it retains the law of an ordinary stain. We say that the red was already there from another source, and she is pure. This appears correct in my humble opinion.
