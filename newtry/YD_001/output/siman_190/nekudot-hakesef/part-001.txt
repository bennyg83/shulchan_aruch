<b>(Taz seif katan 17) He objected.</b> He objected to Bach, but in my humble opinion Bach's words are correct, as I will explain. Bach's statement, 'and so it appears from Tosafot,' does not refer to the rule that if they come to ask together they are impure. A careful reader of Bach will see explicitly that he refers even to a case where they handled the bird one after another, in which case they are impure. Bach writes: 'Two women who handled, etc. It appears that this teaches us that even where they did not handle the bird together, but one after another, and had the first woman asked alone we would have declared her pure because she handled the bird, nevertheless, since they came to ask together, both are impure. And so it appears from Tosafot.' These are his words. This is precisely as I explained. His expression, 'since they came to ask,' rests on his assumption that elsewhere as well the law is that when they come to ask one after another, we attribute the uncertainty leniently, as he ruled above in siman 111, seif 5. The proof he brought from Rashba in Torat HaBayit, page 178b, is no proof, because Rashba follows his own position and rules stringently even above in siman 111. According to the ruling we accept there, that one may be lenient when they come one after another, as I wrote there in Shach, seif katan 19, the same applies here. This is the view of the French rabbis and Ramban cited there in Torat HaBayit, and brought by Beit Yosef, page 205a, beginning 'Rashba wrote.' Rashba writes: 'Some of my teachers, etc. If you ask, how does an unchecked garment differ from the Mishnah in the chapter HaIshah Shehaytah Osah Tzeracheha, where three women wore one garment and blood was found upon it, so that all are impure? Our French rabbis answered that there too, strictly speaking, all should be declared pure. Since, however, all depend upon this garment, it resembles the case of two paths, where if they come to ask together both are impure, but if they come one after another both are pure. These words were not clear to me. If so, where she lent her garment even to a non-Jewish woman who had not seen blood, or where one of the three women later found a stain, we should declare the others pure, since one of them is not coming to ask. Ramban's words are close to those of Tosafot, etc.' End quote. I do not understand Rashba's objection from a non-Jewish woman who had not seen blood, for if she had not seen blood, how could we attribute the stain to her? As to his objection that one of them is not coming to ask, one may answer that according to Tosafot and those who agree with them, in such a case each woman is indeed declared pure, since they came one after another. The Gemara did not distinguish this case because it sought only to teach the basic law that, regarding a stain, all are impure. Perhaps this was the intention of Raah in Bedek HaBayit when he objected to Rashba on this point. Taz's distinction between chametz and the case of two paths on the one hand, and our case on the other, is not convincing, as I will explain. He also objected to Derishah, who wrote that this principle applies specifically to rabbinic matters, claiming that even in Torah matters we say so in Pesachim regarding two paths, and in the second chapter of Ketubot regarding permitting a woman. He himself was imprecise. How could one imagine ruling this way concerning a Torah prohibition? The only basis for leniency is doubt, while a doubt in Torah law is treated stringently. On the contrary, the entire passage in Pesachim proves that this principle applies specifically to rabbinic matters. This is also clear from Rashi there. The Gemara discusses one pile of chametz before two checked houses, where a mouse took chametz and entered one house, and it is unknown which house it entered, and compares this to two paths. Rashi explains: 'If each person asks separately, he is pure, and beit din cannot declare him impure, because we hold that uncertain impurity in a public domain is pure. The same applies to the houses. If each owner asks separately, we tell each one that he need not inspect, because inspection for chametz is rabbinic, and we attribute the uncertainty leniently.' End quote. Thus, only in a rabbinic matter do we attribute the uncertainty leniently, because a rabbinic doubt is treated leniently. The same is true of the paths, where uncertainty in a public domain is pure, but not of a Torah prohibition. The leniency in the second chapter of Ketubot applies specifically to a captive woman, regarding whom the Sages were exceptionally lenient, as explained there in the Gemara. According to this, our case too is one in which we attribute the uncertainty leniently. Just as with chametz we are lenient because inspection for chametz is rabbinic, meaning that by Torah law nullification alone suffices and the Sages required inspection, so too if a stain is found on a woman's body she is pure until she senses blood leaving her flesh, while the Sages decreed impurity for a stain only where there is no other source to which it can be attributed. Therefore, when the women come to ask one after another, they are pure.

<b>(Ibid., seif katan 41) He objected.</b> He objected to Rama's responsum, and all of his proofs can be rejected. Nevertheless, his ruling is reasonable. Examine this carefully. Rabbeinu Yerucham wrote in Netiv 15, part 2: 'It is obvious that we are never concerned for a stain above the genital area, etc., until: If the stain was found on her garment and on her body, and she did not pass through the butchers' market, then from the belt downward she is impure, while from the belt upward she is pure.' End quote. His concluding words, 'from the belt upward,' must refer to a case where she bent backward, as discussed at the outset, and even so she is pure, in accordance with Rashba's opinion. Otherwise, even if the stain were on her body she would be pure, as is implied by the beginning of his words and as is proven in the Gemara at the beginning of the chapter HaRo'eh. The Gemara asks: If the stain was above the belt, why is she impure when it is on her body? Did we not learn that if she saw blood on her body away from the genital area, she is pure? This necessarily refers to a case where she did not pass through the butchers' market, as does the Mishnah that discusses seeing blood, etc. Furthermore, at that stage the Gemara had not yet considered the answer that she had passed through the butchers' market. Moreover, were the other interpretation correct, why did the Gemara ask its question? It could have answered that the case always concerns a stain above the belt where she did not pass through the butchers' market, so that a stain on her body renders her impure while one on her garment leaves her pure, and that the Mishnah concerns a case where she did pass through the market. Rather, it is certain that above the belt, where she did not bend backward, even if she did not pass through the butchers' market, she is pure even when the stain is on her body. This is also the view of all the authorities. The passage must therefore concern a case where she bent backward. This appears correct to me.
