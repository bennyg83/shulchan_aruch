<b>Someone asked concerning.</b> Rabbenu Nissim explains the statement in Perek Bnei HaIr that one asks about the laws of Pesach during Pesach by saying there is a practical consequence: a student who asks a Pesach question within thirty days before Pesach is considered to be asking an appropriate question and therefore receives priority in having his inquiry addressed. In Choshen Mishpat siman 336, I wrote another substantial monetary-law consequence based on the Gemara in Perek HaShoel. The Sages said to Rava, "Master is borrowed to us," meaning, as Rashi explains, "Our teacher is engaged in our work of teaching us Torah all day, so if we borrow an animal from him and it dies we should be exempt because the borrowing occurred while the owner was working for us." Rava became upset and replied, "Do you seek to deprive me of my money? On the contrary, you are borrowed to me for my work, because if I wish to leave one tractate and begin another so that it not be forgotten by me, you cannot object, while you cannot abandon the tractate I choose." The Gemara concludes that this is not always so: during the public lecture days before a festival, when the laws of the festival are taught, Rava is borrowed to them because he cannot switch to another tractate; on other days they are borrowed to him. According to this Gemara, there is a practical consequence here. Since a rav is obligated to teach his students the laws of Pesach during the thirty days before Pesach, if during those thirty days the students borrow something from him and it is lost through unavoidable accident, they are exempt because it is a case of borrowing while the owner is working for the borrower. This appears correct to me.

<b>But he should not accept gifts from people.</b> One may ask from the teaching, "The kohen gadol shall be greater than his brothers," from which Chazal derive that his fellow kohanim make him wealthy from their own property. Sotah 40 likewise recounts that R. Abbahu appointed R. Abba of Akko to a position of leadership so that people would give him portions and make him wealthy. There, however, these payments were part of the established prerogatives of communal leadership, an obligation accepted by the community to give portions to its head. They therefore had the status of compensation for his office. In the same way we understand the account of Ilfa and R. Yochanan in Taanit, where R. Yochanan was appointed leader by the community. The prohibition here concerns accepting personal gifts from an individual. The principal permission for a Torah scholar to benefit from others is so Torah can be sustained among its students and they need not abandon study because of financial burdens. Even one who already possesses some money may still have justification, because we cannot define a fixed measure of what constitutes his necessary livelihood. Tosafot in Perek Eizehu Neshech explain regarding interest received from a gentile that even under the stricter version, which permits only what is needed for livelihood, taxes and royal burdens are included in livelihood needs. Even those who do not pay such taxes have the many expenses of Jewish life, including educating sons in Torah and marrying daughters to talmidei chachamim. Maharshal in Perek Elu Treifot discusses at length the permissibility of similar forms of support for a talmid chacham who needs them. It appears to me that one who is not truly wealthy may accept even a large benefit that eventually makes him wealthy. Peah teaches that one who has two hundred zuz minus one dinar may receive even a thousand dinar in charity. This is comparable to the cases of R. Abba and R. Yochanan mentioned above. But once he has become wealthy and knows that he possesses sufficient principal from which to support himself, he should no longer benefit from the gifts of others. My father-in-law concludes that a head of beit din may accept gifts so as to become wealthy, because greater wealth causes his words to be respected by the public. In truth, the gain here is outweighed by the loss. This creates a serious stumbling block: a litigant who expects a case before the rav may give him a gift some time in advance in order to incline his heart, and there is no more serious form of advance bribery. Moreover, the rav is effectively receiving payment for his Torah. We find an even stronger example with Rabbah bar Avuha in Perek HaMekabel: he was under great financial pressure, yet refused to benefit even from what Heaven offered him when he heard, "Whoever would consume his future world like Rabbah bar Avuha," and so forth. All the more so regarding money given by another human being. Therefore, one who is not compelled by genuine need should distance himself from this ugliness and anything resembling it. All the more so from the greater disgrace practiced by some who are obsessed with money and openly demand that people give them gifts. They disgrace Torah and those who study it. Even regarding priestly gifts Chazal prohibited a kohen from soliciting his portion, all the more so here, where it is a grave wrongdoing. "The righteous person shall live by his faith."
