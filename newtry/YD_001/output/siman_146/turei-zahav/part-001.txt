**** ENGLISH ****
<b>One who purchases scrap metal, etc. </b>There is a passage in the Tur that requires explanation, and we will explain it with the help of Heaven. A Baraisa states: If he performed meshichah before paying the money, he should return it; if he performed meshichah after paying the money, he must cast it into the Dead Sea. The questioner in the Gemara sought to prove from this that meshichah does not acquire from a non-Jew. Rava rejected this proof, for in truth it is a mistaken transaction. Rather, in the first clause, where he paid its value, it appears to be an idol belonging to a Jew; in the latter clause, where he did not pay its value, it does not so appear.

The Tur wrote: If he performed meshichah before paying the money, he should return it to the non-Jew, for it had not yet been acquired. If he performed meshichah after paying the money, it had already been acquired by him, it is an idol belonging to a Jew, it cannot be nullified, and he must cast it into the Dead Sea. The Rambam likewise ruled, etc. This is difficult, for the Gemara concludes that in every case there is no valid acquisition, since it is a mistaken transaction. The distinction is only whether it appears to have been acquired.

My father-in-law, of blessed memory, wrote that when the Tur says, "it had already been acquired by him," this means according to the perception of observers, who will think that he acquired it, since everyone will assume that it was acquired by him. This is entirely unsatisfactory. In truth, acquisition from a non-Jew is effected either through meshichah according to Rabbeinu Tam, or through money according to Rashi, as the Tur wrote below in siman 320. Only in order to satisfy both opinions did he write there, regarding the sanctity of a firstborn animal, that both meshichah and payment should be performed. It is therefore impossible to explain the Tur here as following the Tanna of the Baraisa, who required both. If so, the Baraisa would follow neither Rashi nor Rabbeinu Tam. Since the law is that either one alone suffices, why should we be concerned for observers specifically when both were performed, but not when one alone was performed, for observers could likewise say that it had already been acquired by the Jew? This is especially difficult since the Tur made the prohibition dependent upon there having been an acquisition. Moreover, the language of the Tur does not imply this.

The Perishah wrote that the Tur truly intended what he wrote in siman 320, and therefore required both payment and meshichah. This has already been rejected by what we wrote, for the words of the Tur are the words of the Baraisa. Furthermore, he was not precise in this matter. The Tur required both only as a stringency, such as to remove the animal from the possibility of firstborn sanctity, but not as a leniency. Here, however, this would produce a leniency, permitting him to return the idol whenever both forms of acquisition were not performed. This is plainly untenable. On the contrary, the Tur is stringent when either one was performed, as in siman 132, see there, because meshichah acquires with respect to one who measures wine into the vessel of a non-Jew, while money acquires where one advanced a dinar to a shopkeeper. How, then, could the Tur rule leniently here until both were performed? Clearly, this cannot be the explanation.

It appears to me that the words of the Tur may be explained after noting another difficulty. Why did he need to write here that it is an idol belonging to a Jew, that it cannot be nullified, and that it must be cast into the Dead Sea? Did we not already know the law governing an idol belonging to a Jew?

It therefore appears to me that since, according to the Gemara's conclusion, the matter depends upon payment, one may ask why the Baraisa requires meshichah at all. Meshichah accomplishes nothing here, because the transaction was mistaken, and the only concern is appearances. If so, we should likewise be concerned where there was payment alone, for according to Rashi money ordinarily effects acquisition, and observers may not know that the transaction was mistaken. One might therefore have said that the essential factor is payment, and that when the Tanna said, "after he paid the money he performed meshichah," meshichah was not meant precisely, but merely indicated that he drew the scrap metal to himself and then discovered the idol.

The Tur therefore teaches that this is not so. The true principle is that whenever, had he known that the idol was among the scrap metal, he would legally have acquired it, then when the transaction was mistaken we decree because observers may think that he knew of it. But where, even had he known, the law would not have effected acquisition, we do not decree merely because observers might misunderstand his lack of knowledge. Thus, where he performed meshichah alone, even had he known of the idol he would not have acquired it. When he drew all the scrap metal to himself, he certainly intended to sort it afterward, retaining what was permitted and returning what was forbidden. He desires to acquire the permitted material, but does not desire to acquire the forbidden material, as we say in the chapter HaShoel.

If, however, he already paid for the entire weight of the scrap metal, this cannot be said. Had he known that an idol was included, he would certainly have acquired it. Therefore, when he was unaware of it at the time of the purchase, the Sages were concerned for appearances, but specifically when both payment and meshichah occurred.

One might ask why meshichah is needed, since payment alone would acquire it had he known, and we should therefore be concerned for observers even where he was unaware. The answer is that even if he knew and acquired the idol through payment, as long as it had not come into his possession he would not need to cast it into the Dead Sea. It would suffice for the non-Jew to nullify it, as we proved above, for the requirement applies specifically when it came into the Jew's possession. The Tur therefore had to write here that he must cast it into the Dead Sea, teaching the point explained above.

The meaning of the Tur is thus as follows: One who purchased scrap metal and unknowingly found an idol among it, if he performed meshichah before paying, he should return it. There is no concern for observers, since even had he known of the idol the transaction would not yet have acquired it. But if he performed both acts, he may not return it, since had he known, it would already have been acquired. Should you ask why meshichah is necessary, the Tur answers that it is then an idol belonging to a Jew and must be cast into the Dead Sea, since it came into his possession; therefore the Sages were concerned even though he acted unknowingly.

One may further ask why Rava established the Baraisa as involving a mistaken transaction and a concern for appearances, rather than as involving one who knew of the idol and requiring both acts of acquisition. The answer is that the Baraisa says that he "found" an idol, implying that he did not know of it at the time of the transaction. It should therefore have been permitted in every case. Why, then, is it forbidden when both acts occurred? Necessarily, it is because of observers, in accordance with the distinction explained above.

According to our explanation, the Tur's words, "and so too wrote the Rambam," are also resolved. The Beit Yosef asked that this is an explicit Baraisa. According to what I have written, it is well understood, for the Tur teaches that both acts are required, and payment alone is insufficient, as explained above. Everything is thus properly resolved, with the help of Heaven.

<b>He sold it or pledged it, etc. </b>The Mishnah states: If he sold it or pledged it, Rebbi says it is nullified, while the Sages say it is not nullified. The halacha is known to follow the Sages. The Gemara records a dispute: One opinion says that they disagree where it was given to a non-Jewish smith, but where it was given to a Jewish smith all agree that it is nullified. The other opinion says that they disagree even where it was given to a Jewish smith.

The Tur ruled stringently, that even when it was given to a Jewish smith, the halacha follows the Sages, who say that it is not nullified. The Rambam ruled according to the first version, leniently when it was given to a Jewish smith. The Ran explained his reasoning from the Baraisa taught afterward: If he borrowed money upon it, or a collapse fell upon it, etc., it is not nullified. The Baraisa needed to teach this, for had it taught only that he borrowed upon it, one might have said that since he did not sell it, it is not nullified. The implication is that selling does effect nullification. This must refer to a Jewish smith and follow the Sages. Thus, selling is effective, but merely borrowing upon it is not.

The Ran further wrote that according to Rebbi, pledging it nullifies it specifically when the non-Jew made it an absolute security, saying, "If I do not redeem it by such-and-such time, it shall belong to you," and the time arrived without his redeeming it. Only then is it nullified according to Rebbi, since this is comparable to a sale. Consequently, according to the Rambam, the halacha for us is likewise that such a pledge to a Jewish smith effects nullification.

The Beit Yosef disagreed with the Ran and wrote that regarding a pledge there is no distinction whether it was made an absolute security or not. Even if it was made an absolute security to a Jewish smith, it is not nullified. He proved this because the Gemara, concerning one who borrowed upon it, did not say, "because he did not make it an absolute security." Why did it instead say, "because he did not sell it"? Evidently, with respect to a pledge, we do not distinguish whether it was made an absolute security or not. He also wrote that this follows from several passages in the Rambam.

It appears that the Beit Yosef understands the Rambam as follows. The Rambam wrote: "If he sold it to a Jewish smith, it is nullified. If he pledged it, or sold it to a non-Jew or to a Jew who is not a smith, it is not nullified," end quote. Since the Rambam mentioned only a sale in the first clause, this indicates that even an absolute pledge does not help. Accordingly, the Rambam's latter words refer to two cases: If he pledged it, even to a Jewish smith, or if he sold it to one who is not a smith, it is not nullified. According to this explanation, however, it is difficult why the Shulchan Aruch wrote here, "and some permit it when given to a Jewish smith," which implies that this leniency applies even to a pledge. My father-in-law, of blessed memory, likewise wrote that the Shulchan Aruch's ruling, "some permit," has no application to a pledge.

I am astonished by the Beit Yosef. The Gemara says, "But with a Jewish smith all agree that it is nullified," meaning that the Sages agree with Rebbi. Rebbi explicitly spoke in the Mishnah of both pledging and selling. How can we say that the Sages agreed with him only regarding a sale, while inventing a new dispute regarding a pledge, under which even a pledge to a Jewish smith would not be nullified according to the Sages?

Rather, the words of the Ran are certainly primary. The pledge mentioned in the Mishnah means that it was made an absolute security. In that case, the Sages agree with Rebbi that it is nullified when given to a Jewish smith. The Baraisa's statement that one who borrowed upon it has not nullified it refers to an ordinary pledge, not made absolute, and it deals with a Jewish smith according to the Sages. Specifically an ordinary loan upon it does not nullify it. But if he made it an absolute security until a fixed time and failed to redeem it, it is like a complete sale, as written in Orach Chaim siman 441 regarding chametz belonging to a Jew that is in the possession of a non-Jew.

The Gemara did not need to say, "because he did not make it an absolute security," since it said that he did not sell it, and an actual sale is certainly stronger. The Mishnah, which mentions a pledge, deals with an absolute pledge; therefore, according to Rebbi, it is nullified even when given to a non-Jewish smith. One might ask why the Mishnah needed to teach a pledge at all, since an absolute pledge is comparable to a sale. This is not difficult. Since the case of a non-Jewish smith is under discussion, one might have thought that even an absolute pledge is not nullified, because the non-Jew will reason that the lender values the idol and will not break it. The Mishnah therefore teaches that according to Rebbi it is nevertheless nullified. But in the Baraisa, which concerns a Jewish smith, this reasoning does not apply, and it is obvious that an absolute pledge is like an actual sale.

The Rambam therefore properly mentioned only a sale in the first clause, and did not mention a pledge, lest one mistakenly think that even an ordinary pledge, which was not made absolute, is effective. In truth, however, an absolute pledge is a complete sale and effects nullification when given to a Jewish smith. In the latter clause, which deals with the possession of a non-Jew, the Rambam also mentioned a pledge as a progression from the lesser case to the greater. No form of pledge is effective there, and even if it were equivalent to a sale, a sale itself is ineffective.

Indeed, in the Kesef Mishnah the Rav explicitly wrote in agreement with the Ran. This is also the meaning of the Shulchan Aruch here when it states, "and some permit it," namely, even in the case of a pledge, when it was made an absolute security, the time arrived, and he did not redeem it. The Rav should have explained here in the Shulchan Aruch which type of pledge is permitted, namely one made an absolute security that was not redeemed at its appointed time. This appears clear and primary to me.

<b>Since he nullified one limb, etc. </b>The Beit Yosef explained the Rambam's intent, as the author of this ruling was troubled by what we rule in the dispute between Rav and Shmuel at the end of the chapter Kol HaTzelamim, cited by the Beit Yosef, concerning an idol that broke by itself. There, when an ordinary craftsman cannot restore it, it is nullified according to all opinions. Yet earlier, at the beginning of chapter 25, Rav and Rabbi Yochanan disagree concerning this, and we rule stringently like Rabbi Yochanan.

The Rambam therefore explained that when Rav and Shmuel discuss a case in which an ordinary craftsman cannot restore it and it is permitted, they do not mean that it is permitted without any nullification. Rather, one of the pieces must in any event be nullified. Since it broke by itself, we regard all the pieces as though the idol were still whole. Therefore, just as a non-Jew's nullification of even one limb would have been effective while it was whole, so too it is effective now. But where an ordinary craftsman can restore it, each piece is itself regarded as an idol, and every piece must therefore be nullified separately.

The Tur, however, wrote here that if an ordinary craftsman cannot restore it, it is nullified. This is certainly difficult, for we rule like Rabbi Yochanan that an idol which broke by itself is not nullified. It appears that the Tur also accepts the resolution cited by the Beit Yosef in the name of the Ramban and the Ran, that there is a distinction between a case in which the main body of the idol remains and one in which it does not.

Accordingly, the earlier dispute between Rav and Rabbi Yochanan concerns a case where the main body remains and only a small portion broke off, of sufficient significance that the idol would have been nullified had the non-Jew broken it intentionally. In that case we rule that it is not nullified when it broke by itself. There it is not relevant to ask whether an ordinary craftsman can restore it, since the non-Jew has no desire to restore it. Its main body remains, and he continues to worship it as before.

By contrast, the dispute between Rav and Shmuel concerns a case where the entire idol was broken. This is indicated by their phrase, "an ordinary craftsman can restore it," which shows that its worship has ceased so long as it is not restored. In such a case, the Tur correctly ruled that if it cannot be restored, it is nullified even though it broke by itself, for the non-Jew certainly nullified it after seeing that it was completely broken.

One might ask why the Tur did not state the law where the main body remains. The answer is that it is learned from what the Tur wrote. If an idol that broke by itself is nullified only when an ordinary craftsman cannot restore it, it follows that where restoration is unnecessary because the main body remains, it is forbidden when it broke by itself. This appears to be the correct understanding of the Tur's view.
