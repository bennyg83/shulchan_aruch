<b>המניח עובד כוכבים כו'. </b>בגמרא עביד צריכותא דאי תנא מעביר יינו הוה אמינא דוקא בזה מותר דסבר עובד כוכבים דלמא אתי וחזא לי אבל בקרון או ספינה דאי מפליג ליה לספינה עביד מאי דבעי כלומר הוה אמינא דניחוש להכי קמ"ל דלא חיישינן לזה ואי תנא קרון וספינה לחוד הוה אמינא משום דסבר דלמא אתי בדרך אחר וקאי אצל כותל וחזי לי אבל בחנות סוגר הדלת ועביד מאי דבעי קמ"ל דלא חיישינן להכי וכל הנהו דלעיל סי' קכ"ח דאי סגר הדלת אסור היינו בדידעינן בבירור שסגר הדלת בענין שאין הישראל יוכל לכנוס לשם שלא מדעת העובד כוכבים אבל בדלא ידעינן שרי ואע"ג דאיכא מנעול בפנים שהיה יכול לנעול אם ירצה ומיהו ש"מ שאם בבירור סגר כאן העובד כוכבים הדלת אסור כיון דאית ליה שייכות בבית ואית ליה לאשתמוטיה דברשות עומד שם שהרי ישראל הניחו שם וכן בהיתר דקרון וספינה דאמרינן דטעם ההיתר דסבר דאתא בדרך אחר וקאי אכותל ורואה אלמא דוקא בדאיכא דרך אחר אבל אם אינו אלא דרך א' כיון שהפליג עיניו ממנו אסור דהא א"א לו לבא אא"כ חזי ליה וכן נמי אם בבירור הפליג בספינה או קרון במקום שא"א לראות ודאי אסור אלא דמספיקא לא חיישינן לזה כל זה מדברי הרשב"א בתה"ה וזהו שסיים כאן והוא שלא נודע כו':
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>One who leaves a non-Jew, etc. </b>The Gemara establishes the necessity of each case. Had it taught only one who transports his wine, I might have said that only there it is permitted, because the non-Jew thinks, perhaps he will come and see me; but with a wagon or ship, if the ship travels far away, he can do as he wishes. Meaning, I might have thought that we should be concerned for this; therefore it teaches us that we are not concerned. Had it taught only a wagon and ship, I might have said that the reason is because he thinks, perhaps he will come by another route, stand by the wall, and see me; but in a shop he can close the door and do as he wishes. Therefore it teaches us that we are not concerned for this. All the cases above in siman 128 in which it is forbidden if he closed the door refer to where we know clearly that he closed it in a manner that prevents the Jew from entering without the non-Jew's knowledge. But where we do not know this, it is permitted, even though there is an interior lock which he could have locked had he wished. Nevertheless, we learn from here that if the non-Jew clearly closed the door, it is forbidden, since he has a connection to the house and can excuse himself, for he is standing there with permission, since the Jew left him there. Likewise, regarding the permission involving a wagon or ship, where we say that the reason is that he thinks the Jew may come by another route, stand by the wall, and see him, this applies specifically where there is another route. But if there is only one route, once the Jew has gone beyond sight it is forbidden, for he cannot return without the non-Jew seeing him. Likewise, if the Jew clearly traveled away by ship or wagon to a place from which he cannot see, it is certainly forbidden. But out of doubt alone we are not concerned for this. All this is from the Rashba in Toras HaBayis HaAruch, and this is why the Mechaber concludes here, provided it was not known, etc.

<b>בד"א שיש דרך עקלתון. </b>בגמ' אמרי' זה על מעביר יינו והוא הדין השלישי שזכר הטור והש"ע ריש הסימן וכתב ב"י בשם הר"ן דדוקא במעביר אמרי' כן אבל במניח עובד כוכבים בחנות וישראל יוצא ונכנס לא בעינן דרך עקלתון דכל שעה מסתפי מיניה דלמא השתא אתי וחזי ישראל שהוא מתקרב לחבית והרשב"א כתב בקרון וספינה דבעינן דרך עקלתון כמו שהעתקתי לעיל וכתב ב"י דגם הר"ן מודה בזה ולא קא ממעט אלא מניח עובד כוכבים בחנות דסתמא יכול ישראל לבוא שם פתאום ומיהו אם א"א לישראל לבא פתאום לחנות כגון שיש חלון על הפתח שיכול העובד כוכבים לראות ממנו אם בא הישראל משמע דאסור משום דתו לא מסתפי עכ"ל ותמוהין לי דברי הרב במ"ש דאם יש חלון כו' דאסור דאדרבה כיון דיש חלון מרתת העובד כוכבים שיבא הישראל ויראה דרך אותו החלון כמ"ש בש"ע סי' קכ"ח ס"ג ואפי' את"ל דהיין אינו מונח נגד החלון כאן מ"מ קשה דהא לעיל בסי' קכ"ח ס"ג כתב באם יש נקבים בדלת ואין ישראל יכול לראות דמותר שהרי אין איסור רק אם סגר הדלת במנעול מבפנים אבל אם אין מנעול סגור מותר כל היין שבבית דמרתת ואין שם איסור מחמת שהעובד כוכבים יכול לראות דרך הנקבים ומ"ט יהיה אסור כאן כשיש חלון אם לא מבורר שסגר העובד כוכבים החנות הא ודאי ממש הדין דלעיל ונראה לתת טעם למה באמת אמרו דוקא במעביר ובספינה שצריך דרך עקלתון לפי שהם בשדה מקום פנוי ומ"ה כל שאין שם אלא דרך א' מצי עובד כוכבי' להזהר כל שרואה שהולך מרחוק וא"א שיבא בקרוב כ"כ אבל בבית או חנות אפשר שהלך בדרך קרוב מאד לאחד מן הבתים ובמהרה כהרף עין יבא ויראנו ומרתת ע"כ אין צריכין כאן דרך עקלתון רק בהנך תרתי דהיינו קרון וספינה ומעביר יינו אבל ברישא דמניח בחנות לא. ונ"ל פשוט דגם במעביר יין בין הבתים הוה הדין כן ולא שייך שם דרך עקלתון ותמיד מרתת העובד כוכבים שפתאום יבוא עליו ולא החמירו אלא במקום שהוא פנוי מבתים ואין שם אלא דרך אחד כן נראה לע"ד פשוט אע"פ שבפרישה כתב דלא קאי רק אבבא שלישית לחוד שהוא מעביר מ"מ נראה כמ"ש דקאי גם אמציעתא מאחר שהרשב"א כתב כן בפי' וחד טעמא אית לתרווייהו:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>When is this said? When there is a circuitous route. </b>In the Gemara this was stated regarding one who transports his wine, which is the third case mentioned by the Tur and the Shulchan Aruch at the beginning of the siman. The Beit Yosef wrote in the name of the Ran that only regarding one who transports the wine do we say this. But where one leaves a non-Jew in a shop and the Jew goes in and out, a circuitous route is not required, because at every moment the non-Jew is afraid that the Jew may now come and see him approaching the barrel. The Rashba wrote regarding a wagon and ship that a circuitous route is required, as I cited above. The Beit Yosef wrote that the Ran also agrees to this, and excluded only the case of leaving a non-Jew in a shop, because ordinarily the Jew can come there suddenly. However, if the Jew cannot come suddenly to the shop, such as where there is a window at the entrance through which the non-Jew can see whether the Jew is coming, it appears that it is forbidden, because he is no longer afraid, end quote. The Rav's words that if there is a window it is forbidden are astonishing to me. On the contrary, because there is a window, the non-Jew is afraid that the Jew may come and see him through that window, as stated in Shulchan Aruch, siman 128, seif 3. Even were you to say that the wine is not situated opposite the window, it remains difficult, for above, in siman 128, seif 3, he wrote that if there are holes in the door and the Jew cannot see through them, it is permitted. The prohibition applies only if the non-Jew locked the door from inside. But if the interior lock is not closed, all the wine in the house is permitted, because he is afraid, and there is no prohibition merely because the non-Jew can see through the holes. Why, then, should it be forbidden here when there is a window, unless it is established that the non-Jew closed the shop? This is exactly the same law as above. It appears possible to explain why they required a circuitous route specifically regarding one who transports wine and regarding a ship. They are in a field, an open place. Therefore, whenever there is only one route, the non-Jew can take precautions when he sees the Jew traveling far away, since the Jew cannot return so quickly. But in a house or shop, it is possible that the Jew went only a very short distance to one of the nearby houses, and in the blink of an eye he may return and see him. The non-Jew is therefore afraid. Accordingly, a circuitous route is required only in those two cases, namely a wagon or ship and one who transports his wine, but not in the first case of leaving him in a shop. It also appears obvious to me that if one transports wine between houses, the same law applies, and a circuitous route is irrelevant there. The non-Jew is constantly afraid that the Jew may suddenly come upon him. They were stringent only in an open area without houses, where there is only one route. This appears entirely clear in my humble opinion. Although the Perishah wrote that the requirement applies only to the third case, one who transports the wine, nevertheless it appears as I wrote, that it also applies to the middle case, since the Rashba wrote so explicitly and the same reason applies to both.

<b>או שהודיעו שהוא מפליג. </b>זה קאי אמה שהזכיר לעיל ג' דברים א' מניח עובד כוכבים בחנות ב' שהיה לו יין בספינה כו' ג' שהיה מעביר חבית ממקום למקום כו' ובהך הודעה שמפליג יש מחלוקת שהראב"ד והרשב"א ס"ל דדוקא שמודיעו בפירוש שמפליג כשיעור האיסור והרא"ש אוסר אפילו אומר בסתם שיפליג ויש להקל בזה כדעת הראב"ד והרשב"א דהם רבים לגבי הרא"ש ועוד קי"ל לקולא באם אומר שיפליג כדי פתיחת פתח החבית לחוד אע"פ ששוהה כדי שיפתח ויסתום ולא כאחרים שמביא ב"י בשם הר"ן דאוסרי' בזה וכ' תה"ה בשם הראב"ד דאפי' בהודיעו שמפליג מותר אם יש דרך עקלתון שיוכל לבוא תוך שיעור פתיחה וסתימה והוא לא אמר לכו ואבוא אחריכם:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Or he informed him that he was going far away. </b>This refers to the three cases mentioned above: first, one who leaves a non-Jew in a shop; second, one whose wine was on a ship, etc.; and third, one who was transporting a barrel from place to place, etc. Regarding informing him that he is going far away, there is a dispute. The Ra'avad and Rashba hold that it is forbidden only when he explicitly informs him that he will be away for the amount of time necessary to perform the forbidden act. The Rosh forbids even when he merely says in general that he is going far away. One may be lenient in accordance with the Ra'avad and Rashba, for they are the majority against the Rosh. Furthermore, we rule leniently where he says that he will be away only long enough to open the mouth of the barrel, even though he actually remains away long enough to open and reseal it, unlike the other authorities cited by the Beit Yosef in the name of the Ran, who forbid in this case. Toras HaBayis HaAruch wrote in the name of the Ra'avad that even if he informed him that he was going far away, it is permitted when there is a circuitous route by which he could return within the time required to open and reseal it, provided he did not say, Go, and I will come after you.

My father-in-law of blessed memory found these words of the Beit Yosef difficult, because the Tur should simply have said that it was impossible for the non-Jew to forge the entire seal. He therefore forced himself into explaining the Tur as speaking of two seals, but this is unnecessary.

<b>But when transporting, etc. </b>With a wooden stopper, however, it is permitted, as the Tur explicitly wrote in the name of the Ramban and the Rosh. It appears obvious that this applies even when the Jew does not accompany him at all. The Derishah understood that this applies only where the Jew accompanied him but merely diverted his attention, and because of this he entered into a difficulty and resolution. I do not know what compelled him to this. The Tur wrote that Rabbeinu Yitzchak permits even when it is completely open. My father-in-law of blessed memory resolves it differently. To me, however, there is no difficulty at all. Above, the non-Jew carries it in his hand, and he can easily touch it, making caution impossible. Here, he carries it on his shoulder in the usual manner of transporting a barrel from place to place, and he cannot touch it so easily.

<b>הרבה עוברי דרכים. </b>כ"כ רשב"א והתוס' פ' השוכר (דף ע') כתבו ג"כ בשם רשב"ם להקל אם הוא מעבר רבים אפילו עובד כוכבים וכ' דלא חיישינן לשמא נגע כיון דהאידנא לאו חיבת ניסוך עליהם וסיימו ע"ז היינו דוקא בנקב צר אבל בגיגית או כוס שפתחו רחב קל הוא ליגע ורגילות ליגע בהם דרך מתעסק עכ"ל וב"י מביאם וכתב ד"מ שיש פלוגתא בין רשב"א והרשב"ם דלרשב"ם א"צ שיהא פקוקה דא"כ אפילו בפתח רחב שרי ובדרישה כתב לחלק דכל שיש חיבת ניסוך עליו כמו לדין התלמוד הצריך הרשב"א שיהא פקוקה אפילו במעבר לרבים אפילו בנקב צר אבל התוס' דמיירי בזמן הזה שאין איסור משום ניסוך רק שמא יחליף להנאתו בזה מותר בפתוחה כל שהנקב צר וכן נ"ל עיקר דגם בזמן הזה שאין חיבת ניסוך מכל מקום כל שהנגיעה היא קרוב לודאי אסור אף בזמן הזה כמו שכתב בית יוסף בשם הרשב"א בר"ס קכ"ח לענין יחוד יין אצל עובד כוכבים ומ"ה גם כאן בנקב רחב הוא קרוב לדודאי שנגע ואסור גם עכשיו וע"כ יפה פסק רמ"א בסימן קכ"ח ס"ד לחלק בין נקב צר לרחב בזמן הזה:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Many travelers pass there. </b>So wrote the Rashba. Tosafos in the chapter HaSocher, folio 70a, likewise wrote in the name of the Rashbam to be lenient where it is a public thoroughfare, even if the passersby are non-Jews. They wrote that we are not concerned that someone touched it, because nowadays they do not have an attachment to idolatrous libation. They concluded that this applies specifically where the opening is narrow. But with a vat or cup whose opening is wide, it is easy to touch, and people commonly touch them incidentally, end quote. The Beit Yosef cites them, and the Darkei Moshe wrote that there is a dispute between the Rashba and Rashbam. According to the Rashbam, it need not be stoppered, for if it were stoppered, even a wide opening would be permitted. The Derishah distinguished as follows. Wherever there remains an attachment to libation, as under the law of the Gemara, the Rashba required it to be stoppered even on a public thoroughfare and even when the opening is narrow. Tosafos, however, discuss the present era, when the prohibition is not because of libation but only lest the non-Jew exchange it for his own benefit. In such a case, an open vessel is permitted when its opening is narrow. This appears to me to be the primary explanation. Even nowadays, although there is no attachment to libation, when contact is nearly certain it is forbidden, as the Beit Yosef wrote in the name of the Rashba at the beginning of siman 128 regarding leaving wine secluded with a non-Jew. The Beit Yosef explains why they must depart in alarm here. Without alarm, we are concerned that the non-Jew saw the Jew in the marketplace, left him there, and returned home to pour a libation. He is not afraid, because he left the Jew in the marketplace. But when they leave in alarm, a person sometimes leaves his companion in the marketplace, yet when the latter returns home he discovers that his companion preceded him, for in the confusion he passed before him unnoticed. The non-Jew therefore fears that perhaps the Jew preceded him, end quote. What he wrote, that without alarm we are concerned that the non-Jew saw him in the marketplace, is astonishing. Do we forbid wine merely out of doubt? How is this different from siman 128, seif 3, where out of doubt we do not say that the non-Jew locked the door, even though there is an interior key? This is also evident at the beginning of siman 129, as cited there. It is clear that wine is not forbidden until the basis for prohibition is established. This is also evident from the Mishnah concerning one who did not inform the non-Jew that he was going far away. We do not say that perhaps the non-Jew assumed that the Jew was going far away, though that was in fact the case. Likewise, in seif 10 we are lenient out of doubt, and so in many places. Furthermore, what the Beit Yosef wrote, that when they left in alarm, even if the non-Jew saw him in the marketplace he might not have noticed him, is an excessive leniency. The non-Jew's entry was not in alarm; only the departure was. Frequently a person leaves in alarm because of a disturbance outside, but once the disturbance subsides he regains composure. All the more so here, where the alarm was because of a wedding or funeral. Upon returning home there is no alarm. The Gemara and poskim should therefore have stated that the entry too was in alarm. It therefore appears to me that the reason the case specifies that they departed in alarm is to prevent us from forbidding the wine on the ground that the non-Jew is not afraid that the Jew is guarding it. For if the Jew did not trust the non-Jew, why did he go outside and leave his wine, without fearing that the non-Jew might lock the door, as he actually did, and pour a libation, since he has a connection to the house? Therefore, it was necessary that he leave in alarm, so that he did not remember his wine at that time. The non-Jew is then concerned that perhaps the Jew has already remembered his wine and returned home. According to this, if the non-Jew saw the Jew in the marketplace while returning home, it is clearly forbidden whenever the non-Jew locked the door from inside, even though the departure was in alarm. We do not say that the confusion causes him to fear that he did not see properly, for we have no proof for such a leniency, even if he was still confused upon entering. Conversely, where there was no alarm, there is no basis to be stringent merely because perhaps he saw the Jew in the marketplace, for we are not stringent out of doubt. Rather, the reason is that the non-Jew thinks that the Jew trusts him, for otherwise why did he leave his wine? Although at the beginning of this siman, when he leaves him in a shop without informing him that he is going far away, it is permitted, there we do not know that the non-Jew closed the door, so we say that he did not close it and is afraid that the Jew may return. Here, however, there is a definite adverse circumstance before us, for the door is certainly closed. The only ground for permission is that perhaps the Jew already preceded him upstairs. If the Jew did not depart in alarm, the non-Jew reasons that since he left deliberately he certainly remembered his wine. Why, then, would he have hurried so greatly to precede him? Therefore, they required that his departure be in alarm, so that he did not remember his wine at the time, but may subsequently have remembered it and hurried ahead. This appears entirely clear in my humble opinion.

These are likewise the words of the Ran in the chapter HaSocher. Necessarily, when the Ran wrote in the chapter Elu Metziyos that the closure proves that they did not touch it, he meant a clay seal. This contradicts the Rama's ruling that if it is closed with a stopper, meaning a wooden one, it is permitted, and that only a completely open vessel is forbidden. We have demonstrated that permission applies specifically when it is sealed with clay.

<b>And it goes without saying when there is no vineyard belonging to, etc. </b>It is difficult why he wrote, it goes without saying. It is further difficult that the Tur concludes with the reason that one would not ordinarily steal from this vineyard and hide the stolen item within it, end quote. Why is this reason necessary? In any event it is forbidden. [This is the correct reading, and so it appears in the Tur. Most editions read, permitted, which is an error. What he writes, and in the Gemara, etc., means that the Gemara rejected the proof by suggesting that perhaps the permission rests on the fact that one does not ordinarily hide it there. The Gemara discusses wine found in an orlah vineyard, while the surrounding vineyards provide the basis for permission. This corresponds to the latter clause of this ruling, and the same applies to be lenient, etc., because one reason applies to both. One who examines it will find this.] The general principle is that when majority and proximity conflict, we follow the majority. The Gemara wished to explain the ruling on the basis of majority, but rejected that proof by suggesting that perhaps the permission rests on the fact that one does not ordinarily hide stolen goods within that vineyard. This reason applies particularly to an isolated vineyard. A thief would not hide within it what he stole from that same vineyard, for its owner would certainly notice it when he found it there, since it was taken from him. The phrase, it goes without saying, is therefore well explained.
