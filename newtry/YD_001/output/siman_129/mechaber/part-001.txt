**** ENGLISH ****
The law of one who leaves wine in the hand of a non-Jew while a Jew goes out and comes in (and similar). In it are 20 seifim. If one leaves a non-Jew in his shop and went out; or he had wine on a ship or in a wagon and left it with the non-Jew and went to the beit knesset or to the bathhouse; or a non-Jew was transporting for him a barrel of wine from place to place and he left him alone with the wine and went out — it is permitted, even if he remained away for a long time, because the non-Jew is afraid that at any moment he may come and see him. And this is provided that it is not known that the non-Jew closed the shop, or that he moved the wagon or the ship in a way that they cannot see him. (And even if he closed the shop, so long as there is a crack or a hole through which one can see inside, it is considered like it is open.) In what case is this stated? When there is a winding route by which he can come suddenly without being seen; and similarly, when he went to the bathhouse, he went by a winding route in a way that the non-Jew does not know that he went to the bathhouse. But if the non-Jew saw that he went to enter the bathhouse, he knows that he will not hurry to come. And similarly, if he said to the non-Jew whom he left in the shop, “Guard for me,” he knows that he removed himself from guarding. And similarly, if he said to the non-Jew who is transporting the barrel, “Go and I will come after you,” or he informed him that he is departing: if the barrels are open, once they are out of his sight they are forbidden. If they are sealed: if in his departing there is time for him to remove the covers of the barrel entirely, whether of clay or of lime, and to return it and to wipe it dry, it is forbidden. {Rama: And there are those who say that if they are sealed, they are permitted in every case, for one seal suffices for wine; and one may rely on this in a case of great loss; and see below siman 130.}

If a Jew has wine in his storehouse, and the opening of the storehouse was closed with one key, and he found the opening open, and one non-Jew admits that he opened it in order to draw water from a pit that is in the storehouse in order to build his house — it is permitted. {Rama: A homeowner who told his maidservant to set the table, and he found wine on his table, and his non-Jewish maidservant says that she took the wine from his cellar where there is much wine: the wine in the cellar is permitted, for the non-Jewish maidservant is not believed, and say that she took it from elsewhere (a responsum in Mordechai, perek R. Yishmael); but that which is on the table is forbidden. And similarly, regarding a woman who hid the key of her cellar and did not find it in its place, and she found a jar of wine hidden in straw, and a non-Jewish maidservant says that she took the wine: the wine in the cellar is permitted and the maidservant is not believed (ibid., and Agudah, and Hagahot Ashiri). But if thieves entered the cellar and drew wine and left the spigots open, and afterwards the spigots were found closed, and a non-Jewish maidservant said that she closed them — since the non-Jewish maidservant was alone with the wine and is not “caught as a thief,” and there are indications that she closed them, the wine is forbidden (Mordechai at the end of perek R. Yishmael). However, in our times, when non-Jews do not pour libations, it is permitted, as was explained above in simanim 123 and 124.}
