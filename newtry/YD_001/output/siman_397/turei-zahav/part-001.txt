**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And a gentile speaking incidentally.</b> Maharshal cites Or Zarua: if a gentile woman incidentally tells Reuven that his sister died, Raavyah rules that he must mourn because her statement may be accepted to impose stringency. Another authority rules that the halachah follows the lenient view in mourning and a gentile is not believed to create mourning obligations. R. Yeshayah says that a gentile speaking incidentally is believed only in the special contexts of testimony permitting a woman to remarry, or where the gentile speaks about his own produce and says it is orlah. In ordinary testimony a gentile speaking incidentally is not believed, whether to permit or to prohibit.

<b>He does not mourn.</b> Since the matter is doubtful, we follow the lenient rule in mourning. Maharam Mintz, siman 95, discusses one who hears that a relative died but does not know whether the death occurred within thirty days or earlier. He considers whether mourning should be required. On the one hand, mourning is rabbinic and doubtful mourning is generally lenient. He cites Hilchot Semachot in the name of Maharam that one should not be stringent in doubtful mourning because such stringency can itself create leniencies, such as observing private mourning on Shabbos and neglecting Torah study throughout seven days. On the other hand, one might argue from chazakah that the deceased should be presumed to have remained alive until recently, just as one who leaves an elderly or sick person alive may continue acting on the presumption that he lives. Maharam Mintz does not reach a definitive conclusion. Mahari ibn Lev rules stringently on that basis, and my father-in-law does so here as well. In my opinion, however, there are strong proofs for leniency.

<br><b>First:</b> Pesachim teaches that an onen and one uncovering a collapsed structure may have the Pesach offering slaughtered on their behalf, because they are presently presumed fit. But if the person under the rubble is then found dead, the rescuer must bring Pesach Sheni because he was impure from the outset. Tosafot ask why we do not say the buried person was alive when rescue began and died only later. They answer that once he is found dead, all tumah is treated according to the state in which it is discovered. Thus we do not preserve the prior presumption of life once death is known. This is not merely a technical law of tumah, because the Mishnah derives a practical obligation to bring Pesach Sheni and does not treat the matter as doubtful lest he bring ordinary unconsecrated meat into the Temple courtyard.

<br><b>Second:</b> Ketubot discusses exchanging a cow for a donkey. The owner of the donkey pulled the cow, but before the other party could pull the donkey, the donkey died. The owner of the donkey must prove it was alive at the time of the cow's meshichah. Why do we not simply preserve the chazakah that it was alive until the later discovered death? Even the final explanation, which places the burden based on whose possession the uncertainty arose in, would be unnecessary if a presumption of continued life decided the matter.

<br><b>Third:</b> Even HaEzer siman 17 discusses a murdered man who is identified but where it is unknown when he died. Some permit assuming he was killed within three days and relying on recognition, while others are stringent because perhaps he died earlier. If ordinary chazakah of life remained after known death, we should simply say he was alive until recently.

The correct distinction is that the presumption of life operates only so long as we do not yet know the person is dead. Once death is certain, we no longer say he died only now. Tosafot in Pesachim state this explicitly: once found dead, we say he was already dead earlier. This differs from other chazakot, such as a mikveh presumed full or an animal presumed kosher until a treifah is discovered. Death is different because every living creature is destined to die. While death is unknown, we presume continued life. Once death is established, that presumption no longer tells us the death occurred only at the latest possible moment. Tur in Choshen Mishpat siman 65 uses a similar principle regarding a promissory note: one cannot simply preserve the chazakah that it remains unpaid because a note is inherently destined to be paid. Thus there is no basis to require mourning from a presumption that death occurred recently. I therefore ruled in practice that one need not mourn in this doubtful case, especially because the general rule in mourning is to follow the lenient side, and misplaced stringency can produce Shabbos leniencies and neglect of Torah study.

Maharam Mintz further asks what happens if, after thirty days have already passed, it later becomes clear that the death had in fact occurred within thirty days of the original report. Is that later clarification treated as a distant report, or must the person now observe full mourning because he had some knowledge within thirty days? He compares it to a case concerning twins where uncertainty about viability cannot be resolved until later. In my opinion no mourning is required once thirty days from the death have passed. This is no worse than one who knowingly or unknowingly failed to mourn at all until after thirty days, where there is no later compensatory mourning. I later saw Responsa Maharam Lublin cite Shulchan Aruch here as written and rule accordingly, but he did not sufficiently analyze the issue.
