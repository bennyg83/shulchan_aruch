**** ENGLISH ****
<b>There is no, etc.</b> As it is written, "You shall not follow their statutes." In Torat Kohanim, Parashat Acharei Mot: "You shall not follow their statutes", you shall not follow their customs in matters that they have established for themselves, such as theaters and circuses. Rabbi Meir says: These are the ways of the Amorites that the Sages enumerated. Rabbi Yehudah says: This includes that one should not shave the hair at the back of the head, grow a forelock, or cut the hair in the Roman style. Rambam explained in Sefer HaMitzvot, negative commandment 30, that the theaters and the like were types of gathering places where they assembled for idolatry. This is the meaning of, "He shall not build, etc." In Sifrei, Parashat Re'eh, the verse "Take care" establishes a prohibition, and "lest you be ensnared after them" means lest you be drawn after them or imitate them. One should not say: Since they go out wearing an avgit garment, I too shall go out wearing an avgit garment; since they go out wearing purple, I too shall go out wearing purple; since they go out wearing kilusin, I too shall go out wearing kilusin. All of these are types of garments. This is the meaning of, "They may not imitate them, nor, etc." Rambam wrote, and you already know the verse, "And all who wear foreign clothing." At the end of the eighth chapter of Sanhedrin it is taught that even changing the color of a shoelace is included. Rif explains there that the gentiles of that time made the straps of their shoes red, while Jews made theirs black, so that they would not wear gentile clothing. Arukh explains likewise. He also explains the incident involving a woman wearing a red garment at the beginning of the third chapter of Berakhot, 20a, where Rav Adda tore it because it was gentile clothing.

<b>Or in a matter, etc.</b> As stated in Torat Kohanim cited above, regarding their established customs. The term statute means a practice whose reason is not apparent, as stated in the sixth chapter of Yoma, 67b, concerning "My statutes." Rashi likewise writes in Parashat Kedoshim that statutes are royal decrees for which no reason is given. Ramban writes that they certainly have reasons, but their reasons are not revealed, like a king's decree enacted in his kingdom without revealing its benefit. All the practices enumerated at the end of the sixth chapter of Shabbos and in chapter 7 of the Tosefta are of this kind. Thus the Gemara there says: It is understandable that one who loads him with stones, etc. It also states there that any practice having a medicinal purpose is not prohibited as a gentile custom. This is the meaning of, "But, etc.," and likewise practices performed for a purpose. The Tosefta there teaches: One who says, "Do not interrupt between us, lest our affection be interrupted," violates the prohibition against the ways of the Amorites; but if he says it as an expression of honor, it is permitted. Although the Amorites commonly practiced it, it is nevertheless permitted. So rules Maharik. Ran similarly writes in the first chapter of Avodah Zarah, regarding the statement that one burns objects in honor of kings, that the Torah prohibited only idolatrous statutes and empty practices, all of which serve idolatry. A practice with a rational purpose is permitted. Burning the king's possessions has a reason, namely, to honor him by ensuring that no one else uses them. This is the meaning of, "Therefore they said, etc." So too the Gemara there, 11a, asks: If it is a gentile statute, etc.; and answers that all agree, etc., rather it is an expression of distinction. However, Sanhedrin 52b appears to imply the opposite. A baraita there records Rabbi Yehudah's challenge to the Sages, and their response concerning the ways of the Amorites. This implies that without the Scriptural source the practice would be forbidden, even though beheading has a substantial reason, namely, avoiding a degrading form of execution. See Tosafot there, s.v. "Rather," and Tosafot in Avodah Zarah there, s.v. "And if." The essence of their explanation is that there are two kinds of statutes. One is connected to idolatry, and is forbidden even where a similar act is written in the Torah. The other consists of foolish and empty practices, and is permitted only where the practice is written in the Torah. According to them, the Gemara's statement, "Rather, it is an expression of distinction," does not mean, as Ran says, that the practice has a rational reason and is therefore permitted. Rather, it means that their custom of burning possessions for an important person is not idolatrous. The Gemara in Sanhedrin therefore properly says that, were it not for the Scriptural source, the practice would be forbidden. According to Maharik, however, one would have to explain the opposite, that the practice has no reason. Nevertheless, this requires study, for execution by the sword does have a reason. One may answer that had execution by the sword not been written in the Torah, we would not administer that form of execution at all. Therefore the Gemara says, "Since execution by the sword is written, etc." Yet this is apparently difficult, because severing the head is not written explicitly. Ran's words are therefore exceedingly difficult, as are the Rama's words. <br><b>(Collected note) All this, etc., and therefore they said, etc.</b> I have already written that Ran's words are difficult, and Maharik's words also do not appear correct in light of Sanhedrin 52b, where Rabbi Yehudah challenges the Sages, even though execution by the sword has an evident reason. Although I strained to reconcile their views, their ruling does not appear correct to me in practice. Burning possessions in honor of kings certainly has a reason, as Ran writes, yet the Gemara still asks that, were it not for the Scriptural source, it would be forbidden. Rather, every imitation is forbidden only when the practice is learned from the gentiles. As the Gemara says, since it is written in the Torah, we do not derive it from them. The decisive point is not merely that it is written in the Torah, but that any practice we would have followed independently of them is permitted. The same applies to clothing. Wearing a garment specifically associated with them is forbidden, in accordance with the plain meaning of Rif's words concerning even a shoelace. Certainly, the issue of a single strap is not immodesty. This is why the Gemara says, "Even a shoelace," and why Sifrei states, "Since they go out wearing, etc." A garment that we would have worn regardless of their practice is permitted. Maharik's proofs therefore present no difficulty, because the practices in those cases were not uniquely associated with gentiles. Rambam likewise prohibits only that which is distinctive to them. The same principle applies in every case. Maharik's position does not appear correct at all. (End of collected note.)
