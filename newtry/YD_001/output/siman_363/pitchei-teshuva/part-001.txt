<b>One does not remove.</b> Baer Heitev of Maharit cites Chacham Tzvi that one buried in gentile-owned ground may be exhumed and reburied in a Jewish cemetery. Tiferet Tzvi, Yoreh Deah simanim 59 and 60, agrees and adds that people cannot be forced to remain buried in an old cemetery merely because choosing a new cemetery may imply slight disrespect to those already buried there. A person need not accept even a small degradation in order to avoid such an implication. Noda BiYehudah, Yoreh Deah siman 89, cites R. Moshe Fischels regarding a cemetery leased for only one hundred fifty years, after which the land was to revert to the owners for cultivation or building. The descendants wished to purchase a permanent cemetery and transfer the graves there. He ruled against immediate exhumation for several reasons and distinguished the Chacham Tzvi case, where transfer to a Jewish cemetery itself constituted greater honor. Noda BiYehudah agreed, adding that perhaps in the distant future circumstances would change, the dead would arise, or the land could be purchased permanently. However, this applies only where the community is assured that when the lease expires it will still be permitted to move the remains before the land reverts. If there is a genuine concern that permission will later be denied, they may move the remains now. In any case, from now on they should not bury additional dead in a temporary cemetery when permanent land is available. Chatam Sofer, siman 334, discusses a cemetery assigned during an epidemic, not given permanently, unfenced, unguarded, and subject under local law to removal after six years. A permanent Jewish cemetery had become available. He rules that transfer is plainly permitted and indeed a mitzvah. This is especially so for those whose relatives are buried in the established cemetery, but applies to the other epidemic dead as well because the temporary place is unsecured and liable to desecration. Once the townspeople's own relatives are removed, the public cannot be compelled to undertake large expense to preserve a temporary cemetery for transient strangers. Moreover, burial in land not belonging to the Jewish community resembles burial in a grave not one's own, whereas the established cemetery belongs to the communal burial society and every buried person has a share in it. He therefore urges prompt transfer.

<b>And it is permitted to place lime.</b> Shevut Yaakov, part 2, siman 97, was asked during an epidemic when the authorities ordered that a deceased person either be buried in the family cemetery after lime was poured upon the body to hasten decomposition, or else be buried in an isolated forest. He analyzes Rashba and concludes that it is preferable to use lime and preserve burial in the regular cemetery rather than bury the person in an unprotected forest like an abandoned corpse, where animals could disturb the body. Har Avel, branch 11, discusses someone who instructed his sons to place lime in his grave so his body would decompose rapidly. Although this is technically permitted under siman 363, he writes that one should not devise artifices against the natural process established by Hashem. The custom of scattering earth from Eretz Yisrael upon the body is different, because it is based on the verse "His land will atone for His people" and has a Yerushalmi basis. Noda BiYehudah, second edition, introduction, reports that he himself instructed that earth from Eretz Yisrael prepared for him be scattered on his body. Noda BiYehudah, second edition, Yoreh Deah siman 210, was asked whether physicians may dissect the body of a person who died after unsuccessful surgery for bladder stone in order to discover the cause and thereby save future lives. He rules that if another patient with the same condition is presently before us, it is permitted; otherwise it is forbidden. Chatam Sofer, siman 336, similarly writes that if a comparable patient is presently at hand and dissection is needed for that patient's treatment, it is very likely permitted. But one may not sell his body during life to physicians for dissection after death merely for general medical study, because there is no present pikuach nefesh. Without pikuach nefesh there are concerns of prohibited benefit and degradation of the dead.

<b>To open the grave.</b> At the end of Chavot Yair, in a responsum by R. David Oppenheim, page 247, he cites R. Gershon of Metz concerning two deceased persons who had been buried without one customary burial garment. One was over twenty and one under twenty. He ordered the younger person's grave opened to supply the missing garment because the terror of judgment was not a concern for a minor, but prohibited opening the adult's grave because of the fear and disturbance of judgment, comparing it to Shmuel's words, "Why have you disturbed me?" R. David Oppenheim questions this from Bava Batra 155. See Chacham Tzvi, siman 47, and Knesset Yechezkel, siman 44, who defends R. Gershon's ruling. Shivat Tzion, simanim 64 to 66, discusses an unidentified murder victim whose clothing and papers suggested he was a married man who had disappeared six months earlier. After burial, the wife arrived and said her husband had distinctive bodily marks. Could the grave be opened to inspect those marks and free her from agunah? R. Elazar Fleckeles initially permitted it, arguing that even purchasers may accept degradation of a corpse where their money depends upon it, and here the wife seeks freedom from lifelong agunah. The distinctive signs would not change, and identifying him would also honor him because his children could mourn and recite Kaddish. The author of Shivat Tzion disagreed and prohibited opening the grave, and R. Fleckeles ultimately accepted his reasoning: in the Bava Batra case identity of the corpse was certain and only age was disputed; here perhaps the corpse is not the husband at all, so the grave could be disturbed for nothing. Knesset Yechezkel, Even HaEzer siman 46, however, states simply that one may open a grave to identify the deceased in order to permit his wife, because even relocation to one's own grave can justify disturbing the body, all the more so freeing an agunah. That proof is not conclusive.
