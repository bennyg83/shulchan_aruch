**** ENGLISH ****

See also the Ran, chapter 3 of Shevuot. In many places oaths are called vows, and proofs are brought from oaths to vows, both for leniency and for stringency, as in Nedarim 64b and 65a. Thus it is evident.

Rashi, however, holds that according to Rava even linking to a vow is ineffective. See his comments there, s.v. “Provided that it was vowed,” and s.v. “Correct,” etc. The Rif explains that the initial assumption was that “issar” denotes linking. Since the scriptural issar is a form of linking, it would necessarily apply also to an oath, for its principal verse concerns an oath, “or bound herself by an issar upon her soul through an oath,” and vows are learned from there. The tanna mentioned a vow to teach the greater novelty. Thus the Gemara asks: this is understandable according to Abaye, etc., but difficult according to Rava, who said that issar is not linking, while here it is said to be linking; moreover, if so, it should apply to an oath as well. The answer is: correct the teaching. It is not discussing issar, but means: what expression renders something forbidden like a vow? “It is upon me,” etc. In other words, linking to a vowed object is itself a vow, but linking to an oath is not effective. The law of linking is not learned from issar, but from “When a man vows a vow,” requiring that he vow through something already vowed, as stated in Nedarim 14a, where something inherently forbidden is excluded. So too, one may exclude something merely sworn upon. The Ran states this in the Ramban’s name, and the Rosh agrees. See Tosafot there, s.v. “Rather,” etc. The Ran explains “correct the teaching” like Rashi, as referring to the basic law of a vow, which takes effect only when attached to something vowed. Linking is therefore also a vow, since the essence of a vow operates through linkage, unlike an oath. Nevertheless, linking to a vow is valid, contrary to Rashi, as Tosafot and the Rif prove from the beginning of chapter 4 of Nazir: one who said, etc. Although there the rule applies specifically within the time required for speech, while linkage can occur later, as in “like the day Gedaliah died,” the cases differ. Nor can one say that saying “and I” immediately is simply equivalent to saying “I am a nazirite,” rather than linkage, for the latter clause teaches: if the first was released, etc. We also learned: “I am like Samson, like Delilah’s husband,” etc., and many similar cases. This is the Ran’s explanation.

<b>If he ate, etc.</b> See the discussion there. Rashi’s view is that everything depends upon the first eating, whether he first ate the condition or the prohibited item. If he ate it knowingly, the oath takes effect; if unknowingly, it does not, and he may initially eat the other item, as the Ran explains. This follows from the Gemara’s statement, “We too have learned: Konam,” etc., “he is permitted,” meaning initially permitted with his wife, and from the baraita cited there. As stated above, since it is a mistaken vow he is permitted in the loaf. Certainly, if he ate the prohibited item first, he may then eat the condition, because the oath never took effect. Rashi interprets “the first” as the condition. When the Gemara says, “The first unknowingly and the second knowingly, he is exempt,” the same applies if he first ate the prohibited item unknowingly and later the condition knowingly. The wording follows the latter clause. In both cases “exempt” means exempt and permitted, as above, and the Ran agrees. When it says, “The first knowingly and the second unknowingly, he is liable,” that applies specifically where the condition was eaten first. If he first ate the prohibited item knowingly and later the condition unknowingly, he incurs neither a sacrifice, because the inadvertence must concern the prohibited act, nor lashes, because the condition was later eaten unknowingly. Tosafot’s discussion supports this reading. Thus the opening clause also says specifically, “the first unknowingly,” etc. If both were eaten unknowingly, he is exempt, whether the condition was first, and it is obvious that even if the second was eaten knowingly he remains exempt. If both were eaten knowingly, he incurs lashes whether the condition or the prohibited item was eaten first, for we rule like Rabbi Yochanan. Rabbeinu Yitzchak’s view in Tosafot is that everything depends on eating the condition. If the condition was eaten knowingly, the oath takes effect whether it was eaten first or later; if unknowingly, it does not take effect in either order. “The first” means the condition, as Rashi explains, but the clauses are exact: “the first unknowingly,” etc., means that whenever the condition is eaten unknowingly and the prohibited item knowingly, he is exempt, regardless of order; “the first knowingly and the second unknowingly, he is liable,” means that when the condition is eaten knowingly and the prohibited item unknowingly, he is liable, regardless of order. Thus the dispute between Rashi and Rabbeinu Yitzchak concerns only a case where the prohibited item was eaten first unknowingly and the condition later knowingly: according to Rabbeinu Yitzchak he is liable, while according to Rashi he is exempt. The Tur writes that their disagreement has practical effect only regarding the sacrifice, not regarding the prohibition, and the Shulchan Aruch follows that formulation. This requires study, because it also affects the prohibition: according to Rashi, after eating the prohibited item unknowingly he may eat the condition initially, while according to Rabbeinu Yitzchak he would even incur a sacrifice. The statement here, “If he first ate the condition,” therefore follows all opinions, but if he ate the prohibited item first it is disputed. In seif 17 the Shulchan Aruch rules like Rashi, as explained there. The Raavad also follows Rabbeinu Yitzchak, and Rabbeinu Chananel does likewise, although he calls the prohibited item “the first” and the condition “the second,” and reverses the readings of liability and exemption. The Rambam has another approach: he also reads like Rabbeinu Chananel but, following Tosafot’s terminology, calls the condition “the first,” understands liability as lashes, and makes everything depend on the time the prohibited item is eaten, regardless of whether the condition was eaten knowingly or unknowingly. See Kesef Mishneh; his proof from “Konam if I ate or drank,” etc., requires study. Rabbeinu Tam follows Rashi but interprets “the first” as whichever item was actually eaten first, whether condition or prohibition, in both clauses. He disagrees with Rashi in holding that if the prohibited item was first eaten knowingly and the condition later unknowingly, one is liable to a sacrifice, because inadvertence is needed when the oath is violated, not specifically when the prohibited item is eaten. On his reading the discussion is straightforward, since in both clauses the order makes no difference; Rashi must distinguish between the clauses. See what is written below in seif 17.

<b>If he ate, etc.</b> This is the case in Shevuot where he made each dependent on the other. Here there is certainly no difference which one he ate first, because each is both a condition and a prohibited item. In the second clause, however, Rashi and Rabbeinu Yitzchak differ. In the first clause they agree. According to Rashi, if he ate each knowingly with respect to itself but unknowingly regarding the other, he is exempt from the first, since although the oath took effect through his knowing act, he incurs neither a sacrifice nor lashes for the first, as explained above. This compelled Rashi not to explain like Rabbeinu Tam; according to Rabbeinu Tam he should be liable for the first. Tosafot struggle to answer for Rabbeinu Tam and call the answer forced. Rashi also did not wish to explain like Rabbeinu Yitzchak, that “the first” always means the condition, because in the latter clause the Gemara says, “he ate its condition,” rather than again saying “the first and then the second”; why change the wording? Tosafot discuss this. He is also exempt from the second, because the first was eaten unknowingly with respect to it. According to Rabbeinu Yitzchak too he is exempt from both, because with each item its condition was eaten unknowingly. If he ate each unknowingly with respect to itself but knowingly regarding the other, according to Rashi he is liable only for the second, not for the first, because the first was eaten unknowingly with respect to itself and the oath did not take effect upon it. According to Rabbeinu Yitzchak he is liable for both, since even when the prohibited item is first eaten unknowingly and the condition later knowingly, liability applies retroactively. If both were eaten unknowingly he is exempt; if both knowingly he is liable for both, following Rabbi Yochanan. Thus all agree on exemption from both and liability for both in those final cases. The Tur again writes that the dispute has practical effect only regarding a sacrifice, but regarding prohibition one may not eat either item, and if he ate one and then remembered, the Shulchan Aruch follows this. These words are surprising, for that is correct only according to Rashi. According to Rabbeinu Yitzchak, even if the first was eaten entirely unknowingly and the second knowingly, he is liable to a sacrifice for the first, yet the Tur says the second may be eaten initially. The Perishah labors greatly to explain this, but the truth is that the ruling follows Rashi. Perhaps the Tur rules anonymously like Rashi, as the Ran writes in his novellae that Rashi’s view is the clearest, since all other approaches face the difficulties noted above. The same applies to the statement in seif 16, “Likewise, if he made a loaf conditional,” etc., and then forgot and ate it, whether it was the condition or the prohibited item. Still, this requires study, for if so, why say there specifically, “If he first ate the condition”? It would be a greater novelty even if he first ate the prohibited item, as stated here in seif 17. The wording “he ate it” there also sounds like the condition. Thus seif 16 appears to follow Tosafot, while seif 17 follows Rashi. The Tur’s statement that the dispute has no practical effect regarding prohibition, and certainly the Shulchan Aruch’s statement here that it has no such effect, also remain difficult. The Rambam has a different reading; see Kesef Mishneh. Nevertheless, in my humble opinion Rabbeinu Yitzchak’s view is the clearest. Although the wording “he ate its condition” is imprecise, as Tosafot note, Rashi’s reading requires differentiating between the opening clause, “he ate the first unknowingly,” and the second clause, “the first knowingly,” etc., while Rabbeinu Yitzchak does not. In the final case where each is made dependent on the other, the second clause, “this one unknowingly regarding itself,” etc., would have to mean that liability applies only to the second, which should have been explained; moreover, it would not parallel the opening exemption. Finally, since the Gemara concludes, “both knowingly,” concerning the second one, it implies that in the preceding clause liability applies to both according to all opinions.
