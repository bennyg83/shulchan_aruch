**** ENGLISH ****
<b>But if he swore to violate a rabbinic commandment, etc.</b> This is difficult, for above, siman 238, seif 4, the Mechaber rules like Rambam that one who swears to eat less than a kezayis of neveilah is bound by the oath, although eating half the prohibited measure is forbidden by Torah law, since no Torah lashes are imposed for it. Here, however, he rules that an oath does not take effect even against a rabbinic prohibition. Perhaps his meaning is that the oath does not authorize the transgression, but must instead be released. Likewise, in siman 238, “he is liable for the oath” may mean that he requires release. Another resolution follows the responsum of Maharal ibn Chaviv, siman 103, explaining the Ran: a rabbinic prohibition may be more stringent in this respect than half the Torah measure. The latter is derived only from an inclusive phrase and has no distinct negative commandment explicitly directed to it, and an oath can take effect upon a matter whose prohibition is not explicit. A rabbinic prohibition, however, is supported by the commandment not to deviate from the sages, and the sages may also have reinforced their enactments like Torah law. Maharal ibn Chaviv discusses the practical ruling at length, as does the Mabit in part 2, siman 51. Maharshal and Bach rule that when one swears to commit a rabbinic transgression, although some say the oath takes effect with respect to a korban and lashes, we compel him to seek release and not transgress. The Levush writes similarly. This implies that even where the oath inclusively covers permitted and forbidden matters, he should not transgress but should obtain release, as is also evident from the Ran’s responsum concerning an oath that included principal and interest. Maharshal writes concerning the Tur in the name of the Rosh that other authorities disagree and compel him to seek release from his vow, and that this is the primary ruling; the Bach cites this in his conclusion. It therefore appears that even one who swore to nullify a rabbinic mitzvah is compelled to seek release. Although I do not know precisely which authorities Maharshal intended, and the plain meaning of the poskim and the ruling above in siman 215 do not appear so, one should nevertheless be stringent. Alternatively, the present discussion addresses only the strict law, while it is certainly proper to compel him to seek release.

<b>He said, “I hereby become, etc.”</b> Meaning that even if he says, “I hereby become a nazir today,” he must observe two periods of nezirus, each of thirty days. We rule that even one who accepts nezirus for a single day must observe thirty days; since nezirus can take effect upon an existing nezirus, he must therefore observe two consecutive thirty-day periods. Know that nezirus applies both when the Beis HaMikdash stands and when it does not, in Eretz Yisrael and outside it, as is evident at the end of the third chapter of Nazir. Rambam, however, writes at the end of the second chapter of Hilchos Nazir that one who accepts nezirus nowadays outside Eretz Yisrael is compelled to ascend to Eretz Yisrael and observe it there. While still outside Eretz Yisrael he may not drink wine, become impure through the dead, or cut his hair, and all details of nezirus apply to him, although those days do not count. Semag writes likewise. Raavad disputes Rambam and says that he is not compelled to ascend, since the laws of purity are not practiced among us and we are all impure through the dead, leaving no relevant distinction between Eretz Yisrael and elsewhere. See Kesef Mishneh and Tosafos Yom Tov there. In any event, it is clear that nezirus takes effect in every place and era. This is also implied by the Mechaber’s treatment of nezirus here and in the other cited simanim. He omitted the remaining laws only because the Tur and other poskim likewise omitted them. Rambam and Semag should be consulted for their distinctions, from which many laws of vows may also be learned. Although a doubtful nezirus is treated leniently, a doubtful vow is treated stringently. It nevertheless appears that Rambam and Semag compel ascent only in an ordinary nezirus, because of the impurity of foreign lands, as Kesef Mishneh implies. A person who accepted the nezirus of Shimshon, however, may become impure and is forbidden only wine and haircutting; he therefore observes that nezirus outside Eretz Yisrael. This explains why Rambam and Semag placed the requirement to ascend only before discussing the nezirus of Shimshon. Release by a sage is effective for ordinary nezirus just as for vows, as the Mechaber writes above at the end of siman 229 and Rambam rules at the beginning of the second chapter of Hilchos Nazir. Its source is found in several passages of Nedarim and Nazir. A person who accepted the nezirus of Shimshon, however, cannot obtain release, as Rambam rules, based on Nazir and Makkos. A father or husband may annul the nezirus of his daughter or wife just as he may annul vows, as the Mechaber and Rambam rule. Regarding the nezirus of Shimshon, however, the Mabit, citing Rabbi Yaakov Beirav, writes that a husband cannot annul it. See the later responsa for many rulings concerning ordinary nezirus and the nezirus of Shimshon.

<b>An oath does not take effect, etc.</b> Maharam of Prague, responsum 181, discusses one who vowed that each time he performed a certain act he would pay one peshutah. What remedy is available, since the monetary obligation cannot be released before it takes effect? Must he therefore approach a sage for release after every occurrence, or do we say that once the first is released all are released, because none can take effect before the first? The Riva leaves the matter unresolved. Maharam Mintz explains the doubt as follows. If the declaration is comparable to repeated oaths not to eat the same loaf, the later oaths do not presently take effect; if the first is released, the second then takes effect, and release would be required each time. Alternatively, since he expressed everything in one oath, it may all constitute a single oath, so that release of the first releases all. The wording of the original question, which speaks of a person who made a vow, implies a genuine vow: he declared the act forbidden to himself and added that if he performed it he would pay a peshutah. Payment does not repair the violation of the vow; rather, the monetary obligation begins only after he transgresses. This resembles one who swore not to speak with a certain person and then swore that if he sought release from the first oath, meat would become forbidden to him. He may seek release from the first oath, but not yet from the second, because it has not taken effect. The questioner therefore remained uncertain whether release would be needed after every violation or whether release of the first would release them all.

<b>This applies specifically, etc.; but if he said it twice, etc.</b> Rama inferred in Darkei Moshe from the Gemara in the second chapter of Nedarim that, just as one who says twice, “I hereby become a nazir today,” must observe two periods of nezirus, the same should apply to vows and oaths. Mordechai and Tosafos, however, indicate that one nezirus takes effect upon another only when both are accepted on the same day, for the second declaration then clearly intends an additional period. They imply that if he first accepts thirty days and then forty days, the first thirty may count toward both because the second declaration merely adds days. Darkei Moshe applies this reasoning to vows as well. In my humble opinion, this proof is not compelling. The special rule is stated specifically regarding nezirus, which Scripture teaches can take effect upon an existing nezirus. The Mishnah itself illustrates “a vow within a vow” through repeated nezirus rather than through an ordinary vow, because a nazir’s undertaking is also called a vow. Ran explains that the second nezirus takes effect immediately within the period of the first, although it cannot be counted until the first concludes. Ordinary vows have no comparable rule. Tosafos likewise asks why the Mishnah abandons ordinary vows and answers that such a case cannot be found in ordinary vows; nezirus is different because it has a fixed thirty-day term. This appears to be the Beis Yosef’s intent in disputing the Tur and saying that twenty-two days suffice in the case before him, since the rule of repeated nezirus derives from the minimum thirty-day term and does not establish that every ordinary vow stacks upon itself. The Levush therefore omitted Rama’s statement that repeating a twenty-day abstention from meat creates forty days, and instead explains that only where the second vow adds days may the total be extended. Even should one interpret repeated declarations of a twenty-day abstention as intending a second twenty-day period, that result would flow from the person’s intent, not from a legal principle that one ordinary vow takes effect within another. If he intended the second vow merely to rest upon the first, he would be forbidden only twenty days. Rashba likewise distinguishes ordinary vows from nezirus. Rama’s ruling is therefore not conclusive.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>If he swore, “I will not eat,” etc.</b> Derishah asks how, according to this, a mistaken expressive oath can ever require a korban, since the Tur and Mechaber rule above at the beginning of siman 236 that an oath concerning either the past or the future can require a korban. He answers that if a person swears falsely about a fact, such as saying, “I swear that I ate today,” when he did not, his forgetfulness makes him inadvertent and liable. Here, however, he did not swear directly to a fact but conditionally, such as, “If so-and-so said this, I will not eat this loaf,” and it turned out that he had said it; because the oath rested upon a mistaken condition, it never took effect. This is surprising, for an explicit Gemara contradicts him. Shevuos 26 asks what constitutes inadvertence in an expressive oath concerning the past and answers: he knew that the false oath was forbidden but did not know that it required a korban. Rambam rules accordingly. Nevertheless, regarding the permissibility of a conditionally prohibited loaf, it appears that the case is considered a mistaken oath only when, at the moment of swearing, he was certain that the condition was false. For example, he swore not to eat the loaf if a certain person had said something, while being certain that the person had not said it. This accords with the Gemara’s expression, “Your heart compelled you.” If he was uncertain whether the statement had been made, or did not know at all, the oath is not a mistaken oath and the loaf is forbidden.

<b>He did not remember, etc.</b> The Levush questions this, since even if he did not remember the oath, the second item should still be forbidden, and he discusses the matter at length. Bach likewise questions it and writes that according to Rabbi Yochanan the second item is forbidden. In Perishah he explains that when the person made each item dependent upon the other, he intended the first one eaten to serve as the condition. His oath was structured as follows: “If I eat this one, I will not eat the other.” The second item was to be the prohibited item; he did not intend each to function simultaneously as both condition and prohibition. Before he ate either, each bore both possible designations because he could choose which to eat first. Once he ate one, his intention identified it as the condition, leaving only the other as the prohibited item. Consequently, even if he later eats the second deliberately, there is no further liability for an oath, even according to Rabbi Yochanan, because the oath takes effect only when the condition is eaten deliberately.
