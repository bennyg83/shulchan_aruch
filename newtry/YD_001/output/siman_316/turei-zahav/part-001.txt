<b>Or if a gentile speaks incidentally, not in order to enhance the sale, and so forth.</b> A case arose in which a Jew was owed money by a gentile. The gentile had no cash and offered a cow. He showed him a cow with a calf beside it but not nursing. The gentile said he would give the cow without the calf for eight gold coins because he wished to raise the calf. The Jew replied that the cow looked old and perhaps this was her sixth or seventh calf, and that he wanted a young cow and would pay more for a younger animal. The gentile answered: "I cannot tell you she is very young, but she is not as old as you say. Believe me, this is only her fourth calf." The Jew accepted the cow, and it later gave birth while in his possession. At first glance the newborn is a doubtful firstborn, because a gentile speaking incidentally is generally not believed. In my opinion, however, there is strong reason to permit. Bechorot discusses purchasing from a gentile. Rav says the next birth is certainly a firstborn, because had the cow previously given birth the seller would have praised it as one that had already calved and was no longer exposed to the danger of first birth. Shmuel treats the matter as doubtful, and R. Yochanan says it is certainly ordinary, because had it not yet calved the seller would have warned the Jew about the prohibition. Even though the halachah does not follow Rav, the others disagree only because competing inferences exist. Where no competing inference exists, all should agree that failure to enhance the sale is significant. Here, if the cow had truly never calved and the nearby calf was not hers, the gentile could have increased the price by telling the Jew that she was younger. The Jew expressly said he would pay more for a younger cow. Instead, the gentile reduced his own sale advantage by saying this was already her fourth calf. This is stronger than ordinary incidental testimony. We permit not because a gentile is intrinsically believed, but because his willingness to lose money is a powerful indication that he spoke truthfully. Gittin gives a parallel regarding a scribe who says a Sefer Torah was written improperly. Where his statement causes him to lose his entire wage, we say perhaps he is telling the truth and accept the inference. <br><b>I found</b> further support in Maharik, who likewise relied on a gentile confession before a judge where the confession exposed him to serious personal risk. All the more so here, where the gentile had every incentive to claim the cow was younger and thereby receive a higher price, yet did the opposite. We may therefore rely on the inference that the cow had already calved and the later calf born to the Jew was not a firstborn.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Nevertheless it is forbidden.</b> The reason follows Piskei Mahari, siman 130, citing Sha'arei Dura regarding a wolf that attacked an animal. Ri would not permit on a double doubt: perhaps it attacked with its teeth, and even if with its claw, perhaps this is not the injured animal, because once the first doubt is Torah-level it is not nullified even in a majority, certainly not one against one. I have difficulty with this. Beit Yosef in siman 57 already asks how Ri could disagree with the Talmudic rule of sefek sefeika in a Torah prohibition, and answers that the first doubt had already become known separately. In siman 110 I gave another explanation based on Semag and Semak, that the permissibility of sefek sefeika depends on whether the doubts could have arisen and been resolved successively, or whether the two doubts are truly equal, as in two mixtures. None of that applies here where two offspring were born, one male and one female. There is a double doubt regarding the male: perhaps the mother had already given birth previously, and even if not, perhaps the male was born after the female. Why should we not apply sefek sefeika and say there is no firstborn status? If two males were born and there were doubt which came first, then certainly one of them is a firstborn and, because both belong to one person and the questions cannot be separated, this resembles two paths where both ask simultaneously and we act stringently. But with one male and one female, there should be room for leniency. Perhaps this is why Rama framed the example of sefek sefeika as two offspring, where each individually can be said not to be the firstborn, rather than Mahari's example of one male and one female. The matter requires practical study.

<b>עד שיודיעו המוכר. </b>הכי אית ליה לר' יוחנן העתקתיו לעיל בסעיף ג'. ויש אומרים ס"ל כשמואל דלעיל דהספק הוא שמא ישחטנה תיכף והקשה על זה בדרישה מהא דכתב בסעיף ט"ז ולענין אותו ואת בנו באם שחטו אמו היום על המוכר להודיעו וכל שלא הודיעו אין חושש והיינו כרבי יוחנן דהכא ותירץ דסתם מי שקונה בהמה קונהו כדי לשחטו מיד ומש"ה ודאי מודיע אותו מש"ה נמי כאן אף אם לא ילדה כבר אין צריך להודיע שלא בכרה דהא מסתמא ישחטנה תיכף עכ"ל <i data-commentator="Nekudot HaKesef" data-label="♯" data-order="2"></i>ודברים תמוהים כתב שהרי בפרק קמא דחולין כתב לענין שחט בסכין של עובד כוכבים בהמה בריאה שאין איסור דהוה מקלקל שבהמה בחייה עומדת לגדל ולדות כו' ותו דאם כן היה קשה מאי טעמא דרב ורבי יוחנן דעל כל אחד קשה הא לשחיטה קיימא מסתמא ועיקר קושייתו נראה לתרץ דודאי התם אמרינן שפיר אם יש איסור היה מודיע שאין סברא כנגד זה על כן ודאי אזלינן בתר סברא זאת מה שאין כן כאן שיש ב' סברות מכחישות זו את זו דהיינו אם איתא שבכרה כבר הוה אשבוחי משבח וסברא כנגדה אם איתא דלא בכרה עדיין היה לו להודיע לאפרושי מאיסורא על כן נשאר הדבר בספק לשמואל) וזה ברור ופשוט לפי ע"ד:

<b>Until the seller informs him.</b> This is R. Yochanan's view cited above. The dissenting view follows Shmuel, who treats the matter as doubtful because perhaps the buyer intends immediate slaughter. Derishah asks from seif 16 concerning oto ve'et beno: if the seller slaughtered the mother that day, he must inform the purchaser, and absent such notification the buyer need not suspect. That is like R. Yochanan here. Derishah answers that ordinarily one who buys an animal intends immediate slaughter, and therefore the seller would certainly notify him. He then says the same logic means that here, even if the cow had never calved, the seller need not warn him, because he assumes immediate slaughter. This is astonishing. Chullin says that slaughtering a healthy animal with a gentile's knife can be considered destructive because a living animal stands to produce offspring, showing that an animal is not simply presumed to be for immediate slaughter. Moreover, if that were the ordinary presumption, Rav and R. Yochanan themselves would be difficult. The better answer is that in the oto ve'et beno case only one inference exists: if a prohibition were present the seller would warn him, so we rely on that inference. Here two opposing inferences exist: if the cow had already calved the seller would praise it, while if it had not calved he should warn about the prohibition. Shmuel therefore leaves the matter doubtful. This is clear.
