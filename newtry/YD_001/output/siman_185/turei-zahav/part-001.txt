<b>ומאחר שעברו כו'. <i data-commentator="Nekudot HaKesef" data-label="♯" data-order="1"></i></b>קשה מאי קמ"ל דאח"כ דוקא נאמנת ובלא"ה אינה נאמנת פשיטא שאינה נאמנת כיון שא"א שטבלה בתוך אותו זמן ונראה שאפילו אם ספק לו אם עברו כ"כ ימים שאפשר שטבלה דהיינו שרגילה ללבוש לבנים ביום ו' לראייתה ועכשיו א"א בכך אלא שאם טבלה היתה צריכה ללבוש לבנים ביום ה' והיא אומרת שטבלה נאמנת כיון שעכ"פ אפשר בכך:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And after sufficient days had passed, etc. <i data-commentator="Nekudot HaKesef" data-label="♯" data-order="1"></i></b>This is difficult. What does this teach us by saying that specifically afterward she is believed, whereas otherwise she is not believed? It is obvious that she is not believed, since it was impossible for her to immerse within that period. It appears that even if he is uncertain whether enough days have passed for it to have been possible for her to immerse, she is believed. For example, she is accustomed to begin wearing white garments on the sixth day from the onset of her seeing, and under the present circumstances it would not have been possible for her to immerse unless she had begun wearing white garments on the fifth day. If she says that she immersed, she is believed, since it was at least possible for this to have occurred.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>She is regarded as certainly impure. </b>A subsequent explanation does not help, as stated in seif 3. The Tur asked: Is a woman who has established herself as a niddah in the presence of her neighbors treated more stringently than one who explicitly says, "I am impure," concerning whom an explanation does help? The Rashba answered that a woman may say, "I am impure," because of embarrassment or duress. However, to perform such a significant act as wearing the garments of a niddah is not something for which she is believed to offer a subsequent explanation. Therefore, an explanation does not help afterward in that case. See what I wrote concerning this in siman 1, seif 13; I also alluded to it in siman 127. I found it written in the name of Maharar Leib of Prague that a woman who established herself as a niddah in the presence of her neighbors is different, because not everyone knows of her explanation. If so, upon what did she rely when she established herself as impure before everyone? This is unlike a woman who says to her husband, "I am impure," for she may have relied upon subsequently telling him the explanation. This is a correct answer. I further learned from this that even when she merely made a statement, there is a distinction. If she said in the presence of many people that she was impure, a subsequent explanation does not help. Therefore, the poskim specifically wrote, "If she said to her husband, 'I am impure,'" for this law applies only when she said it to her husband alone. <br><b>An incident occurred </b>involving a woman who gave birth three times in the eighth month. She was concerned that the evil eye was causing this and wished to conceal her pregnancy from the members of her household and her neighbors. Her husband agreed that at the beginning of her pregnancy she would say, "I am impure," in order to conceal the pregnancy. The Rama was asked whether her husband was permitted to have relations with her in private. He ruled that it was permitted and discussed at length the matter of offering an explanation after she had established herself as a niddah in the presence of her neighbors. <i data-commentator="Nekudot HaKesef" data-label="♯" data-order="2"></i>In my humble opinion, this case does not fall at all within the category of one who established herself as a niddah, since she disclosed the matter before she established herself as impure. According to the Ramban, the principal basis for stringency when she established herself as a niddah is that she would not have performed an act merely because of the explanation, as the Beit Yosef wrote in the name of the Rashba. Here, however, she eliminated that proof in advance. This resembles one who gives advance notice invalidating a get or a gift. Although he later performs a significant act, nevertheless, since he initially disclosed that the subsequent act was not intended to have legal effect, we do not follow the later act. The same applies here. There is no distinction between her having disclosed it beforehand to her husband or having disclosed it before witnesses, for since her husband knows the truth, the Torah did not prohibit her to him at all. Even according to the reason I wrote in the name of Maharar Leib of Prague, that when the matter is known to the public it is worse, nevertheless, here it is permitted. That reasoning is effective only to establish her as impure with respect to her husband, such that he cannot rely upon a leniency. Here, however, her husband knew that she was truly pure before she established herself otherwise before the public, and it is certainly permitted. It also appears so from the final chapter of Kiddushin, where it is stated that we administer stoning and burning on the basis of established presumptions. There was an incident involving a woman who came to Yerushalayim with a child riding on her shoulder. She raised him, and when he grew older he had relations with her, and they were stoned. This was not because he was certainly her son, but because he was constantly attached to her. There too, if at the time she first came to Yerushalayim she had told two witnesses that he was not her son, but that she intended to raise him and establish him before the public as though he were her son, they certainly would not later have stoned her, for there would have been no established presumption at all, since she had contradicted it in advance. Even if she later explicitly said that he was her son, it would not matter, since she had already invalidated whatever she might say afterward. All this appears obvious. I am astonished that the Rama, in responsum 2, did not address this point, but merely compared the case to one who provides an explanation after the presumption was established. Although such an explanation does not help when she established herself as a niddah according to the Ramban, he nevertheless held that here it was a strong explanation and therefore helped; see there. In my view, however, there was no presumption at all, because the remedy preceded the injury: she disclosed the matter to her husband beforehand, at a time when she was believed concerning herself that she was pure. This appears obvious in my humble opinion. I further saw in that responsum that the Rama asked why the Sages said that when she established herself as a niddah her husband receives lashes, rather than saying that she receives lashes. He answered as he did there. It appears to me that the statement teaches a greater novelty concerning the husband. He might have had grounds for leniency because she did not tell him that she was a niddah, as women ordinarily tell their husbands, and he could have said, "I relied upon this and attributed her change of garments to some other matter." The teaching is that this is of no consequence, and he receives lashes. So it appears in my humble opinion.

<b>החכם נאמן. </b>נראה לי דוקא החכם עצמו כיון שהיא סומכת עליו ואשתכח שיקרא משא"כ אם אין החכם לפנינו ועד אחד מעיד שהחכם אסר לה אינו נאמן דהא אמרינן בפרק ב' דכתובות (דף כ"ב) כל מקום שהאמינה תורה עד אחד הרי כאן שנים כו' וא"כ ה"נ אין אותו העד נאמן להכחיש אותה שהיא כשנים ותו דגם לענין זנות אין עד א' נאמן לאסרה וה"ה כאן נראה לי. והך נאמנות דחכם כאן היינו דוקא לענין איסור אבל לענין ממון כגון להפסידה כתובתה אין החכם נאמן יותר משאר עד אחד כן מבואר מדברי הרא"ש פרק המדיר:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>The sage is believed. </b>It appears to me that this applies specifically to the sage himself, since she relies upon him and is thereby found to have spoken falsely. However, if the sage is not present and a single witness testifies that the sage ruled the matter forbidden for her, he is not believed. For we say in the second chapter of Kesubos, 22a, that wherever the Torah believed one witness, he is regarded as two witnesses, etc. Accordingly, here as well, that witness is not believed to contradict her, since she is regarded as two witnesses. Furthermore, even concerning adultery, one witness is not believed to forbid her to her husband, and the same applies here, in my opinion. The credibility granted to the sage here applies specifically regarding the prohibition. However, concerning monetary matters, such as causing her to forfeit her kesubah, the sage is no more credible than any other single witness. This is clear from the words of the Rosh in the chapter HaMadir.
