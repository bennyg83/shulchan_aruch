<b>(Taz s.k. 10) He elaborated.</b> He elaborated in challenging the Rama, and his language is exceedingly confused. In truth, there is no difficulty at all. One who studies the rulings of Mahara'i will see clearly that, at first, in the case of a woman who examines the folds and crevices, he permits her even though she does not know that her wound discharges blood. His reason is that, when she touches that particular place during the examination, she feels some pain, whereas in the other folds and crevices she feels no pain at all. This proves that the blood came from the wound. According to this reason, even a woman who has no fixed veset is pure. Therefore, in se'if 7, the Shulchan Aruch states without qualification that the blood may be attributed to the wound on that side, etc., and it does not say that this applies only to a woman who has a fixed veset when she sees outside the time of her veset.

Mahara'i then added another reason for permitting her. Even if one were to say that the pain is not proof that the blood came from the wound, since she has a veset and she sees outside the time of her veset, she may be permitted in accordance with the Mordechai. He explains that, although Rashi disagrees with the Mordechai, the Mordechai's view may nevertheless be joined to the other grounds for leniency. That is to say, when she has a wound, even if she does not know whether it discharges blood, she is pure. Accordingly, the words of the Rama are correct.

Even without this, there is no difficulty. Even were one to maintain that Mahara'i does not allow blood to be attributed to such a wound, that would be only with respect to establishing her as pure, for in that respect he did not wish to rely on the Mordechai alone. However, we do not establish her as one who sees blood because of intercourse when she sees outside the time of her veset and has a wound, even though she does not know whether the wound discharges blood. I distinguished between these matters in the Shach, s.k. 20 and 24; see there. This resolves everything that the Taz challenged against the Rama, including his challenge from siman 191. In any event, there is no difficulty from siman 191, in accordance with what I wrote in the Shach there, s.k. 7; see there and examine it carefully.

It further appears that there is no difficulty at all from siman 191. Certainly, where examination is impossible, Mahari Segal did not say that she is impure. He required an examination only where it is possible to clarify the matter. There, an examination is possible, as Mahari Weil wrote in a responsum, cited by the Taz himself in siman 191, letter 3, concerning the instruction that she insert clean absorbent material, etc. A woman has two openings. One, through which urine exits, is lower and close to the exterior. The other, through which menstrual blood exits, is higher and extends deeply into the body. It is therefore possible for her to insert clean absorbent material into the deeper opening, urinate, and then determine whether blood is found on the material. In that case, the wound is where she experiences pain during urination. Here, however, the wound is in the source. Whether the blood comes from the source or from the wound, blood will always be found on the material. Examination with a tube is likewise inapplicable here, since she has a wound in the source.

Mahari Segal's refusal to be lenient without examination was also for the reason he himself gave, namely, that she had no clearly established wound. Although Mahari Weil held that pain during urination is stronger evidence than the presence of a wound, Mahari Segal did not agree. This also removes the Taz's earlier challenge against the Rama from siman 191. There the Rama first cited the view that it appears permissible in every circumstance, even for a woman who has no fixed veset. That is the view of Mahari Weil, who held that pain during urination is stronger evidence than the presence of a wound. He then cited the view of Mahari Segal, who held that it is weaker than a wound; see there.

The Taz also wrote: “It is furthermore difficult, for in the Darchei Moshe, etc., and this is contrary to what is evident to anyone who studies the glosses to Shaarei Dura, etc.” This is incorrect in light of what I have written. Mahari Segal required examination only for the reason he stated, that in the present case she had no clearly established wound. When she does have a clearly established wound, even if she does not know whether it discharges blood, she may attribute the blood to it when she sees outside the time of her veset, as the Darchei Moshe wrote in his name. The Taz's assumption that the Mordechai disagrees with the Rashba is also incorrect, as I wrote in the Shach, s.k. 24.

<b>(Taz s.k. 15) He elaborated.</b> He elaborated in attempting to prove that there is a scribal error in the words of the Rama. He challenged the Levush, followed the approach of the Bach, and inferred from the Bach's words that when the occurrences were consecutive we do not attribute them to childbirth. His words are incorrect, and there is no doubt that he did not study the source of this law carefully. I will first prove from the Rama's own language that the interpretation is not as he maintained, and I will then prove it from the source of the law.

First, when the Rama writes at the beginning that this occurred three times, if he meant only nonconsecutive occurrences, he should not have left the matter unstated. He should have explained that it applies specifically when they were not consecutive. Second, if they were not consecutive, what significance is there to three occurrences? As soon as she did not see blood after the second occurrence, the third sighting would have no significance, just as with any other woman. Third, the phrase “and in the interim she did not see” implies that she did not see between the births. If the Rama meant the intervals between the sightings, he should have written, “and between the sightings she did not see.” Fourth, the Rama writes that we attribute the blood to childbirth just as we attribute blood to a wound, meaning that it is pure blood, as I will explain below. If so, what difference is there whether she saw three consecutive times or not? Fifth, if the Taz's interpretation were correct, the Rama should have explained that, nevertheless, intercourse is forbidden close to childbirth. Sixth, when the Rama writes, “All this applies if she already transgressed and had intercourse between the earlier births,” he should, according to the Taz, have written that she transgressed and had intercourse between the earlier sightings. Seventh, all the editions read, “she transgressed and had intercourse.” Eighth, according to the Taz, the fact that she transgressed and had intercourse is effective only after the fourth childbirth and close to it. If so, examination with a tube should likewise be effective only close to the fourth childbirth, and the Taz himself indeed wrote that tube examination is not effective in such a case. Yet whenever tube examination is required, if she transgressed and had intercourse without seeing blood, this has the same status as tube examination. Accordingly, when the Rama writes, “We do not attribute it to childbirth, but she requires examination with a tube,” he should have explained that tube examination is required specifically after childbirth. Ninth, how can she ever become permitted through tube examination? Once she saw blood three times, intercourse is forbidden. She therefore cannot give birth again so that we might say that she should examine herself with a tube after childbirth. Rather, she would necessarily have to be divorced. In what circumstance, then, could the Rama say that she requires tube examination?

It is therefore clear that the Rama's words must be understood as I wrote in the Shach, s.k. 31. The first clause also concerns consecutive occurrences, as the Levush likewise implies. The Rama did not state this explicitly in the first clause because he spoke without qualification, thereby including every circumstance. In the latter clause he had to specify that the occurrences were consecutive, because otherwise she would be permitted. The reason he did not permit her in the first clause merely because she had transgressed and had intercourse, which is comparable to tube examination, is as I wrote in the Shach, that she subsequently returned and saw blood after three consecutive acts of intercourse. Alternatively, and this is the principal explanation, tube examination alone would certainly not have permitted her unless we also say that childbirth is comparable to a wound. The examination was not conducted close to childbirth, and the case is comparable to a woman who sees blood after every immersion, for whom the fact that she transgressed and had intercourse between one immersion and the next is ineffective, as I wrote in the Shach, s.k. 29. Nevertheless, tube examination, or the fact that she transgressed and had intercourse without seeing blood, is effective here because we say that since she does not now see blood, what she saw originally came from the childbirth wound.

Accordingly, when the Rama writes at the beginning, “If it occurred that she saw three times during the first intercourse after childbirth, etc.,” it is unnecessary to explain, as I wrote in the Shach, that she later returned and saw three consecutive times. Were that the meaning, the Rama should have written “during the first acts of intercourse,” in the plural. Rather, the statement is to be understood simply. Even so, we require the reason that the blood is attributed to childbirth, which is like a wound. The fact that she transgressed and had intercourse would not by itself permit her, for perhaps she established a veset connected with childbirth, as I explained. Nevertheless, when she transgressed and had intercourse even long after childbirth and did not then see blood, we attribute her earlier blood to the childbirth wound. All the more so, when she does not see blood during tube examination, even long after childbirth, we attribute the earlier blood to the childbirth wound. The Rama therefore wrote without qualification that she requires examination with a tube. This is unlike a woman who sees blood after every immersion. Although she had intercourse between immersions without seeing blood, she remains forbidden, because there is no wound there to which the blood can be attributed.

<b>And now.</b> We shall prove this from the glosses to Shaarei Dura, which are the source of this law, as I have explained. Since the Taz built the foundation of his argument on the claim that the glosses to Shaarei Dura support him, I will set out their language so that the truth of the matter will be clear.

This is the text of a responsum of Maharam Yochanan to Mahari Segal concerning a woman who saw blood because of intercourse: “Regarding the woman who, shortly after childbirth, saw blood because of intercourse when she had intercourse, and afterward no longer saw blood because of intercourse; she immersed, then again saw blood during intercourse, and the same occurred a third time. Three weeks after the third immersion she menstruated naturally, as women do, immersed, and afterward no longer saw blood because of intercourse. The same sequence occurred after three births without any change. The question was not brought until after the third birth.” This concludes the question.

At first glance, the phrase “after the third birth, in this manner without any change” could imply that after the third birth as well, following the three sightings caused by intercourse, she menstruated naturally, immersed, and did not subsequently see blood because of intercourse. However, the words of Mahari HaLevi imply that this was not so, for he states that after the third birth she had a new sighting because of intercourse that had not yet been uprooted. Even if the facts were as first understood, I am astonished at Mahari HaLevi's strong objection. Although I know that, because of his great humility and piety, he sent the matter to me, it is evident that he did not see the words of Rabbenu Chananel, who was far more lenient.

Rabbenu Chananel wrote: “It appears from the Tosefta that the Sages required examination with a tube only for a woman whose husband was adversely affected three times immediately after the marriage, and who had no permitted acts of intercourse. The baraisa states, ‘He married her, and she saw blood because of intercourse a first, second, and third time.’ This implies that if her experience included both acts of intercourse that resulted in prohibition and acts that did not, she does not become forbidden to her husband even after three acts that resulted in prohibition.” This view and these words of Rabbenu Chananel were cited in a responsum signed by Azriel son of Rabbi Yechiel. Although that responsum states, “Nevertheless, we do not rely upon it,” consecutive occurrences are at least required. For a woman who married three husbands and saw blood only once with each does not become forbidden until she is established through three distinct male forces. The same applies with her first husband when permitted acts of intercourse intervened, for even with the same husband not all acts are assumed equal until she is established through three acts that caused prohibition.

The responsum continues at length. We see from Rabbenu Chananel that once permitted acts were observed with this husband, even if she later became established through three acts of intercourse that caused prohibition, he permits her without examination because this husband's acts are not all equal. Although the later authority wrote that we do not rely on Rabbenu Chananel, that was because the prohibited acts increased and were the final ones. One could therefore say that after the prohibited acts increased and became established, we may no longer permit her. In the present case, however, after this man's acts increased and became established as prohibited after the first birth, they again became permitted as they had been before she conceived. The same happened after the second birth. No one would say that we should not attribute the sightings during intercourse to the varying physical effects. There is therefore a basis for permitting her, and we should not assume that the later authority disagreed so completely with Rabbenu Chananel without any additional distinction.

Rather, since this man had previously been established through permitted acts, when blood later appeared we may say that it came from the sides and not from the source. All the more so here, where the blood may be attributed to the wounds of childbirth. The sides were weakened, struck, and made sore, and it may be that intercourse caused blood to emerge until she regained strength after childbirth. This is not impure blood. We see that the Gemara attributes blood to a wound leniently. The Rashba wrote that a woman is believed concerning a wound when she says that blood issues from it. This does not mean that she must state with certainty that this particular blood came from the wound. Rather, she is believed when she says that she has a wound, and we then say that the blood flowed from it. The Tosefta likewise states only that she is believed to say that she has a wound. Although the Mordechai is stringent and requires her to know that the blood came from the wound, I wrote in the Shach, s.k. 24, that the Mordechai and Rashba do not disagree. The Rashba means that she need not know that the blood presently before us came from the wound; the Mordechai requires that she know that the wound is of a type that discharges blood.

In an ordinary case, most wounds do not discharge blood. Here, however, permitted acts had already been established, and the blood during intercourse appeared only after childbirth. We know that childbirth wounds ordinarily discharge much blood until the wound heals sufficiently that contact no longer causes bleeding. There is therefore strong reason to attribute the blood to childbirth. Concerning the hymenal blood of a young bride, Beis Hillel permits intercourse until the wound heals, and distinguishes between blood found while standing and blood found while sitting. If blood found after intercourse while the wound has not healed may be attributed to the wound, all the more so here according to the Rashba. At childbirth the source opens and much blood emerges. Even if visible bleeding has ceased, contact during intercourse may cause it to recur. The childbirth wound is greater than the hymenal wound of a young bride, regarding which four nights are permitted, and for a minor the permission extends for as long as twelve months until the wound heals.

The distinctions between standing and sitting, or between a hard and soft place, apply where blood emerged without intercourse. If the blood does not emerge on its own while she stands or sits on a hard place, I concede that intercourse may nevertheless cause bleeding. This is so only near childbirth, before the wound has healed. Mahari HaLevi presumably did not see the explicit words of Rabbenu Chananel, for otherwise he would not have objected without offering a reason, although his general practice is to rule stringently.

I have written briefly. I found still stronger support in the explicit words of Rabbenu Chananel. Although Rabbi Azriel wrote that we do not follow his view in practice, we should not strain to be so stringent where so many grounds for attribution are present, as explained above. We should not presume that Rabbenu Chananel erred. Know that were it not for Rabbenu Chananel's view, I would not have left the matter without submitting the question to our teachers. One should follow the majority of the authorities before us. Peace, and the peace of Torah, to you. Thus far the responsum of Maharam Yochanan.

The responsum was copied in the glosses to Shaarei Dura, siman 16. It clearly concerns consecutive occurrences. First, the question states that shortly after childbirth she saw blood because of intercourse, immersed, and saw again. If she had not seen blood because of intercourse, why would she have immersed again? Second, the phrase “afterward she no longer saw blood because of intercourse” implies that this occurred only after three consecutive sightings. Third, the question was not brought until after the third birth. Had she sought a ruling after the first birth, she could not have been permitted without permitted acts of intercourse intervening. This too shows that the sightings were consecutive. Fourth, the responsum speaks of “after three sightings,” which implies consecutive sightings. Fifth, the statement that “we do not rely upon Rabbenu Chananel” is explained by the fact that the prohibited acts were the final ones, not by the fact that they were consecutive. Sixth, the responsum says that after the acts increased, the husband was established in prohibition after the first birth. This cannot refer to a single sighting after one birth. It necessarily means that she saw blood after three consecutive acts following one birth. Seventh, it states that the blood may be attributed to childbirth wounds even when she sees after the third birth. Eighth, it says that even if she sees many times because of intercourse, the blood may be attributed to the wound until it heals. Ninth, it states that this is not impure blood, and that the Gemara attributes blood to a wound leniently.

The rule that emerges is that the Rama's first clause likewise concerns three consecutive sightings after each birth. Even so, she is permitted, because afterward she became established through permitted acts of intercourse, and we attribute the earlier blood, for purposes of the laws of niddah, to the childbirth wound. We do not permit her merely because she had intercourse without seeing, for perhaps she established a veset associated with childbirth. Nevertheless, once she afterward had intercourse without seeing, or underwent tube examination without seeing even at a time distant from childbirth, we say that the earlier blood came from the childbirth wound. There is therefore no basis for distinguishing between consecutive and nonconsecutive sightings. Once she was permitted after three births because permitted acts intervened, we do not say that she established a veset for childbirth. The question of what the law would be if she then saw two additional times still requires analysis.

<b>(Taz s.k. 16) He follows.</b> He follows his preceding position, but it has already been explained that his words are untenable.

<b>Gloss.</b> In the Beis Yosef, beginning “And what he said, that this measure, etc.”: Since, when blood is found, we do not render them definitely impure, it appears that the reason is that we may say that the blood came only after intercourse, etc. This is difficult, for even when the blood is found within the time required for her to descend from the bed, we do not render her definitely impure. It is only a doubt, except when it is found immediately, as is evident from the Mishnah and Gemara. In truth, however, their view is that since one is liable to bring an asham talui, this is called seeing blood because of intercourse, as is explicit in the Semag, Raavad, and other poskim.

<b>In the Beis Yosef.</b> “Know that the Semag wrote in the following language, etc.,” until the end of the paragraph. It appears to me that, because in the chapter HaBa Al Yevimto the Gemara rules with respect to marriage and lashes in accordance with Rabbi, that two occurrences establish a chazakah, whereas with respect to vesetos and an ox becoming forewarned it rules in accordance with Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel, that three occurrences are required, the Semag therefore ruled with respect to marriage that she is permitted only to a second husband. Once she saw blood because of intercourse with the second husband, she is established with respect to marriage. However, with respect to vesetos, meaning with the first husband or the second husband himself, she is not established until three occurrences. This is comparable to a woman presumed to cause the death of her husbands, regarding whom the poskim ruled that she is forbidden to marry a third husband, yet she is entitled to a kesubah from the second, as explained in Even HaEzer, siman 9. Evidently, with respect to monetary matters we do not establish a chazakah until three occurrences, like an ox becoming forewarned, whereas with respect to marriage we establish it after two. This is what Tosafos and the Rosh wrote in the chapter HaBa Al Yevimto, and the Rosh wrote the same at the beginning of the chapter Na'arah SheNispatsah; see there. The other poskim, however, treated these marriages like vesetos, since the matter arises because of a veset. This concludes the gloss.
