**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And nowadays.</b> Rosh and Tosafot there explain that a true expert must be both a great scholar and especially proficient in examining blemishes. This is why the Gemara distinguishes between a chacham for vows and a mumcheh for a firstborn. Beit Yosef gives an additional reason: there is no nasi in Eretz Yisrael nowadays from whom authorization can be obtained, based on Rambam's statement at the beginning of chapter 3 that a firstborn is examined by an expert authorized by the nasi of Eretz Yisrael. It appears that Beit Yosef inferred this from the term mumcheh, as in the beginning of Sanhedrin concerning fines that require ordained judges. But there is no proof, because Sanhedrin 5a also calls one who judges alone a mumcheh even without formal authorization. Another possible inference is from Bechorot concerning permitting a firstborn outside Eretz Yisrael. Rambam writes similarly. Rashi, however, explains there that the concern is that the animal is not fit for sacrifice, not lack of authorization. Nor is there proof from Sanhedrin, where the phrase "he may permit firstborn animals" appears alongside "he may issue rulings," since that passage concerns a student who requires authorization because of a particular incident, as discussed above in siman 242. There is, however, a Yerushalmi in which Rav asked Rabban Gamliel son of Rabbi, after his father's death, for permission to permit firstborn animals. Ordinarily, after one's teacher dies one no longer requires permission. Yoma 78a likewise recounts that they asked an elder about such authority. Tosafot in Bechorot nevertheless write that formal authorization is unnecessary. We also find many Amoraim in Babylonia who permitted firstborn animals individually. Perhaps they had received authorization. Tosafot's words can also be understood as saying only that semikhah is unnecessary, while authorization out of respect for the nasi remains required. Nowadays, when there is no nasi, perhaps even that is unnecessary. This is suggested by Tosafot's wording that in our time there is no sufficiently qualified expert, implying that authorization itself is not the obstacle. Rosh likewise writes at the end of Bechorot that nowadays there is no chacham and mumcheh because people are not sufficiently expert in blemishes. The two reasons reported in Derishah as though both were from Rosh are therefore mistaken and stem from Beit Yosef's discussion.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And a notch.</b> Rosh writes in the name of Ri that a notched ear is an obvious blemish. He infers this from the Gemara's frequent use of the case of one who notches a firstborn's ear, including Bava Kamma 98a and Bechorot 34b. Rambam, however, lists only the three blemishes mentioned earlier, and the Mishnah likewise lists only those three. Why then does it not include a notched ear? The Gemara's use of a notched ear may simply be because it is the first blemish listed in the Mishnah. According to Ri, who permits relying on a notched ear, the same applies to any blemish clearly recognizable without specialized expertise, even if an ordinary person does not know that halachah regards it as a blemish. The need for an expert is due to practical proficiency, not abstract scholarship. Rosh therefore lists in chapter 6 several such blemishes, and Tur records them here. He explains that non-obvious blemishes include cataracts of the eye and similar defects. Rambam disagrees and follows his own position that the problem is authorization, so only a blemish universally known may be acted upon without an expert. Rosh follows his own position. The Shulchan Aruch is therefore difficult, because it combines Rambam's approach with Ri's and assumes that a notched ear is considered universally obvious even according to Rambam. That is not so. Are the other blemishes listed by Rosh any less obvious than a notched ear? Rosh mentioned a notched ear only to teach the novelty that it too counts as an obvious blemish, whereas Shulchan Aruch omitted the other blemishes Rosh and Tur listed and retained only the notched ear.
