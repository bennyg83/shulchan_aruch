**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And he suffered while wearing it, etc.</b> But if he did not suffer while wearing it, there is no proof of his intent, and it is forbidden even for loading and covering. The responsa of Mabit, part 1, siman 117, page 23, column 4, discusses one who entered a ship with his wife for a sea outing. A storm arose, and while calming her so that she would not fear, he swore that he would never again enter a ship at sea with his wife. Later he needed to travel by ship in order to settle in another city. It appears that his intent concerned only an outing. See there, where he discusses this at length. Mabit further writes in part 2, siman 61, page 32, column 2, regarding two people who lived in one house, ate at one table, conducted business in partnership, and worked in one shop. A quarrel arose concerning payment of the rent for the house and shop, and one of them suddenly swore that they would not sit together in one house and would not eat or drink together. His oath is interpreted according to reason. He is forbidden only to live together in one house as before and to eat and drink in the house from one common fund. Once they have separated from one another and from the house, and each maintains his own household expenses, he may eat in the other person's home. Certainly he may eat with him elsewhere, including at a wedding celebration or a bris. The proof is from the cases of one who was carrying wool and one who was wearing clothing, for we follow the person's intent and the circumstances in which the vow was made.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>All this applies specifically, etc., unless there is a clearly demonstrated assessment, etc.</b> Mabit writes in part 4, siman 57, page 46, column 4, that the distinction applies in a matter involving a monetary claim. Where no monetary claim is involved, however, even if he was adjured without qualification, we follow the evident assessment of intent. This conclusion is not compelling. The unqualified wording of the Rama also implies that there is no distinction.

<b>All the more so, a community, etc.</b> This is the Rama's view, derived in Darkei Moshe from the Rashbatz, unlike Terumas HaDeshen, siman 281. In my humble opinion, the Rashbatz does not contradict Terumas HaDeshen. Terumas HaDeshen concludes that if the community enacted the matter under cherem, it is certainly a Torah prohibition like an oath and is forbidden. His uncertainty concerns only an ordinary communal enactment without cherem, whether it is treated as Torah law or rabbinic law. See there, where this is explicit, although Beit Yosef cites it briefly. The Rashbatz explicitly discusses an oath, which is a Torah prohibition. Maharshal likewise distinguishes this explicitly in the first chapter of Chullin, siman 36. One may further distinguish that the Rashbatz speaks specifically of an individual who swore, where vows follow ordinary human usage, and according to ordinary usage the item is forbidden. A communal enactment is different. Maharshal makes this distinction explicitly. He cites the unresolved question of Rabbi Yirmiyah concerning meat from animals slaughtered by stabbing that the Jewish people brought with them into Eretz Yisrael. The Rosh writes that this affects one who forbade a type of food upon himself beginning at a certain time, but possessed some of that food from before the time arrived, and also a case where beit din sought to prohibit a food, such as gentile cheese or cooked foods, and a Jew already possessed some of it. Maharshal explains that a full Torah vow or oath requires stringency because the question remains unresolved, whereas an ordinary rabbinic form of vow permits what was already in his possession because a rabbinic doubt is treated leniently. He nevertheless rejects applying ordinary human usage in vows to other prohibitions, and concludes that the latter application, to an enactment of beit din, is primary. If beit din merely sought to prohibit the item, the unresolved rabbinic doubt is treated leniently. If beit din imposed the prohibition under cherem and curse, it is treated as Torah law, for beit din has authority, when empowered by the government, to impose penalties beyond the strict law in order to strengthen observance. I have cited his words at length because they contain several legal distinctions. His objection to the Rosh is not valid, because the Rosh addresses a vow limited by time, not the general rule that vows follow ordinary human usage. That rule is obvious. The Rosh addresses a place or time where ordinary usage is not otherwise clear, since usage varies by place and time, as explained in the previous siman. In any event, Maharshal's words establish distinctions between an enactment under cherem and an ordinary enactment, and between an individual and a community, unlike the Rama.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>That he would not reside, etc.</b> See above, siman 217, seif 32, in the gloss. Mabit writes in part 1, siman 188, page 78, column 3, concerning one who swore that he would not eat outside his home. We do not follow his claim that he intended only that country and not another country. Following the intent of the person making the vow applies where the expression can bear two meanings, as in the case of cherem discussed above in siman 208. But one who vows concerning a particular matter is not believed to say that he intended to forbid it only in this city and not elsewhere. An unqualified personal vow applies everywhere, and he should have specified otherwise. See there for additional laws. However, when the circumstance that prompted the vow is evident, such as where wicked people lived in that city and he vowed not to eat in their homes, or a similar case, we follow that circumstance.
