<b>ונצטער בלבישתו. </b>זו היא דעת הרמב"ם דגם באיסור לבישה בעינן הוכחה דנתכוין ללבישה ולמד כן מברייתא דתנן היה לבוש והיצר לו וכתב ב"י שהרא"ש חולק וס"ל דבזה לא בעינן הוכחה דה"ה בנודר סתם שאין במשמע אלא ללבוש וברייתא דקתני היה לבוש צמר והיצר לו לאו דוקא וכן הוא דעת הטור שכתב היה לבוש בהן ונדר כו' ולא כתב והיצר לו כמו דכתב ברישא אלא שצריך לתרץ למה יכתוב הטור היה לבוש בהן דגם הלבישה לא היה לו להזכיר כיון שגם סתם האומר כן דינא הכי לפי דעת הרא"ש ומו"ח ז"ל האריך לתרץ קושיא זו ואמר שלבישה בעינן עכ"פ. ולע"ד נראה דודאי לא בעינן מידי אלא דהטור כתב כן לרבותא דאע"פ שהיה לבוש בהן ולא היצר לו הסברא נותנת שלא נתכוין ללבישה שתהיה אסורה עליו כיון שאנו רואין שהוא לבוש בהן בשעת נדרו ואין לו שום צער מהם אפ"ה אמרינן נתכוין לאיסור לבישה וכ"ש בלא לבש בהן כלל כנ"ל פשוט:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And he was distressed by wearing it. </b>This is the Rambam’s opinion, that even regarding a prohibition against wearing, proof is required that he intended wearing. He derived this from the Baraisa that teaches that he was wearing it and it caused him distress. The Beit Yosef wrote that the Rosh disagrees and holds that no such proof is required, for the same law applies when one vows without qualification, since the statement implies only wearing. The Baraisa’s statement that he was wearing wool and it caused him distress is not precise. This is also the Tur’s opinion, for he wrote that he was wearing them and made a vow, but did not write that they caused him distress, as he wrote in the first clause. One must still explain why the Tur wrote that he was wearing them, for according to the Rosh he should not have needed to mention wearing either, since the same law applies even when one speaks without qualification. My revered father-in-law, of blessed memory, discussed this question at length and answered that wearing is at least required. In my humble opinion, nothing is required. Rather, the Tur wrote this as a greater novelty. Although he was wearing them and they caused him no distress, reason would suggest that he did not intend to forbid wearing them, since we see that he was wearing them at the time of the vow and suffered no distress from them. Nevertheless, we say that he intended to forbid wearing them. All the more so where he was not wearing them at all. This appears straightforward.

<b>באומדנא דמוכח. </b>דברי הג"ה הזאת דברי ריב"ש סימן קצ"ה וצריך שתדע דהתם קאי לענין שאחד היה מבקש מהקהל שיקבלו לסופר זקן אחד ועמו נער אחד וקבלו הקהל את שניהם בנדר בסתם ואח"כ סילקו הזקן שלא עשה כהוגן ורצו איזה יחידים לומר שגם הנער נסתלק כיון שעל ידי בקשת המבקש קבלום ובקשתו היתה שיהיה עם הזקן וכיון שנסתלק הזקן ממילא נסתלק הנער לזה השיב הריב"ש שם כיון דבלשון קבלת הקהל משמע שלא תלאו זה בזה אין אנו משגיחים בבקשת המבקש ולא אמרינן דניזול בתר אומדנא דהיינו שודאי נתכוונו כפי שביקש המבקש כמו דאזלינן הכא בתר אומדנא לענין קונם צמר דהיינו דוקא בנודר בינו לבין עצמו הולכים אחר כוונתו משום דבעינן שיהיו פיו ולבו שוין אבל בנותן מתנה לחבירו כל שלשון המתנה כולל הולכים אחריו ואין הולכים בזה אחר אומדנא דמוכח אא"כ באומדנא כההיא דמי שהלך בנו למדינת הים והוא בחושן המשפט סי' רמ"ו סעיף א' עד כאן מבואר כוונתו דאם הלשון מורה על ענין אחר אף על פי שמצד כוונת הלב יש להבין שהיה איזה דבר בדעתו שעל ידו יתבטל משמעות הלשון הא לא אמרינן כיון שהוא לטובת חבירו זכה חבירו מצד לשונו של הנותן דבזה לא בעינן פיו ולבו שוין אא"כ הוה אומדנא דמוכח מה שאין כן בנודר בינו לבין עצמו אנן מבטלים הלשון לפי משמעותו ומפרשינן ליה על דבר אחר כההיא דרישא שלפי המשמעות משמע איסור לבישה לפי דעת הרא"ש ולפי דעת הרמב"ם שתיהם במשמע כמ"ש בסעיף א' מ"מ מתוך כוונתו אנו מפרשים דבריו שעל הפשלה לאחוריו קאמר שיהיה אסור ולא בלבישה בזה דוקא הם חולקים אבל באמת אם נתן מתנה לחבירו ואמר לשון שיש לו שני משמעות ודאי הברירה ביד הנותן לפרשו בענין שטוב לו והמוציא מחבירו עליו הראייה ובדרך זה תדע להבין הג"ה זאת שהיא סתומה:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Through a clearly proven assessment. </b>The words of this gloss are from the Rivash, siman 195. You should know that there the case concerned a person who asked the community to appoint an elderly scribe together with a young man. The community accepted both of them through an unspecified vow. They later removed the elderly man because he acted improperly, and certain individuals wished to argue that the young man was also removed. Since they had accepted both through the requester’s proposal, and his proposal was that the young man serve with the elder, the removal of the elder should automatically remove the young man as well. The Rivash answered that since the wording of the community’s acceptance indicates that they did not make one dependent upon the other, we disregard the requester’s proposal. We do not say that we should follow an assessment that they certainly intended to accept them according to his proposal, as we do here regarding one who says, “Wool is a konam.” Only one who vows privately is judged according to his intention, because his mouth and heart must be in agreement. When one gives a gift to another, however, whenever the wording of the gift includes the recipient, we follow that wording and do not follow even a compelling assessment, unless it is an assessment like the case of one whose son traveled overseas, discussed in Choshen Mishpat, siman 246, seif 1. His intention is therefore clear. If the wording indicates one matter, even though one may infer from his inner intention that he had something else in mind that would negate the plain meaning of his words, we do not say so. Since it is for the benefit of the other person, the recipient acquires through the giver’s wording, and in such a case the giver’s mouth and heart need not be in agreement unless there is a clearly proven assessment. This differs from one who vows privately, where we set aside the plain implication of his wording and interpret it in accordance with another meaning. In the first case, according to the Rosh the words imply a prohibition against wearing, while according to the Rambam both meanings are included, as written in seif 1. Nevertheless, from his intention we interpret his words to mean that he forbade placing it over his shoulder, and not wearing it. This is the particular point upon which they disagree. If, however, one gave a gift to another and used wording with two possible meanings, the giver certainly has the right to interpret it in the manner more favorable to himself, and the burden of proof rests upon the one seeking to extract property from another. In this manner you can understand this otherwise obscure gloss.

<b>אזלינן בתר כוונתו. </b>דברים אלו הם דברי הרשב"ץ הביאן בית יוסף ואע"פ שדין זה נפתח בגדולים ונסתיים בגדולים מ"מ תורה היא וללמוד אני צריך כי לא די שקשה עליו מדברי התלמוד אלא שקשה עליו דברי עצמו כמו נמי ב' מינים שהם מניעת הריוח והצלה מהפסד כמו שמצינו חילוק זה לענין דבר האבוד בח"ה ובאבילות סי' ש"פ סעיף ו' שאין דומה זה לזה והכא נמי דכוותיה:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>We follow his intention. </b>These are the words of the Rashbatz, cited by the Beit Yosef. Although this ruling began with great authorities and ended with great authorities, it is nevertheless Torah and I must study it, for it is difficult not only from the words of the Gemara but also from his own words, as we will explain. His statement that we follow the person’s intention is astonishing. <i data-commentator="Nekudot HaKesef" data-label="♯" data-order="1"></i>We rule like Shmuel that one’s mouth and heart must be in agreement, as written above in siman 210. What difference does his intention make when he explicitly said that he would not make wine, which implies that wine already made is not included? It is stated explicitly regarding, “You shall not make for yourself a carved image”: I know only that one may not make it; from where do we know that one may not retain one already made? The Torah therefore states, “You shall not have.” Thus, something already made is not included in the words, “you shall not make.” The Terumas HaDeshen, siman 277, wrote that we judge according to his intention even where it conflicts with the words uttered by his lips, etc. It is obvious that he meant only that we should not interpret the wording according to its simplest implication, as written above regarding the prohibition against wearing wool according to the Rosh. Nevertheless, the language can still be interpreted consistently with his intention. This is not so where the intention completely contradicts the words, as here. The Rashbatz’s proof from the case in seif 1, where one vows, “Wool shall not come upon me,” and we follow his intention, is not comparable. There the intention does not contradict the wording at all, whereas here it does. Moreover, he contradicts his own words. At the end of siman 208, the Beit Yosef cites the Rashbatz concerning one who said that he would not marry a woman within three years. If he used wording that means he would not perform the marriage ceremony with her, and then transgressed and married her, he is like one who swore regarding a loaf and then ate it, and he may seek release from the oath. If, however, he used wording meaning that she should not be his wife within three years, release is unavailable, etc. The Shulchan Aruch ruled accordingly. Thus, we follow the meaning of the words and do not say that when he stated that he would not perform the marriage ceremony, he meant that she should not be his wife. The same applies here. When he says that he will not make wine, he does not mean that he should have no wine. These are precisely the same matter. It is even a kal vachomer. There, it is likely that he was not particular about the marriage ceremony itself, for people do not ordinarily care about the exact time of the ceremony. Rather, he did not want the burden of marriage hanging around his neck and did not wish to be occupied with his wife’s needs. He included this within the act of marriage because marriage is the beginning, and he referred to the beginning. Nevertheless, we say that we follow the plain meaning of his wording. All the more so here, where he did not intend to cause himself a loss because he feared danger by forbidding wine that he already possessed. Rather, he intended not to involve himself in producing new wine, which means foregoing future profit. He therefore said specifically that he would not make wine. Certainly we should follow the wording and forbid only what actually emerged from his lips. Furthermore, if two types of merchandise were dangerous to sell to a non-Jew and he swore concerning only one of them, would we say that we follow his intention because he feared the danger and therefore forbid the second type as well? Certainly not. The same applies here, for avoiding future profit and saving oneself from an existing loss are two different categories, just as we find this distinction regarding a financial loss on Chol HaMoed and in the laws of mourning, siman 380, seif 6. One is not comparable to the other, and the same applies here.

<b>And all the more so a community, etc. </b>The Rama derived this from the ruling of the Rashbatz cited by the Shulchan Aruch. I say that even if that ruling has no basis, as we argued, this law is nevertheless correct. It is explicit in the words of the Rosh in the first chapter of Chullin regarding the Gemara’s unresolved question whether the limbs of animals slaughtered by stabbing, which the Jewish people brought with them into Eretz Yisrael, were permitted. The Rosh explains that the practical difference concerns one who forbids upon himself a certain species from a specified time onward, and when that time arrives he still possesses some of that species that he had been eating until then. The question is whether the remainder is permitted. Alternatively, it concerns a beit din that wished to forbid a certain item, whether what Jews already possessed from that species is included. Since the matter remained unresolved, we rule stringently in a Torah matter. Nevertheless, one might say that on the contrary, this proves leniency here. Since the question remained unresolved, every unresolved rabbinic matter is treated leniently, and here the prohibition is rabbinic, for it arises only from a communal enactment. I likewise saw in Terumas HaDeshen, siman 281, that he cited one of the great authorities who ruled concerning a communal enactment not to sell meat above the fixed price. One person possessed meat that he had slaughtered before the enactment in order to sell it at a higher price. That authority permitted it because the meat was already in his possession before the enactment, and he brought proof from this unresolved question. The author of Terumas HaDeshen wrote that this applied because it was merely an enactment and was not made under a formal curse. If, however, they imposed a cherem, it becomes a Torah matter and we rule stringently. He then wrote: “Although I have some hesitation concerning that ruling, for the Semag writes that one who swears not to enter into the communal enactment has sworn falsely, etc., which indicates that a communal enactment itself has Torah force,” and he concludes that where vows are only rabbinic, such as a vow concerning something intangible and similar cases, the unresolved question is rabbinic and we rule leniently. According to this, there is no basis for the Rama’s phrase, “all the more so,” regarding a communal enactment. The Shulchan Aruch first discussed an oath, which is a Torah matter, and how can the Rama derive from this a communal enactment that is not from the Torah? Although the Terumas HaDeshen wrote that he had some hesitation regarding an enactment, it is clear there that he did not intend to disagree and rule stringently so as to overturn the practical ruling of that great authority. In truth, I already wrote in siman 228, seif 33, a sound resolution to the Terumas HaDeshen’s hesitation. Only an enactment intended to safeguard against sin has Torah force. An enactment intended to preserve money or for other reasons unrelated to sin does not. I later saw that the Maharshal, in the first chapter of Chullin, siman 36, cited the Rosh’s explanation of the unresolved question. He wrote that the Rosh’s first answer, concerning one who forbids something upon himself, would mean that it is a rabbinic doubt, for whenever one forbids an item upon himself in the language of a vow by saying, “I will not eat such-and-such,” without associating it with an already prohibited matter, the prohibition is only rabbinic. The Maharshal disagrees with the Rosh on this point, for with vows we follow ordinary speech, and according to ordinary speech it is forbidden to eat that species once the specified time arrives. He therefore accepts the Rosh’s second answer, concerning a beit din that wished to forbid something, where the doubt is rabbinic and we rule leniently. This applies specifically where the beit din merely wished to prohibit it. If, however, they decreed under cherem and curse not to eat that item, it is a Torah matter, for a beit din has the authority to impose cherem and excommunication even beyond the strict law when their intention is to safeguard against sin. The doubt is then a Torah doubt and we rule stringently. Accordingly, when the sages of Russia once decreed at a large public gathering that no one should drink ordinary Jewish wine unless reliable witnesses testified to its kashrus, and they set a time for the decree, even wine already remaining in their possession became forbidden, for it was a Torah doubt, end quote. Thus, regarding an enactment made without cherem, his words clearly require leniency in a case of doubt. Since the case cited by the Rama is a rabbinic doubt, we rule leniently, and there is no prohibition unless the communal enactment was made under cherem. Even then there is a practical difference if the remaining item became mixed into another mixture in a manner involving doubt, for it would constitute a double doubt. The underlying prohibition itself is uncertain, since the Gemara left it unresolved. The law concerning when a doubtful prohibition that becomes mixed creates a double doubt was explained above in siman 110, at the end of seif 9, where we required two mixtures. If, however, the source were the Rashbatz’s ruling and the item were certainly forbidden, it would not be considered a double doubt until there were three mixtures, as written there in siman 110. Truth directs its own path, and this is only a doubtful prohibition, as the Rosh stated explicitly. This appears entirely clear to me.
