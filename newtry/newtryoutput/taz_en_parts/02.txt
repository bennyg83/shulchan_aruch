“Whether he fell ill that day” — and it is not forbidden as muktzeh, etc. Beit Yosef in the name of the Rosh: we rule like Rabbi Shimon, who said muktzeh applies only to a lamp that was kindled on Shabbat [I have a text question: it should read “that burns on Shabbat,” as in siman 279]; and to dried figs and raisins that one actively rejected with one’s hands and mentally abandoned, etc. — end quote; afterward he wrote in the name of the Rambam and Rav Hamaggid — unlike the Ra’avad and Ran, who did not permit one who fell ill on Shabbat because of muktzeh even for Rabbi Shimon — and it is difficult from what Beit Yosef wrote in siman 310 that Rabbi Shimon admits regarding something not fit for permitted use on Shabbat, etc., for here there was certainly a prohibition on the animal as long as it was not slaughtered, and even so we permit for Rabbi Shimon as muktzeh here; therefore the reason is: since later that same Shabbat it was permitted and became fit — so Beit Yosef there — only in such a case does Rabbi Shimon permit, namely when the prohibition departed — and this is very difficult: for in that case we do not rule like Rabbi Shimon — we forbid a lamp kindled in it that Shabbat even after it went out: for Rabbi Shimon it is permitted and we forbid — siman 279; so why did he rule here to permit an animal slaughtered on Shabbat for one who fell ill on Shabbat? One could answer that Beit Yosef in siman 310 follows the Ra’avad and Ran, but here Beit Yosef brings that he holds one who slaughters for a sick person is permitted to a healthy person for one who fell ill on erev Shabbat, but on Shabbat it is muktzeh — even for Rabbi Shimon this is not [the same case], for it is clear that according to the halacha Beit Yosef in siman 310 comes to resolve that halacha not contradict halacha, and Beit Yosef wondered at the Ran: how did he not notice that we rule like Rabbi Shimon that there is no muktzeh — and according to his words one should wonder the reverse on those who permit. It appears to me: the reason we do not rule like Rabbi Shimon for a leniency that comes when the prohibition is removed is that we say: since it was set aside for a prohibited use, it is set aside the whole day — and this is for a thing that on Shabbat became permitted through a change in the prohibition as it was, e.g. a lamp kindled that Shabbat: even if it went out, it is forbidden to move it — we say: since it was lit on erev Shabbat it is as if still lit and we do not look at the change in the lamp; and a sukkah whose sechach fell — we say: since it was on the sukkah on erev Shabbat it is as if still hanging; and coins on a pillow we view as if still placed — not so one who slaughters on Shabbat for a sick person: the animal is as it is, as it was on erev Shabbat while alive, it is still the same, only a new factor arose that a person fell ill and the Torah permitted for pikuach nefesh; and if on erev Shabbat that same sick person had a hetter, there would be no muktzeh at all from prohibition — we do not say “since it was set aside” — only from the person having set his mind away from it the whole Shabbat and not having it in mind: we rule like Rabbi Shimon that muktzeh is permitted — as I wrote, correct and clear. And although this distinction is not found explicit in the meforshim and the Ra’avad and Ran do not hold thus, still those who permit — the Rambam and Rosh — can well hold this, for it is a good sevara in my humble opinion.
