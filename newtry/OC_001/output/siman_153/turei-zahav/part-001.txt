<b>Another sanctity equivalent to it.</b> Regarding a Sefer Torah, the Shulchan Aruch rules at the beginning of Yoreh Deah, Hilchos Sefer Torah, that it is forbidden to sell one in order to purchase another, since this does not elevate its sanctity. Although no higher sanctity exists, it is nevertheless forbidden, for this is an unresolved question in the Gemara. Certainly, then, regarding other sacred objects whose sanctity can be elevated, it is forbidden to sell them in order to purchase equivalents. So writes the Ran. Beit Yosef, however, citing Mahari Abuhav, infers from the Tur’s words, “but conversely, to lower them from their sanctity, one may not,” that purchasing an equivalent is permitted. This is the permissive opinion cited here. I cannot understand Mahari Abuhav’s view or why the Shulchan Aruch brings it. If Mahari Abuhav refers also to a Sefer Torah and permits purchasing another Sefer Torah, the matter is an unresolved question, and we rule stringently regarding selling an old one to purchase a new one. Even if he follows the lenient view of Rabbeinu Manoach cited at the beginning of Yoreh Deah, the Tur explicitly rules there that it is forbidden. How, then, can Mahari Abuhav infer from the Tur’s words here that purchasing an equivalent is permitted? The Shulchan Aruch is likewise difficult, for in Yoreh Deah he rules stringently concerning a Sefer Torah, while here he records a dispute. If Mahari Abuhav refers only to other sacred objects and agrees that a Sefer Torah may not be exchanged for an equivalent, and the Shulchan Aruch likewise discusses only other sacred objects, the matter is still difficult, for the Ran argues that all the more so other sacred objects may not be exchanged for equivalents, since their sanctity can be elevated. Moreover, why did Mahari Abuhav infer this from the Tur rather than from the Mishnah itself, which says that sacred books may not be exchanged for a mantle, implying that they may be exchanged for equivalent books? The Gemara sought to resolve the question whether an old Sefer Torah may be sold to buy a new one from the Mishnah’s statement that a Sefer Torah may not be exchanged for sacred books, implying that Torah for Torah is permitted. It rejected the proof by explaining that the Mishnah deals with a completed sale: once it was sold, the proceeds may be used for another Sefer Torah, for otherwise what could be purchased with them? Thus, regarding a Sefer Torah there is a distinction between after the fact and initially. After the fact it is certainly permitted to buy another Sefer Torah, but initially the question remains unresolved. Regarding other sacred objects, however, even after the fact one can purchase something of greater sanctity, and therefore one could infer from the Mishnah’s prohibition only against lowering sanctity that purchasing an equivalent is permitted. Yet the Ran suggests that no inference can be drawn from the Mishnah, because once the Tanna stated regarding a Sefer Torah that only lowering sanctity is forbidden, he used the same formulation for the other objects. The Tur, however, grouped them all together in one statement, “but conversely, to lower them from their sanctity, one may not,” although he rules in Yoreh Deah that a Sefer Torah may not initially be exchanged for an equivalent. If equivalents were also forbidden for the other sacred objects, why did he not state the greater novelty that even an equivalent is forbidden? One may therefore say that because, for the other sacred objects, only lowering sanctity is forbidden, he included a Sefer Torah with them and relied on his ruling in Yoreh Deah that a Sefer Torah may not even be exchanged for an equivalent. This correctly explains Mahari Abuhav’s second difficulty. The first remains, for if exchanging other sacred objects for equivalents is more lenient than exchanging a Sefer Torah, how could the Gemara attempt to prove from the Sefer Torah clause that purchasing a new one is permitted? Perhaps it is forbidden, and the Mishnah uses the language of lowering sanctity only because of the other objects. This requires study. Nowadays we see many people sell sacred books and buy others with the proceeds. It appears that when a person initially purchases a book, his intention is to retain it only as long as he needs it, and if a better edition becomes available he will sell this one and buy the better one. This is effective even without an explicit stipulation. A similar principle appears elsewhere, including siman 154, seif 8, in the gloss.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>They may change the surplus, etc.</b> Rashi explains that they may lower it, because it has not yet come into actual use for the more stringent sanctity. This implies that it must at least be spent for a sacred purpose, but may be used even for a lesser sanctity. This also appears to be the intention of the Tur, Rambam, and Shulchan Aruch, who write at the end that it may be changed even to a lesser sanctity. Proof is that here they use the expression “they may change,” whereas in seif 7, when the seven trustees consented, the Shulchan Aruch writes that they may spend the money for whatever they wish, meaning even for a secular purpose. Here, where a mitzvah purpose is required, all communal needs are included within mitzvah purposes. See below for proof.

<b>The Kesef Mishneh asks</b> in the name of Ramach: Why may they not change the money? This should be comparable to spinning thread for a garment, which all agree does not create sanctity. Even regarding bricks, the beginning of the chapter states that they have no sanctity; all the more so collected money. He therefore suggests explaining the passage about collected money differently, or ruling that it is not halachah. Beit Yosef later asks regarding a house built without designation: Just as money collected for building a synagogue acquires synagogue sanctity, why do donated wood and stones not acquire sanctity, so that they may be changed only to a higher sanctity? He answers that one may distinguish between materials delivered to the treasurer and those not delivered, interpreting the rule that new bricks lack sanctity as applying only before delivery. He admits that this is forced. My father-in-law, the Bach, called this true Torah, but I am astonished. There is no difficulty. The principle that designation alone is ineffective is not relevant to the donor’s vow. Whenever a person vows an object for a sacred purpose, he must fulfill his vow. If he designated a particular object but has not yet used it, he may substitute another because designation alone is ineffective, but he must still fulfill the vow in another way. Thus, one who built a house for a synagogue has not sanctified the building until prayer is held there, but he has not discharged his vow and must use it as a synagogue. The same applies to new bricks: he must fulfill his vow and use them for a synagogue, but before actual use they possess no inherent sanctity. The same applies to one who wove a garment for the dead, or even spun thread for such a garment. The garment itself is not prohibited for benefit, but the person who vowed must fulfill his vow and provide a garment, whichever one he chooses.

The rule here that collected money may be changed only to a higher sanctity does not mean that the money itself has become sacred, for designation alone is ineffective. Rather, if the contributors wish to fulfill their obligation to build a synagogue by spending this money on another mitzvah, they have not fulfilled their obligation. They discharge it only by using it for the synagogue. If they wish to lend this money temporarily for another purpose and later replace it with other money for the synagogue, that is certainly permitted, because the collected coins themselves possess no sanctity. Their obligation remains until they build the synagogue. This is explicit in seif 13, where collected money may be used temporarily for a mitzvah until it is replenished. This applies only where there has been mere designation. Once an object has actually served a sacred purpose, sanctity inheres in the object, and it may not be lent even for another mitzvah with a replacement supplied. Therefore, old bricks that were part of a synagogue may not be lent and replaced by others.

Proof that the obligation arises from the vow appears in Sanhedrin 48. Rava holds that designation alone is ineffective. The Gemara challenges him from the law that surplus money collected for a deceased person belongs to the heirs. It answers that the deceased suffers humiliation from the collection and waives that humiliation so that the surplus should go to his heirs. The question and answer concern only the surplus. The amount needed for the burial clearly belongs to the deceased. Why is it not mere designation? Necessarily, it must be given because of the vow made on his behalf. Mordechai likewise discusses one who vowed land for a synagogue but the priests prevented construction and the congregation wished to change its purpose. Maharam rules that although they may not change it without Reuven’s consent, Reuven likewise may not retract. It is no less than saying, “I will give to so-and-so,” which becomes a vow where that was his intention, and all the more so here, where poor people are involved. If they cannot build now, they must wait in case permission is later granted. The same applies to money collected for a synagogue. They are obligated by their vow and may not use it to discharge a lesser sacred obligation, although the money itself is not sacred. Beit Yosef himself later writes that new bricks do not revert to the donor; they may be lent, but remain communal property, like the land discussed by Maharam. This is the foundation of all the rules stated here.

One difficulty remains. In Sanhedrin the surplus belongs to the heirs only because of the deceased’s waiver. Otherwise, the heirs would not receive it. Why, then, must the surplus here be spent at least for a lesser sanctity, as Rashi and the poskim imply? Why may it not be used for a secular purpose? It appears that when people collect money for a Sefer Torah or synagogue, they ordinarily intend from the outset that any surplus should be used for another mitzvah, since they removed it from their possession for a mitzvah. When they collect for a deceased person, however, their initial intent is that the surplus should go to the heirs, because the deceased suffers humiliation from the collection only if the surplus is not given to them.

<b>One might ask</b> about Rashi’s explanation that the surplus must at least be used for a lesser sanctity. Megillah 27 states that Rava permits use of a surplus after collection. Abaye challenges him from a Baraisa: “When does this apply? When they did not stipulate; if they stipulated, it may even be used for hiring a messenger to the governor.” If this concerns money remaining after a sale, why should a stipulation help? It must concern money remaining after collection, implying that without a stipulation it may not be used. Why is this a difficulty? Perhaps a stipulation is needed only for a secular purpose, while no stipulation is needed for a lesser sanctity. It appears that the questioner regarded hiring a messenger for communal needs as a mitzvah purpose, since communal needs may be attended to on Shabbos.

Beit Yosef writes that this applies when they bring their own wood and stones and build with them. If, however, they collected money to build a synagogue, sanctity rests upon the money, and when they purchase wood and stones with it, the sanctity of the money transfers to those materials, which may then be changed only to a higher sanctity. He infers this from the rule that if money was collected and a surplus remained, the surplus may be used, implying that before the intended object was purchased the money may not be used otherwise. According to his view, this is obvious. If the money is sacred, purchasing wood with it plainly does not remove the sanctity; rather, the object is brought closer to actual sacred use. Beit Yosef needed to state this only to distinguish materials brought from one’s own home. But what does Rama’s gloss add here? If delivery to a treasurer created the prohibition, the Gemara could have answered that the Baraisa concerns money delivered to the treasurer. Clearly, delivery makes no difference. <b>אין הצבור יכולין להחזיק בו. </b> הטעם בב"י בשם מהרי"ק סי' ע' דל"ש חזקה בס"ת העשוי מתחלתה על מנת כן שיקראו בו רבים ושיהיה תמיד מונח בב"ה עד יום פקוד אותו הבעלים ואפי' עדים לא מהני לומר דשל צבור הם כי נוגעין בעדות הם וסילוק לא מהני אא"כ יש להם ס"ת אחרת מדוייק וכשר כמות' ובתשובת רש"ל סימן ט"ז כתוב באחד שנתן ס"ת להיכל מכמה שנים ועמדה שם והיו עליה מעילין של הקדש שהן לשל צבור והיתה בפני המקדיש כמה שנים בהיכל ולא שמע אדם שהיה אומר שלי הוא ולא נתתיה לחלוטין אמת שהיתה נקראת על שמו כי כן הדרך של העשירים כ"א קונה ס"ת ונותנה להיכל וכ"א מכיר ס"ת שלו להתיחס בה פסק שם דאם בא אח"כ איהו גופים ורצה לקחת לעצמו לאו כל כמיניה אם אין לו ראיה דחזקה שהוא אקדשה דהוא הוה מוציא ועליו הראייה. ומש"ה ראיתי ושמעתי שבני אדם כשמתנדבין ס"ת לב"ה היו מתנין ברבים שאינה לחלוטין וא"כ אותו שלא התנה איהו דאפסיד אנפשיה כו' וכתב שם דקודם לזה היה מעשה כזה ולקח בעל הס"ת את הס"ת ואמר ע"ז דאין ראיה שבאמת היה שלא כדין והסכים על אותו פסק זקני מהר"ר יצחק בר בצלאל ז"ל ונראה שהוא חולק על פסק הש"ע כאן שהוא בשם מהרי"ק דס"ל דאין הקהל נקראים מוחזקין וכן מסתבר כיון שידוע שהיתה שלו מתחלה היאך יחזקוהו הקהל מספק להוציא מחזקה שלו שהיתה ודאי שלו ומה שהיו לו מעילין של הקהל אין ראיה כלל כי כן המנהג להחליף המעילין מס"ת אל ס"ת הן של יחידים הן של הקהל:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>The community cannot take possession of it.</b> Beit Yosef, in the name of Maharik, siman 70, explains that no presumption applies to a Sefer Torah that from the outset was made on condition that the public read from it and that it remain constantly in the synagogue until the owner recalls it. Even witnesses do not suffice to say that it belongs to the community, because they are interested parties, and removing their interest does not help unless the community has another Sefer Torah as accurate and valid as this one. Responsa Maharshal, siman 16, discusses one who placed a Sefer Torah in the ark many years earlier. It remained there and had sacred mantles belonging to the community, and for years in the donor's presence no one heard him say, “It is mine and I did not give it absolutely.” It was, however, called by his name, as is customary for wealthy people, each of whom purchases a Sefer Torah and places it in the ark, while everyone knows which Sefer Torah is his. Maharshal ruled that if the donor himself later seeks to take it back, he is not believed without proof, because the presumption is that he consecrated it, and he is the one seeking to remove it and therefore bears the burden of proof. For this reason, I have seen and heard that when people donate a Sefer Torah to a synagogue, they stipulate publicly that the gift is not absolute. One who failed to stipulate caused his own loss. He further writes that an earlier case had occurred in which the owner took back the Sefer Torah, but that is no proof, for perhaps it was done unlawfully. My grandfather, Rabbi Yitzchak son of Betzalel, of blessed memory, agreed with this ruling. It appears that this disputes the Shulchan Aruch's ruling here, which follows Maharik and holds that the congregation is not regarded as possessing it. The Shulchan Aruch's view is more reasonable, because once it is known that the Sefer Torah originally belonged to the individual, how can the congregation retain it on the basis of doubt and remove it from his certain prior ownership? The fact that it had communal mantles proves nothing, for the custom is to exchange mantles from one Sefer Torah to another, whether privately owned or communal.
