<b>There.</b> “Therefore Beit Yosef wrote, ‘some permit.’” The Shulchan Aruch’s statement, “and it is permitted to read,” etc., means that one may sell with the intention of purchasing another object of equivalent sanctity. Beit Yosef therefore writes “some permit,” referring to Rambam’s view that this is permitted even initially. If the object was already sold, everyone agrees that one may purchase another object of equivalent sanctity.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Seif katan 5: But there is, etc.</b> Meaning, when a synagogue was sold without the townspeople being present, it is presumed that they do not wish their mitzvah to be entirely forgotten and that the synagogue’s sanctity should transfer to the proceeds. The sale proceeds may therefore not be changed to another use. Regarding money merely collected, however, there is obviously no inherent sanctity. The prohibition against changing it is because the townspeople contributed only for the synagogue. Magen Avraham then asks from money collected for a deceased person, which is only designation, yet we do not explain the restriction there merely by saying that the people gave only for the deceased. He answers that the reason here is likewise comparable to the deceased. There, although it is only designation, changing the use is prohibited because it is degrading once money was collected specifically for the deceased. So too, when townspeople gave money specifically for a synagogue and it was collected for that purpose, changing its use is degrading. This does not apply to an individual donor, since there was no public collection and therefore no degradation; the fact that he gave it for a synagogue does not by itself prevent him from changing it. The same applies when they purchase materials with communal funds that had previously been collected without designation for a synagogue. Seif 8, concerning a synagogue that was built but not yet used for prayer, deals with such a case: designation alone is ineffective. The same applies to new bricks donated by an individual, or to bricks acquired with public funds originally collected for another purpose and only afterward designated for a synagogue.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>In Taz, seif katan 2: It appears that the questioner holds that this too is called, etc.</b> Below, seif katan 5, Taz writes that according to the answer, which establishes the case as money remaining from a sale where the seven trustees acted with the townspeople present, “hiring a messenger to the governor” must be a secular purpose. According to this, the original difficulty returns: the Gemara could have answered that the Baraisa indeed concerns money remaining after collection, and its requirement of a prior stipulation applies only to secular use.

Taz’s words may be explained based on Tosafos. The answerer deliberately speaks of surplus money even though the seven trustees with the townspeople present may spend the principal itself as they wish, in order to teach that even after the sacred object has already been purchased, use of the surplus still requires an explicit communal stipulation. The questioner initially held that no stipulation can ever help regarding money from a sale. He therefore necessarily understood the Baraisa as referring to collected funds, and from it sought to prove against Rabbah that such funds may not be lowered even to a lesser sanctity. This proof works only if, without a stipulation, even a lesser sanctity is forbidden. Then the Baraisa teaches that even the surplus may not be used without a stipulation, not even for hiring a communal messenger, which the questioner regarded as a mitzvah need. If, however, collected funds may be used for a lesser sanctity and only secular use is prohibited, Tosafos’ question becomes decisive: why mention surplus? A stipulation made when collecting the money plainly controls even the principal, all the more so the surplus. Nor can one answer that the Baraisa teaches that the surplus still requires a stipulation for secular use, for Rabbah himself says only that it is “permitted,” and Rashi limits this to a lesser sanctity, showing that reason itself dictates that secular use is forbidden. Why would the Tanna need to teach what is already obvious?

The answerer therefore establishes the Baraisa as money remaining after a sale and teaches that only when the seven trustees acted with the townspeople’s consent may it be used even for hiring a messenger, a secular purpose. Without that consent, even the surplus may not be lowered to a lesser sanctity. This is a genuine novelty.

Nevertheless, Taz’s assertion that the questioner considered hiring the messenger a mitzvah need requires study. The questioner’s proof works even if it is a secular purpose. If collected surplus may not be lowered to a lesser sanctity, the Baraisa teaches that without a stipulation it remains prohibited even for a lesser sanctity, while with a stipulation it may be used even for a secular purpose. This parallels the answerer’s understanding of money remaining after a sale. Conversely, if collected surplus may be lowered to a lesser sanctity, the Baraisa’s statement about a secular purpose is obvious. Thus, both the questioner and answerer could regard hiring the messenger as secular.

Perhaps Taz’s intent is as follows. The Mishnah states that one may not use the proceeds of sacred books to purchase a mantle, “and likewise their surpluses.” Rabbah explains that this applies only when they sold and had money left. According to the answerer, the Baraisa also concerns sale proceeds: when the seven trustees did not stipulate with the townspeople, the Mishnah’s rule applies and even the surplus proceeds of sacred books may not be used for a mantle, a lower sanctity; when they did stipulate, the money may be used even for hiring a messenger, a secular purpose. According to the questioner, however, the Baraisa is an independent rule concerning collected money, not an explanation of the Mishnah, which concerns sale proceeds. If hiring the messenger were secular, the Baraisa would not itself show that without a stipulation even a lesser sanctity is forbidden. Its opening might prohibit only secular use, the direct opposite of the closing permission. The Tanna should have stated explicitly that even a lesser sanctity is forbidden. One cannot say that this prohibition is self-evident, because then the Baraisa’s principal novelty would be expressed only in its closing clause, namely the obvious rule that a collection made subject to a condition may be used according to that condition.

Taz therefore explains that the questioner necessarily regarded hiring the messenger as a mitzvah need. The closing clause then implies that without a stipulation even that mitzvah need is forbidden, thereby proving that collected surplus may not be used even for a lesser sanctity. Although one could still ask why the Baraisa does not state the prohibition explicitly, the wording “even for hiring a messenger” itself indicates that without a stipulation that very mitzvah need is forbidden. The answerer replies that the Baraisa concerns sale proceeds and supplements the Mishnah: without the seven trustees’ stipulation, the Mishnah’s prohibition against lowering sanctity applies; with their stipulation, even a wholly secular purpose is permitted. Since it refers back to the Mishnah, there was no need to restate the prohibition against a lesser sanctity.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Magen Avraham, seif katan 12: In the presence of the townspeople, etc.</b> Beit Yosef cites Mordechai that the rule concerning city synagogues applies only in an ordinary case. If the residents appointed seven trustees and conduct affairs according to their authority, then if the city residents built the synagogue from their own funds, it is as though they stipulated from the outset. This shows that even where affairs are conducted through the seven trustees, the synagogue must still have been built from the residents’ own funds. Beit Yosef writes that according to Rambam and Rosh, even if they built it with their own funds, because it is a city synagogue they built it for the benefit of everyone. Magen Avraham responds that this is incorrect: Rambam and Rosh also agree that the residents may retain this authority if they stipulated initially. In Mordechai’s case, their practice of conducting affairs through the trustees is itself equivalent to an initial stipulation.

<b>Seif katan 22: As written in Yoreh Deah, siman 259, seif 2, in the gloss.</b> See what my great-uncle, the gaon, wrote in Toras Chaim to Sanhedrin 21a: An individual who writes a Sefer Torah for himself and gives it to a synagogue for public reading, formally consecrating it, does not act properly. Once it is consecrated, it belongs to hekdesh rather than to him, and he no longer fulfills his obligation with it, because the word “for yourselves” requires that it belong to you, as we find regarding the four species. One cannot say that fulfillment depends only upon the act of writing, for if an individual wrote a Sefer Torah and it was later lost, he clearly must write another. Therefore, one should not consecrate a Sefer Torah unless he writes another for himself. See there. See also my Responsa Beit Ephraim, Yoreh Deah, siman 62, regarding whether partners who jointly write a Sefer Torah fulfill the mitzvah.
