<b>Seif 4.</b> In the gloss: “Nevertheless, he should go to the beis haknesses to hear the Megillah,” and so on. It should be noted that I was uncertain about a woman's law, whether she is permitted to go to the beis haknesses to hear the Megillah. What brought me to this doubt was what a learned person prompted me to explain regarding the words of Responsa HaRosh, klal 27, din 2. He wrote as follows: A mourner does not leave the entrance of his house during the first Shabbos to hear the blessing at a bris or the blessings of a wedding. That which was difficult to you, that a mourner is obligated in all the mitzvos stated in the Torah, refers to a positive commandment that is on his own body, such as tzitzis and tefillin, or circumcising his son. But to go to a bris or a chuppah and hear the blessing for the sake of chesed, he may not leave the entrance of his house. This is the end of his words, and it is brought in the Shach, Yoreh De'ah, siman 393; see there. The learned questioner asked about this: why is it different from Megillah, where it is explicit here that he is permitted to go to hear the Megillah? This is the content of the questioner's words.

At first glance it seemed to me to resolve it as follows. The intent of the Rosh is specifically that where there is a mitzvah through an act, such as putting on tefillin and the like, there he is permitted to go even though he thereby nullifies mourning, since the whole prohibition of mourning is through refraining and a Rabbinic negative commandment. Even in Torah law, a positive commandment overrides a negative commandment, all the more so in Rabbinic law, because an act is stronger. But hearing blessings and the like is not an act with his body; therefore wherever there is no act even in a Torah obligation, it does not override, and the same applies to a Rabbinic obligation, for whatever the Rabbis enacted they enacted in the pattern of Torah law.

Therefore, regarding Megillah, since each person is obligated to read the Megillah, and the fact that one person discharges another is by agency, as the Pri Megadim wrote in Orach Chayim and as I wrote in the sefer Kappos Temarim, and I elaborated on this elsewhere, the essential mitzvah is an obligation upon his own body through an act, only the agent acts like him. If so, there is an act here, and in this respect it is similar to tzitzis and tefillin. According to this, it would appear that this applies specifically to a male, whose obligation is to read the Megillah, and the fact that one discharges another is through agency, so it is considered an act and mourning is deferred. But regarding a woman, since she is not obligated in reading but only in hearing, whenever she hears she fulfills her obligation and does not need agency at all. This is therefore similar to hearing the blessings, and mourning is not deferred because of it. She would be forbidden to go out to hear the Megillah, since there is no act with her body. So it seemed possible to say.

However, in truth there is no proof from this, for one can say that Megillah reading cannot be learned from other matters, since even Torah study is interrupted in order to hear Megillah reading, and several Torah mitzvos are deferred because of Megillah reading. Therefore mourning is also deferred because of Megillah reading. On the contrary, I wonder about those who forbid below in the Shulchan Aruch for a mourner to leave his house to hear the Megillah; and even regarding those who permit it here, the Rema nevertheless wrote that if it is possible to gather a minyan in his house, that is preferable. Why should this be so? After all, Torah study is interrupted to hear Megillah reading, and it is explained by the authorities that even if it is possible to read afterward with ten, nevertheless one interrupts in order to hear it with the community. If so, if Torah study, which is stringent, is interrupted, all the more so mourning, which is lenient. This kal vachomer itself was learned in the Talmud regarding Torah from the Avodah: if the Avodah, which is stringent, is interrupted, is Torah study, which is lenient, not certainly interrupted? If so, this kal vachomer should certainly be learned regarding mourning, which is lighter than Torah and the Avodah. This requires analysis.

Regarding the questioner's original difficulty on the Rosh, namely why it differs from Megillah, even without this there is no difficulty. Since the Shulchan Aruch has several opinions about whether a mourner may leave his house, one can say that the Rosh truly holds that even to hear the Megillah he does not leave his house. However, one who examines the language of Responsa HaRosh carefully will see that his intent is as follows: the distinction is between a case where the essence of the mitzvah depends on his body, such as tzitzis, tefillin, and circumcision, where the essence of the mitzvah depends on his body, and in such a case we say that mourning is deferred before them; and a matter that is not done by him alone but only together with others, such as hearing the blessing of a bris or of a chuppah, where if others were not performing the mitzvah, he would have no obligation at all, and the mitzvah is not essentially an obligation on his body. Rather, if others are performing the mitzvah, he is also obligated to go with them to do chesed. For such a matter, mourning is not deferred. If so, there is no difficulty from Megillah, for there, whether regarding men or women, the obligation depends on the body of each individual by himself, either to read the Megillah or to hear it; therefore mourning is deferred before them.

Regarding what I asked above, why a mourner should not go to hear the Megillah, and why it should be different from the Avodah and Torah study, one can answer according to what I wrote in siman 232 and siman 431, that there is a distinction between one who began permissibly, who does not interrupt, and one who began in a prohibited way, who interrupts according to everyone. So too here: that kohanim in their Avodah and leviim on their platform interrupt their service, and likewise those of the house of Rebbe relied on this, refers to when they began on Purim itself, at the time when the obligation of reading had already taken effect upon them; therefore they are obligated to interrupt. But if he began permissibly, he does not interrupt. If so, mourning, which took effect before Purim, once this obligation of mourning took effect before the Megillah, he does not interrupt his mourning to read the Megillah. According to this, it would appear that if mourning took effect only on Purim itself, such as where the deceased was buried on the night of Purim and the like, then according to everyone he is permitted to go out to hear the Megillah. Even if he can gather a minyan in his house, he should nevertheless go hear it with the community, even according to those who hold that mourning applies on Purim as well. So it appears to my humble opinion at present; examine this carefully.

While speaking about this, I will mention what occurred to me to ask regarding what the Talmud says there in Megillah, that Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi said: A person is obligated to read the Megillah at night and repeat it by day, as it is written, “so that my glory may sing to You and not be silent,” and so on. They initially thought this meant to review its daytime mishnah. One of the Rabbis said to them that it is like someone who says, “I will review this passage and repeat it.” This is difficult, for from the language of Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi it implies that his novelty is about the daytime, that one is obligated to read also by day. If the novelty were about the night, and the day were obvious to him, he should have said: A person is obligated to read the Megillah also at night. Since he said: A person is obligated to read the Megillah at night and repeat it by day, it implies that the main novelty is about the day. This is difficult regarding the daytime reading: why did he need the verse “so that my glory may sing,” and so on? Especially regarding the other view, how could they initially understand it as teaching only to review its daytime mishnah, while thinking there was no obligation of reading by day? How could they have thought so? Since the verse says “family and family,” we learn that kohanim in their Avodah, leviim on their platform, and Yisraelim at their stations all interrupt their Avodah to hear Megillah reading. At night there is no platform and no station service, so it must necessarily be speaking of the daytime reading. If so, it is proven that there is a reading by day.

The same difficulty arises from kohanim in their Avodah. Although the burning of limbs is valid the entire night, nevertheless, since we have written that it is proven from the words of the authorities that where another mitzvah took effect upon him before the Megillah, it is not deferred because of the Megillah, it must be that when kohanim in their Avodah interrupt, this means by day, when this obligation and the Megillah obligation took effect upon them together. This is unlike the burning of limbs at night, whose obligation took effect upon them from the thirteenth day, before the Megillah obligation. If so, one can learn by kal vachomer: if mourning is not deferred before Megillah, all the more so the Avodah, which is more stringent. Necessarily, then, it is not speaking of the night, where there is no Avodah except what remains from the day. It must be speaking of the day. How, then, could one have initially thought that there is no Megillah reading by day? This requires analysis at present; examine it carefully.

There, in that same seif in the gloss: “And some say that mourning does not apply on Purim, neither on the fourteenth nor on the fifteenth,” and so on. It should be noted that from this I learned another new law regarding one who heard a near report of death on Purim, and in the end it became a distant report. Its law is as explained in the Shulchan Aruch and in Shulchan Aruch Yoreh De'ah, siman 402, regarding one who heard a near report on Shabbos and in the end it became distant, that he observes mourning only for one hour. It is explained there in the Beis Yosef in the name of Responsa HaRashba that the reason is this: although mourning applies on Shabbos and Shabbos counts even at the beginning, nevertheless here it is different, for afterward mourning would apply only because of Shabbos, since it is still the thirtieth day. If so, the secondary matter should not be more stringent than the primary one: on Shabbos itself he does not observe mourning, and should its consequence cause him to observe mourning? See there. If so, here too, since mourning is not observed on Purim, the cause should not be more stringent than Purim itself.

According to this, it appears further that even if Purim occurred after Shabbos and the thirtieth day was on Purim, and he was informed on Shabbos, this also does not have the law of a near report. Its law is like one who heard during the regel, and after the regel it became distant. At first glance one could distinguish and say that on a regel it properly has the law of a distant report, because mourning does not take effect upon him during the regel, since the regel does not count toward the seven days. If so, either way, at present mourning does not take effect upon him, and after the regel, when mourning would begin upon him, it is already distant; therefore it has the law of a distant report, even though it is still actually within thirty days. And on Shabbos, although Shabbos counts and mourning takes effect upon him, nevertheless, since on Shabbos we are speaking of the thirtieth day itself, and since it is the last day and we hold that part of the day is like the whole day, if he had known previously the mourning would already have ceased from him. It is only because he still did not know about it that its law is like the preceding day. Therefore, it is enough to be stringent if mourning takes effect upon him immediately; but if it is now Shabbos and mourning does not take effect upon him in all its laws, they were not stringent to treat it like the preceding day, and we say that part of the day is like the whole day. However, if he was informed on a Shabbos that is within thirty days, and immediately adjacent to Shabbos is Purim, a regel on which mourning is not observed, and afterward the report becomes distant, then perhaps it properly has the law of a near report, since he was informed truly within thirty days and Shabbos counts toward the seven.

Nevertheless, according to the reason stated by the Rashba in his responsum, that the secondary matter should not be more stringent than the primary matter itself, it appears that there is no distinction. In every case the mourning has ceased from him, since afterward it would take effect only because of Shabbos, and the secondary matter should not be more stringent than the primary. In truth, if not for the words of the Rashba, it would have appeared that the reason one who heard a near report on Shabbos, and after the regel it became distant, observes only one hour, is because the reason for a near report is that the day of hearing is like the day of presence. Therefore this would apply specifically on a day when, if the person had died now, the status of first-day aninus would take effect, whether before burial or after burial. In such a case it is relevant to say that the day of hearing is like the day of presence. But on Shabbos and a regel, if his relative died now, the law of aninus after burial would not take effect, as the Rashba wrote in the laws of Chol HaMoed that if the first day occurs on Chol HaMoed, he puts on tefillin. Even according to those who disagree there, that is for another reason, because in any event Chol HaMoed is not essentially a time for tefillin. Apart from that, they would agree. If so, the stringency of the first day after burial does not take effect, and when the primary element is nullified, the secondary is also nullified.

But on Purim, since aninus takes effect upon him by law, and even according to those who hold that he is permitted in meat and wine, this is only because it is a positive commandment of the public; but from tefillin he is certainly exempt on the first day even on Purim. Since the aninus of the first day after burial takes effect upon him, the secondary element also takes effect. This is unlike Shabbos and a regel, where if tefillin were relevant on them he would put on tefillin as well, and would not be exempt from tefillin because of aninus after burial. If so, since the stringency of one day takes effect upon him, the secondary element should also take effect. Nevertheless, it appears that since the halachah follows the lenient view in mourning, one may rely on the reasoning of the Rashba, and especially since my determination does not outweigh the words of the Rashba even as a drop in the sea. According to the Rashba's reason, Purim is also like Shabbos in this respect. Especially since the language of the Rema here mentions that Purim is like Shabbos, and also the opinion of the Baal Halachos Gedolos is that Purim has the law of the festivals, that it interrupts and does not count at all. According to both of these, its law should certainly be like one who heard a near report on a regel and after the regel it became distant, where he observes only one day. Therefore in this law there is a double doubt: perhaps the law follows the Baal Halachos Gedolos and Purim has the law of the festivals; and if you say it does not follow the Baal Halachos Gedolos, perhaps the reason of the Rashba is correct, and then even if its law is like Shabbos, it is like a distant report here. Therefore one may rely on this as practical halachah, especially in mourning, which is Rabbinic. So it appears to my humble opinion clearly as law; examine this carefully.

May Hashem fence the breaches of His people Yisrael, may death be swallowed up forever, may He gladden us with His salvation, and let us conclude with the words of the holy Rema at the end of siman 697: “A good-hearted person is always joyful.” I will present here what I said in my novellae on Orach Chayim in the year 5589, regarding what is stated above in siman 695: A person is obligated to become intoxicated on Purim until he does not know the difference between cursed is Haman and blessed is Mordechai. It appears possible to say a new idea. Chazal said according to one opinion that the reason we do not recite Hallel on Purim is that we do not recite song over a miracle that occurred outside Eretz Yisrael. It appears that this is explicit in the words of the singer David, peace be upon him, who said: “Who will give from Tziyon the salvation of Yisrael” Yaakov will rejoice, Yisrael will be glad.? Since the essence of song is said over joy, and if there is no joy there is no song, he said: Who will give from Tziyon the salvation of Yisrael? If the salvation is from Tziyon, meaning from Eretz Yisrael, then Yaakov will rejoice and Yisrael will be glad. But if the salvation is from outside Eretz Yisrael and not from Tziyon, Yisrael has no complete joy in it, and there is no song.

Therefore it appears that even according to the other opinion, which the Talmud concludes in Arachin, that its reading is its Hallel, and since they were exiled they returned to their original permission, nevertheless they made a distinction to show that a miracle outside Eretz Yisrael and a miracle in Eretz Yisrael are not equal. Therefore on Purim they established feasting and joy, with feasting first. Regarding all the other Yamim Tovim we find only the mitzvah of joy, not feasting. This is similar to the enactment on Purim to give honor to Eretz Yisrael through the enactment for walled cities, as the Ran wrote. So too they enacted further that all festivals made for Yisrael in Eretz Yisrael, or before they entered Eretz Yisrael when all lands were equal, belong to joy alone. But on Purim, since they had already merited Eretz Yisrael beforehand, it would have been fitting not to rejoice at all over a miracle outside Eretz Yisrael. Therefore, although they enacted joy, they enacted feasting first, so that the joy should not come from the miracle alone, but also from the feast, as the way of anyone who drinks is that his heart becomes glad. Thus the joy will also be through the feast, to show that the feast too causes the joy, and that our joy is not complete because the miracle was outside Eretz Yisrael and not in Eretz Yisrael. Therefore it is “feasting” and afterward “joy.”

For this reason, although they enacted reading the Megillah, which is in place of Hallel according to us, since we rule that its reading is its Hallel, nevertheless they also enacted becoming intoxicated on Purim until one does not know the difference between cursed is Haman and so on, so that he will forget to say the song. Since its reading is its Hallel, it is proven that recounting the miracle is the Hallel. If so, saying cursed is Haman and blessed is Mordechai is also Hallel and song. Therefore one is obligated to become intoxicated until he does not know the difference between cursed is Haman and so on, so that the song will not be in his mouth all day and will not be a complete song, since it was a miracle outside Eretz Yisrael and our joy in it is not complete. Therefore the song too should not be complete all day, but he should forget it. This is unlike a miracle in Eretz Yisrael, about which it is said: “When Hashem returns the return of Tziyon,” and it will be a miracle in Eretz Yisrael, then “our mouths will be filled with laughter,” and our tongues will be filled with song all day. We will not need to drink to forget the song; rather, “my tongue will speak of Your righteousness, all day Your praise,” and the song will be complete. So may it be with Hashem's help, if Hashem wills, speedily and soon, and may the redeemer come to Tziyon speedily in our days, amen and amen.
