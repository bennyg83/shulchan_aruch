[4] <b>During the rainy season, etc.</b> We are concerned that he may err regarding "Vesein tal umatar." On Motzaei Shabbos, however, Havdalah appears at the beginning of the blessing, where error would seemingly not apply. Nevertheless, we are concerned that people may think it is a separate blessing. Gemara. The Levush adds that one might therefore err in the regular Amidah and recite "Atah chonein" as one blessing and "Atah chonantanu" as a separate blessing. This is difficult, for if so, we should say the same regarding the request for rain. Why, then, does the Gemara give the reason that he may err? It is strained to answer that Havdalah is different because one Tanna maintains that it is recited as a separate blessing, and therefore one may err, whereas no authority says this regarding the request for rain. The Perishah answers that only Havdalah, which is unrelated to the blessing of Atah Chonein, could be considered a separate blessing. The request for rain, however, is germane to the phrase "and satisfy us from Your land's goodness." See below, at the beginning of siman 294. The Beit Yosef answers that the concern is not that one will recite Atah Chonantanu as an independent blessing in the regular Amidah, since throughout the year the custom is to include it in Chonein HaDaas. Rather, the concern is that one may mistakenly think it is like other blessings for which omission requires repetition. Regarding the request for rain, no such concern exists, because its omission truly does require repetition. This explanation is correct. However, I found in Sefer Zikaron by the gaon Rabbi Yosef HaKohen, paragraph 255: "If he recites both the request for rain and Havdalah, they will appear to be two independent blessings." Thus, this reason is in fact stated even regarding the request for rain, and all the answers above fall away. It therefore appears to me that when the Gemara gives another reason for the request for rain, it is responding to Mar Zutra's challenge that Havdalah should be recited, and it does not accept the reason that it may be mistaken for a separate blessing. According to our ruling, however, which follows Shmuel that Havdalah is not recited because it would appear to be a separate blessing, the same applies to the request for rain. The poskim nevertheless gave the reason that one may err regarding the request for rain as an additional consideration, meaning that even one who permits Havdalah agrees that the request for rain is not recited. This also explains the implication of the Shulchan Aruch that even one who is certain not to err does not mention the request for rain. The Kesef Mishneh, chapter 2 of Hilchos Tefillah, rules similarly. At first glance, this contradicts the ruling below in siman 128 concerning Bircas Kohanim, but according to my explanation it is somewhat resolved, because here there is the additional concern that it will appear to be a separate blessing.

[7] <b>And if he is able to stand, etc.</b> Nachalas Tzvi explains the reason as follows: the Rambam's text of the Gemara, Berachos 29, reads, "A short prayer may be recited even while walking," implying that ideally it is preferable to stand. This is imprecise, for the Kesef Mishneh in chapter 4 already wrote in the name of Rabbeinu Yonah that the Rambam derived this from the incident in which Rav Chisda and Rav Sheshes were traveling. Rav Chisda stopped and prayed, and Rav Sheshes also stopped, applying the principle, "Since it can be done in the better manner, do not be called bad." The Rashba explains this explicitly in his chiddushim. There is, however, difficulty with the Rashba's statement: "This prayer was a short prayer, for if it had been Havineinu, standing would not derive merely from the principle of doing the better thing, because by law Havineinu must be recited standing." The plain meaning of the Gemara indicates that the discussion also refers to Tefillas HaDerech, since Rav Sheshes permits its recitation while walking, as is also clear from the Beit Yosef. It appears that the Kesef Mishneh did not mean a short prayer specifically, but rather any prayer analogous to a short prayer, because they share the same law. This is also implied by Abudraham, folio 131. Yet there remains a difficulty, for the Gemara on 29 says, "The halachah is that Havineinu is recited standing," and the expression "the halachah" implies that there is a dispute. Perhaps his text did not contain the word "the halachah," as it is also absent from the Rif and the Rosh.

[8] <b>That You lead us, etc.</b> The complete text is: "and return us, etc., to my home in peace, for You are, etc." This is the wording of the Rosh. Maadanei Melech writes that although this phrase is absent from the Gemara and the Rif, it is nevertheless an appropriate addition. I also found it in the chiddushim of the Rashba and Rabbeinu Yonah, and in Abudraham. Sefer Tzeidah LaDerech, folio 9, writes that some are accustomed to recite after Tefillas HaDerech "He who dwells in the shelter of the Most High" and "A song of ascents, I lift my eyes, etc." People of piety also recite upon leaving the city gate: "House of Yaakov, go and let us walk in the light of Hashem. For all the nations will walk each in the name of its god, but we will walk in the name of Hashem our God forever and ever." The Shelah, folio 115, writes that if Tefillas HaDerech is fluent in a person's mouth, he may travel securely. If not, he should guard himself from danger, Heaven forbid. Every discerning person should take this to heart. This is the meaning of the teaching, "Consult your Creator and then depart." Emek Berachah writes that when setting out on a journey it is proper to give tzedakah, as it is stated, "Righteousness will go before him, etc." It is also proper to study Torah before departure. The Shelah, folio 185, writes that a traveler has a great obligation to engage in Torah study. He should therefore never cease reflecting on Torah matters, thereby fulfilling the verse, "When you walk, it will guide you." He should study orally whatever he can, or occupy himself with psalms or teachings and explain them. The Gemara in Taanis 5 says that analytical study is forbidden while traveling, though perhaps it is permitted when one is seated in a wagon. A person should not eat more than in years of famine, because of intestinal illness.
