**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Se'if 2</b> A pregnant woman who smelled, etc. Note: See the Magen Avraham, who wrote that if she says, 'I need,' she is believed even if her face has not changed. It seems possible to bring a proof for his words from an explicit Mishnah in Avos chapter 5, among the ten miracles performed for our fathers in the Temple, where it says: no woman ever miscarried from the smell of consecrated meat. The Tosafos Yom Tov there brought in the name of Rashi that if she smelled it, they would not give her consecrated meat. The Tosafos Chadashim were astonished at this from an explicit sugya in Yoma, where it is stated that if a pregnant woman smelled consecrated meat, one places for her a spindle's amount in sauce; he left Rashi with a difficulty. In the margin there, he brought in the name of the Gaon Rabbi Shaul, of blessed memory, that the meaning is a case where the woman's pregnancy is not recognizable, and she is not believed to say she smelled, etc. In my humble opinion this does not seem correct. Since one witness is believed in matters of prohibition, what difference is there whether her pregnancy was established, so she is believed to say she smelled and we do not suspect her of lying, or whether she says she is pregnant? Why should she be believed for this and not for that? To say that smelling is common, pregnancy too is common. Therefore it seems that his law is not true; certainly she is believed to say that she is pregnant and that she smelled. Rashi's intent is as follows. The Tosafos Chadashim's inference is true: it was difficult to Rashi why the Mishnah used the phrase 'no woman miscarried' rather than 'no woman smelled.' But this is not because of what he wrote. Seemingly there is no difficulty, because the Tanna wanted to state the main benefit and miracle, not that she did not smell, for what would it matter if she smelled? Therefore he stated the main miracle and benefit, that she did not miscarry. But Rashi found difficult why this is counted among the miracles performed for our fathers in the Temple, since the miracle that she did not miscarry is a benefit for people outside, and why count it among the benefits of the Temple? One must say that the benefit and miracle were for the Temple itself, namely that they would not need to profane consecrated meat for her. Had she been at risk of miscarriage, they would have needed to profane consecrated meat for her; therefore it was counted as a Temple miracle. But if so, why say 'she did not miscarry'? It should have said 'she did not smell,' for although regarding the benefit to people it says the benefit to them, that she did not miscarry, if it counts the miracle as relevant to consecrated meat, the miracle for the consecrated meat was that she did not smell, and it should have said so. Therefore Rashi was forced to say that in truth the miracle was not that she did not smell, but that even if she smelled she did not miscarry. Consequently, the benefit was to the consecrated meat: even if she smelled, they would not give her, because there was no concern of danger, since it was established that no woman ever miscarried from this. In general, regarding other prohibitions where there is no miracle, even if she merely says she smelled, we give her. But with consecrated meat, since the miracle had already been established that she does not miscarry from consecrated meat, her law is like any other person: if we see that her face changes, we give her, because then the danger is from her own body, as with any person, and the concern is for her body, not for the fetus. But if her face did not change and she merely says so, we do not give her, for there is no concern for her as she has not changed color, and there is no concern for the fetus because the miracle had been established that she would not miscarry from the smell of consecrated meat. Therefore it is properly counted as a miracle of the Temple, since it was established that she would not miscarry even if she smelled, they would not give her, and the consecrated meat would not be profaned. Rashi's view is thus sound: only after the miracle would they not give her. It proves that without this, they would give her even on her statement alone. Necessarily this is so, for the beraisa there explicitly says that we give her even from consecrated meat; necessarily that case speaks where her face changed. Examine this well. Do not refute our words from the Tosafos Yom Tov at the beginning of Yoma, s.v. 'and the vinegar,' who wrote that they appointed another Kohen beneath him because we do not rely on miracles. That is no contradiction. Where there is no definite prohibition, we do not rely on a miracle; but where a clear prohibition would be done, certainly we do not permit a definite prohibition once the miracle is known and established. What we generally say, that one does not rely on a miracle, refers to a miracle that has not yet been established. But once a miracle has already been established, one certainly relies on it; only where there is a remedy without concern of prohibition, even in such a case one does not rely on it. But where there is prohibition, one relies on the miracle. This is clear and correct in my humble opinion; examine carefully. In the Talmud in Yoma, the source of this law is the case of a pregnant woman who smelled; Rashi explained that it was Yom Kippur. The book Shemos BaAretz wondered what Rashi teaches us by this, since it is explicit in the Talmud, and he pressed greatly to answer. In my humble opinion it is very sound. Rashi intends to exclude the additional time of Yom Kippur. Since the addition to Yom Kippur is according to the Torah, one might have thought that even then, if she smelled, we whisper in her ear. Although it is explicit in the Talmud and below in siman 618 that women who eat until it gets dark are not protested against, one might have thought that this applies only if she eats on her own; but if she smelled, regarding not giving her to eat, and especially since this is because of the fetus, there would be an obligation to whisper in her ear. Therefore Rashi explained that this is not so; it speaks only of Yom Kippur itself. But during the additional time of Yom Kippur, since if women take food on their own we do not protest, whispering is also not required. It is possible that this is implied to Rashi because if it were during the addition to Yom Kippur, the verse 'the wicked are estranged from the womb' would not be applied to him, since it is not explicit in the Torah, and if women eat then we do not protest against them; he would not be called wicked for this. Rather, it speaks of Yom Kippur itself. Examine carefully.
