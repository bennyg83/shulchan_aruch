<b>(ס"ק א) אסור כו'</b> שאין צריך עיון כו' ועיין סי' רע"ה דשם מבואר דאפי' שמן כשר אסור לשמש נגדו דבר הצריך עיון ע"כ כאן מיירי אפי' דבר שא"צ עיון והט"ז לקמן סי' תרע"ג ס"ק ג' כ' אע"ג דבפתיל' כשר' כ' לקמן סי' רע"ה אי איכא אחר עמו מותר אבל כשהפתיל' או השמן פסול ל"מ כשאחר עמו:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Paragraph 1: It is forbidden, etc.; that does not require close inspection, etc.</b> See siman 275, where it is explained that even with valid oil it is forbidden to perform by its light a task requiring close inspection. The present ruling therefore concerns even a task that requires no close inspection. Taz below in siman 673, paragraph 3, writes that although siman 275 permits a task by a valid wick if another person is present, when the wick or oil is invalid, another person's presence does not help.

(ס"ק ג) בלא נר כגון למשוך יין לרבותא נקטינן דהא גם למשיכת יין צריך נר וכמו שכ' מ"א סי' רס"ג ס"ק ט"ו בשם מהרי"ל דהדלקת נר במרתף לצורך משיכ' יין הוי צורך אכילה ורשאי לברך עליו מ"מ אין בו צורך כ"כ דניחוש שמא יטה ועיין בספר א"ר:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Paragraph 3: Without a lamp; for example, drawing wine.</b> This example is stated as a novelty, since drawing wine also requires a lamp. Magen Avraham in siman 263, paragraph 15, cites Maharil that lighting a lamp in a cellar in order to draw wine is a food-related need and one may recite a blessing over it. Nevertheless, it does not require such close attention that we should fear he might tilt the lamp. See Eliyah Rabbah.

<b>(ס"ק ד) בדיעבד ונ"ל דאם יש כו'</b> ר"ל דרמ"א לא רצה לסמוך להתיר אם יש נר כשר אלא כשנר הכשר אינו עומד אצל נר הפסול אלא הוא באותו חדר או בחדר אחר לכן לא התירו רמ"א כי אם לעת הצורך אבל אם הנר כשר עומד אצל הפסול אפי' ליכא צורך מותר וכמ"ש סי' רע"ו גוי שהדליק נר לצורך ישראל אסור ליהנות ממנו אבל אם היה לישראל כבר נר דולק מותר להשתמש נגד נר גוי כ"ז שנר ישראל דולק דזה לא מקרי נהנה מנר גוי כיון דא"ל בלאו הכי נר שלו ואע"ג דע"י נר גוי נתוסף אור' וה"ה הכא:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Paragraph 4: After the fact; and it appears to me that if there is, etc.</b> Rema did not wish to rely upon the permission when a valid lamp is present if the valid lamp is not next to the invalid one but merely in the same or another room; he therefore permits only when necessary. If the valid lamp stands beside the invalid one, however, it is permitted even without necessity. Siman 276 states that if a non-Jew lights a lamp for a Jew, benefit is forbidden, but if the Jew already had a burning lamp, he may use the non-Jew's lamp as long as the Jewish lamp remains lit. This is not considered benefit from the non-Jew's lamp, since he already had his own light, even though the non-Jew's lamp adds illumination. The same applies here.

להשתמש כו' והוא תשובת רשב"א משמע דהוי ס"ד דאע"ג דלכתחלה אין מדליקין בו מ"מ אם הדליקו מותר להשתמש לאורו. ומ"ש דלהדליק לכתחלה הוי גזירה לגזירה י"ל דזה ל"מ גזירה שמא ישתמש דקרוב לודאי שישתמש בו וכעין מ"ש התוס' דף כ"ג ע"ב ד"ה גזירה כו' לענין גזירת שמא יטה אלא לפ"ז מנ"ל למ"א דהב"ח חולק דלשון הב"ח מור' דמיירי לכתחל' וי"ל וק"ל:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>He follows his own reasoning, etc.; there is no permission through a shamash beside Chanukah lights, but according to us it is plainly permitted.</b> At first glance, he means that Bach holds that even when a shamash stands beside the Chanukah light, using the light there is forbidden. Although he has another lamp (the shamash) his use near the Chanukah light is still called benefit from it. The same would apply here. According to us, who permit use near a Chanukah light when a shamash is present, it would also be permitted here. This is incorrect. Rema writes below in siman 673 that the shamash should be made longer than the other lamps, so that if one comes to use light, he will use that lamp. This implies that despite the shamash, Rema agrees that use by the Chanukah lights themselves is forbidden. Magen Avraham there, paragraph 4, likewise cites Milchamos Hashem: It is nevertheless forbidden to use their light, because an observer will say that all of them were lit for his needs, as sometimes happens. Furthermore, Chanukah lights do not resemble our case. There the concern is that an observer will say they were lit for personal use; on Shabbos this reason does not apply, because one may use the lamp's light, as Magen Avraham writes in a similar context above in siman 263, paragraph 22. Rather, Magen Avraham's somewhat awkward language refers to another matter. Bach below discusses Terumas HaDeshen's case, cited by Beis Yosef, where one Chanukah lamp became mixed with two shamash lamps. It is not nullified in the majority because it is an item counted individually, and Shulchan Aruch rules accordingly. Rema writes that if two lamps are lit, it is known that one is permitted, since only one Chanukah lamp was mixed in; one permitted lamp certainly burns, and their light may be used. Although even with a shamash present one may not use the Chanukah lights because observers may say they were lit for him, that applies while they burn at the mitzvah time for the mitzvah. In this mixture they do not burn for the mitzvah and truly were lit for his needs. Bach disagrees there and forbids even this case, against Rema. Magen Avraham refers to that ruling. Since Terumas HaDeshen's case involved shamash lamps, Magen Avraham also says "shamash," though his wording remains imprecise. Tosefes Shabbos challenges Magen Avraham from Tosafos, Shabbos 27a, s.v. "Rabbi Yishmael." The Gemara says one may not light with tar because its odor is foul. Tosafos asks why that reason is needed: Rabbi Yishmael holds that anything derived from wood may not be used because it does not draw after the wick, and tar derives from wood. According to Magen Avraham, what is their question? The foul-odor reason is needed to prohibit it even when a valid lamp burns beside it, whereas the reason that it does not draw after the wick would permit that situation. In my humble opinion, Magen Avraham permits only after the fact; initially he agrees that one may not rely upon the other lamp. His wording indicates this. Rabbi Yishmael discusses initial lighting, so Tosafos properly asks why the bad-odor reason is needed; failure to draw after the wick suffices to prohibit initial lighting even beside a valid lamp. Tosefes Shabbos argues that if Magen Avraham permits using it after the fact, all the more should initial lighting be permitted: using it involves the single decree lest one tilt, yet it is permitted, while initial lighting would involve a double decree; lest one use it and then tilt. In my humble opinion, this does not follow. Rema writes, "If he lit, etc., it is forbidden to use," from Responsa Rashba, implying that one might have thought initial lighting forbidden but after-the-fact use permitted. The proposed double decree can be answered: concern that he will use the lamp is not a mere decree, because it is nearly certain, like Tosafos on 23b, s.v. "a decree," concerning the decree lest one tilt. Yet according to this, how does Magen Avraham know Bach disagrees, since Bach's wording indicates an initial case? One can answer this, and it is straightforward.

<b>(ס"ק ה) נתכוין כו'</b> דכשמכוין כו' זה דעת הרי"ף דבכוונתו תליא מלתא. ומ"ש מ"א ל"ג שמא יטה הוא לאו דוקא. דהא בכרך כו' ל"ל לשמא יטה דהא כה"ג האור נתלה בו. אלא בנתכוין להעבות אסור דגזרינן שמא ידליק בעיני' עם פתילה פסולה לבד אלא כוונת מ"א במ"ש כ"ג שמא יטה דל"ג שיבא לכלל חשש שמא יטה דהיינו שידליק בו בעיני' דהא גם השתא שכרך כו' לא עשאו להאיר:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Paragraph 5: He intended, etc.; when he intends, etc.</b> This is Rif's view that the law depends upon intention. Magen Avraham's phrase "we do not decree lest he tilt" is imprecise. When he wraps, etc., there is no need for a concern lest he tilt, because in that configuration the flame catches properly. Rather, if he intends to thicken the wick, it is forbidden because we decree lest he light the invalid material alone. Magen Avraham's phrase means that we do not decree that he may reach the concern of tilting (namely, by later lighting it alone) for even now, when he wrapped it, he did not make it to illuminate.

(סק"ו) ומטעם זה ובב"י כתב דזה לכולי עלמא שרי ר"ל דבש"ע פוסק כדעת הרי"ף דבכוונתו תליא מלתא ומ"ש בגמרא להקפות מותר היינו שכוונתו להקשות. אבל ר"ש ס"ל אפי' כוונתו להקשות מ"מ גזרינן דלמא אתיא לאדלוקי בעינייהו כיון דעכ"פ גם הפסול דולק. ומ"ש להקפות מותר. ר"ל שמניחו תחת הפתילה הכשרה שיהיה צף למעלה אבל הפסול אינו דולק כלל. וכיון שכתב הרב"י ומטעם זה כו' משמע דוקא מה"ט הואיל וקי"ל כהרי"ף דבכוונ' תליא לכן ג"כ מותר לכרוך ע"ג קש וגמי כו' דשם ג"כ אין כוונתו להוסיף אורה. אבל לדעת רש"י אפילו ע"ג קש וגמי אסור דלא אזלינן בתר הכוונה וגזרינן דלמא אתיא לאדלוקי בעינייהו אבל בב"י כתב דע"ג גמי וקש לכ"ע שרי אפילו לשיטת רש"י דל"ל דלמא אתי לאדלוקי בעינייהו דל"ח לפתילה. וכתב עוד מגן אברהם ואם כן אפילו נתכוין כו' ר"ל דתחל' נא דייק מג"א כ"א על לשון הרב"י שכתב ומטעם זה ועכשיו הוסיף דנ"מ לדינא גם לדידן דקי"ל כהדי"ף מ"מ לפי מ"ש בב"י אפילו נתכוין כו' דמודה הרי"ף דאסור מ"מ בגמי וקש מודה דשרי דשם ליכא למגזר:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Paragraph 6: For this reason; Beis Yosef writes that everyone permits this.</b> Shulchan Aruch rules like Rif that the law depends on intention. The Gemara's permission "to stiffen" therefore means that his intent is to stiffen the wick. Rashi, however, holds that even when his intention is to stiffen it, we decree lest he come to light the invalid material by itself, since it also burns. The Gemara's permission means that he places it beneath the valid wick so that the latter floats above, while the invalid material does not burn at all. Beis Yosef's phrase "for this reason" implies that only because we rule like Rif and follow intention may one wrap the wick around straw or reeds, where he likewise does not intend additional light. According to Rashi, even wrapping around straw or reeds should be forbidden, because we do not follow intention and decree lest he light them alone. Beis Yosef, however, writes that everyone permits wrapping around reeds or straw, even Rashi, because they are not considered a wick and there is no concern he will light them alone. Magen Avraham adds, "If so, even if he intended, etc." Initially Magen Avraham only scrutinized Beis Yosef's phrase "for this reason." He now adds a practical consequence for us: although we rule like Rif, Beis Yosef's explanation implies that even if one intended to thicken the wick (a case Rif normally forbids) he agrees that reeds and straw are permitted, because no decree applies there.
