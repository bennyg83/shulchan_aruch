**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Seif 2, gloss.</b> Some write that one should be careful to recite Pitum HaKetores from a written text rather than by heart, etc. Note: This concern follows only the usual understanding of the statement, "Whoever occupies himself with the passage of an olah is considered as though he offered an olah," namely, that it is as though he personally offered it. Therefore they compare it to the law that one who compounded the ketores and omitted one of its ingredients is liable to death. However, according to what I wrote in my work on Orach Chayim, siman 1, and proved there, verbal recitation is not considered actual offering; rather, it is as though one delivered the offering to a kohen to offer. I later found the same in Tevuos Shor. This is necessarily so, since we are certainly tamei meis and are non-kohanim. One who delivers ketores lacking one ingredient to a kohen is certainly not liable to death. Perhaps this is the reason for Beit Yosef's ruling, cited by Magen Avraham, that one is not liable to death through recitation. Examine this carefully.<br><b>There, gloss.</b> Mourner's Kaddish is recited after Aleinu, etc. Note: A case came before me involving one person who was uncertain whether his father died on the twentieth or the twenty-first of the month, though he certainly died on one of those two days. Another person was uncertain whether his father died on the nineteenth, twentieth, or twenty-first. On the nineteenth there were other yahrzeits, so the second person could not recite Kaddish. On the twentieth and twenty-first, both uncertain claimants came to recite Kaddish. The first argued that he should receive the Kaddishim on both days, because on one of those days he would certainly be reciting Kaddish on the correct yahrzeit, whereas the second remained uncertain even across both days. I was asked for the ruling. I saw in Responsa Meir Nesivim that he was asked this question and brought proof from Yevamos 96 regarding five women whose children became intermingled. We do not say that one man should perform yibbum with all of them, which shows that allowing each person the possibility of fulfilling the mitzvah is preferable to one person certainly fulfilling it. See there. In my humble opinion, this is no proof. First, Tosafos in Pesachim, regarding five Pesach offerings whose hides became intermingled, asks that semichah is not indispensable and answers that it is preferable for one person to enter a possible liability of kares rather than for the majority to act improperly. Their words explicitly imply a distinction between a case of only two people, where it is uncertain which one acted improperly, and a case where the majority would act properly. If the majority would act properly, they should not now act improperly for the sake of the minority facing possible kares. So too here, perhaps that ruling applies only where there are five or a similar number, such that a definite mitzvah could be achieved only if one person performed yibbum with all of them. If definite fulfillment were preferred, only one person, a minority of the group, would perform a definite mitzvah. If each performs yibbum with one, however, every person performs a possible mitzvah, and a possible mitzvah also has value. It is therefore preferable for the majority to perform possible mitzvos rather than for the minority to perform a definite mitzvah. Especially according to the opinion that the mitzvah of yibbum takes precedence, if all were to perform chalitzah they would certainly act improperly. Since the majority would not fulfill yibbum and would instead act improperly by performing chalitzah, the majority should not act improperly for the sake of the one individual who would act properly. Perhaps if only two became intermingled, we would indeed say that one should perform yibbum with both, so that one person performs a definite mitzvah rather than both performing only possible mitzvos. Even if we assume that the same rule applies with two, so that each performs yibbum with one and chalitzah with the other, the cases still differ. There, if one were required to perform chalitzah with all, then according to the opinion that yibbum takes precedence he would certainly act improperly. Thus, just as one would certainly perform a mitzvah, the two would certainly do the opposite and act improperly. Everything improper they would do would result only from allowing the first person to act properly. Why should one consideration outweigh the other? They should therefore all act under uncertainty. In our case, however, if the person with the broader doubt does not recite Kaddish on the twentieth and twenty-first, he does not certainly act improperly, for perhaps his yahrzeit was on the nineteenth. He only possibly acts improperly, whereas the other person certainly acts properly on one of the two days. True, the second did not recite Kaddish on the nineteenth and may therefore have acted improperly with certainty, but that failure was not caused by the first claimant; it was caused by the other yahrzeits present that day. The first claimant's certain fulfillment causes the second only a possible loss. Therefore certainty takes precedence over doubt. In the intermingled-yibbum case, by contrast, all of the improper conduct results directly from enabling the one person to act properly, so neither side outweighs the other. Moreover, there the ruling would require one person actively to perform chalitzah so that another may perform yibbum. He may say, "Why should I act improperly for the sake of your certainty? My own doubt is more important to me than your certainty," for a person is partial to himself. Here, however, we do not compel the second claimant to perform any act; he merely refrains from reciting Kaddish. The question is directed to the congregation where they pray, namely, to whom they should assign the Kaddish. From the congregation's perspective the claimants are equal, and we therefore say that it is more fitting to assign the Kaddish to one who will certainly recite it properly rather than to one who will do so only possibly. For us, certainty takes precedence over doubt. This appears correct in my humble opinion, contrary to Responsa Meir Nesivim. Examine this carefully, for it is clear.
