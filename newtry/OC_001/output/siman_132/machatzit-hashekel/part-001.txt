The same would apply that the announcement should be made before reciting the two verses, for at first glance those two verses have no connection to Kedushah. See Beit Yosef for the reason they are recited. Although Levush writes that according to the reason cited by Beit Yosef in the name of Kol Bo, “Why do we recite this Kedushah,” the two verses are connected to Kedushah, Kol Bo states as follows: Since they were unable to recite Kedushah within the Amidah because a decree forbade responding to Kedushah and guards watched whether they would respond, after the prayer, when the guards departed, they would recite “And one called to another,” meaning the threefold Kedushah, and translate it because of the children. They did this twice, corresponding to the Kedushah of Yotzer and that of the Amidah. Although the decree has now ceased, they did not abolish Kedushah DeSidra, lest sin bring the decree back. Levush explains that for this reason they first recited “A redeemer shall come to Zion,” as an apology before the Holy One, blessed is He, for delaying Kedushah until then because the oppression of the kingdoms prevented them from reciting it within the Amidah. They therefore pray for redemption and say, “A redeemer shall come to Zion.” Since they were likewise prevented from engaging in Torah, they also say, “And as for Me, this is My covenant,” reminding Him of His promise that Torah would not depart from our mouths. Afterward they say, “Who dwells upon the praises of Israel,” which the Sages interpret to mean that the Holy One, blessed is He, rests His Shechinah among us to hear the praises of Israel. The word yoshev denotes remaining, and we say this as a supplication: although we delayed it, it was not through rebellion or betrayal, and therefore we entreat Him to remain and hear our prayer as is His way. According to this, the two verses are indeed connected to Kedushah. Nevertheless, this is not the primary reason for reciting Kedushah DeSidra. Rather, it is based on Sotah 49a: From the day the Beis HaMikdash was destroyed, no day passes whose curse is not greater than that of the preceding day. On what does the world endure, since the curse continually increases? On Kedushah DeSidra and on the response “Amen, yehei Shemei Rabbah” following aggadic study. Rashi explains that through Kedushah DeSidra, all Israel engage daily in a small measure of Torah by reciting Scripture and its translation, since at that time they understood Aramaic, and they are therefore considered as engaging in Torah. A proof may be brought from the beginning of the second chapter of Yoma. Although all Temple services were allocated among the kohanim by lottery, the Sages initially instituted no lottery for the morning terumas hadeshen until they saw that the matter led to danger. The Gemara explains that since terumas hadeshen was performed before dawn, while it was still night, one might have thought the kohanim did not consider it an important service, since all services are performed by day, and therefore they would not quarrel over who should perform it. No lottery was needed; whoever wished could do so. Another reason is that because it was performed before dawn and sleep exerted its force, many kohanim would not come, so there was no need to fear contention. If many nevertheless wished to perform it, they would run up the thirty-two-amah ramp, and whoever first reached within four amos of the altar merited the service. If two were equal within those four amos, the supervisor told them, “Extend your fingers.” Rashi and Rabbi Ovadiah of Bartenura explain that everyone extended a finger. This was the lottery: each kohen extended one finger, the supervisor announced a number, and the one on whom the count ended won. He would announce one hundred or two hundred, begin counting from one, and repeatedly circle through their fingers, since it is forbidden to count Jews directly, until he reached the announced number. Thus, although only the two who reached within four amos of the altar had qualified, they nevertheless had to participate in the lottery together with all the kohanim. Eliyah Rabbah rejects this proof, arguing that there it was impossible for only those two to conduct the lottery, as Tosafos Yom Tov writes. Tosafos Yom Tov states that a finger lottery cannot be conducted between two people, for if the agreed number is even or odd, once one knows with whom the count begins, one also knows on whom it will end, so it is not a genuine lottery. This does not refute the proof. Tosafos Yom Tov wrote this only regarding Rashi and Rabbi Ovadiah of Bartenura, who explain that the instruction “Extend your fingers” was addressed to all the kohanim. Why did they not explain that it was directed only to the two who were equal within the four amos? Tosafos Yom Tov answers that this cannot be, because no genuine lottery could be made between only two. If the person announcing the number also began the count, he could ensure victory for whichever one he wished: an even number would award it to the second, and an odd number to the first. If many kohanim participate, however, it is not easy to know on whom the count will end. Yet even according to Rambam and Ritva, who hold that the one announcing the number did not begin the count, a lottery between two would technically be possible because the announcer did not know with whom the count would begin. Nevertheless, the expression “Extend your fingers” implies a true lottery like the other Temple lotteries, and that is inapplicable to only two people. Moreover, there would be no need to say “Extend your fingers.” Although one may not count Jews and they therefore extended fingers to be counted, with only two there is no need to count them at all. One person could state that the count begins with Reuven and ends with Shimon; then another person, who does not know with whom it begins, could announce one hundred or two hundred, and it would automatically be known without any counting that Shimon won. If he announced one hundred and one, Reuven would win. Necessarily, as Rashi and Rabbi Ovadiah explain, all the kohanim were told to extend their fingers. Magen Avraham’s ruling is nevertheless compelling. The Mishnah itself is difficult: if the two kohanim who were equal within four amos had already qualified, why should everyone take part in the lottery? They should have drawn lots only between those two. According to Rambam and Ritva this could have been done through counting, and even according to those who hold that the announcer began counting, so that it could not be done between only two, they could have instituted that he not begin the count or used a different kind of lottery. Why should those two lose after having already qualified and be required to draw lots with all the kohanim? Necessarily, when such a tie occurs, the earlier qualification is entirely void, and even those two have not acquired the right. The same applies here. However, Responsa Me’il Tzedakah, siman 10, disagrees with Magen Avraham for another reason. There, even the two who qualified through running had not acquired an absolute right, for it was insufficient that one should merit a Temple service merely because he was fleet-footed. Initially, they thought a lottery unnecessary for the reasons stated above. When it happened that many wished to perform the service, they allowed the fastest runner to win, even though this was not a complete acquisition, because no alternative was available while they did not wish to institute a lottery. When two were equal and a lottery between them was therefore unavoidable, why should the others lose, since those two had never acquired a complete right through running? Here, however, the two claimants received a complete right from Heaven, while the others lost. The lottery should therefore be only between those two. See there, where he derives additional laws from this distinction.

The same is implied in siman 174. Sema also cites this proof in siman 311. There, two people own two fields jointly and wish to divide them. One of them owns another field adjacent to one of the jointly owned fields and says, “Give me without lottery the field adjoining the one I already own, so that my two fields will be contiguous.” Although the two jointly owned fields are equal, the other may object and insist that they draw lots. Even though this appears to be the attribute of Sodom, since the fields are equal, we do not compel him because he may profit through his insistence. The lottery may award him the field adjacent to his fellow’s field, and in order to induce him to exchange it so that the other’s two fields will be together, the other may pay him additional money. Shach in siman 311 writes that this applies specifically where the person suffered a loss, meaning there, where his wine sank, and therefore the law was lenient in not compelling him to forego his right when he benefits through insisting. But in an ordinary case it would not apply. In truth, Shach himself retracts this conclusion there. See there.

<b>It is preferable for his son.</b> Meaning, when a son is mourning for his father or mother and wishes to travel far away to a place where he will be unable to recite Kaddish, he therefore wants to appoint another person to recite Kaddish in his place. Rather than have someone recite it without payment. The reason may be like the law in Bava Kamma concerning one who injures another and must pay for his medical care. If the injurer says, “I will provide a physician who will heal you without charge,” the injured person may say, “I do not want that; pay a physician.” People say, “A physician who heals for nothing is worth nothing,” meaning that because he works without pay he will not exert himself as much in the treatment. The same applies here: when one pays another to recite Kaddish, he will be more careful in its recitation. Furthermore, mussar works write that a mitzvah performed without cost is not considered as precious.
