<b>The Magen Avraham wrote: at the time of forgiveness, one must say the sin, as the Bach wrote.</b> The Bach wrote this in the name of Derashas Maharash. Although the Bach gave a reason, in these words: according to what we wrote in siman 608, that one must say the sin in his confession before the Omnipresent, the same applies in his confession before his fellow, for in this there is no distinction between transgressions between him and the Omnipresent and between him and his fellow, end quote. The Beis Yosef ruled in siman 607 that with a sin between him and the Omnipresent one need not specify. The Magen Avraham holds that nevertheless, with a sin between him and his fellow, the Beis Yosef agrees that one must specify, for this is the view of the Rif and Rosh: according to the Beis Yosef, they hold that with a sin between him and the Omnipresent one need not specify, but nevertheless, with a sin between him and his fellow, they hold one must specify. So too is the view of the Raavad in chapter 2 of the Laws of Repentance. According to the Kesef Mishneh, even the Rambam agrees that although with a sin between him and the Omnipresent he holds one need not specify, nevertheless with a sin between him and his fellow he agrees that one must specify. It seems to me that if his fellow is embarrassed by this, he should not specify it. Perhaps with this one can resolve the words of the Yerushalmi brought by the Bach, which says: one who sinned against his fellow must say to him, "I acted wrongly against you." If he accepts him, good; and if not, he brings people and appeases him before them, etc. The Bach wrote that the language "I acted wrongly against you" is not precise, but he must specify that wrongdoing, end quote of the Bach. According to the Magen Avraham's words, one can say that the Yerushalmi speaks of a sin by which his fellow is embarrassed. The Bach concluded as follows: know that in Sefer Ein Yaakov he inferred from the Yerushalmi's language that the sinner should initially go himself to ask forgiveness. If the other does not accept him, he then brings people with him and again asks him before them. This is unlike the practice of some people to first send an intermediary to see whether the insulted party will accept the appeasement of the insulter, end quote. But the Pri Chadash wrote about him: this does not seem correct to me, since in the end he goes to appease him. If the insulted party is appeased, what does it matter if a stranger passed between them? Also in Sefer Yefei Mar'eh he wrote that his words are not compelled. This is the main view, end quote.
