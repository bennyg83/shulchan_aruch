**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Whether a wooden cabinet is considered a tent, etc.</b> In our Talmud the two cases are explained: if it is ten handbreadths high, it is not considered an interruption, while if it is not ten handbreadths high, it certainly is an interruption. The Yerushalmi is uncertain, but it is unclear whether it discusses a cabinet ten handbreadths high while a lower one is certainly effective, or a cabinet lower than ten while a taller one is certainly ineffective, or whether the Yerushalmi is uncertain in every case, both when it is ten handbreadths high and when it is not. A cabinet may perhaps be more fixed than a canopy, as will be stated below. Rabbeinu Yerucham writes: “If his heart sees the ervah, it is forbidden. This is also stated in the Yerushalmi.” Rabbeinu Yerucham must therefore have explained the Yerushalmi as the Magen Avraham does, and he ruled leniently on its unresolved question, as the Magen Avraham writes. His words are then well understood. If he had explained the Yerushalmi like the Rosh, as questioning whether one's heart seeing ervah is forbidden, what would he mean by concluding, “This is also stated in the Yerushalmi”? If he referred to his statement that one's heart seeing ervah is forbidden, treating the question stringently, he would not need proof from the Yerushalmi's uncertainty, because this is already a dispute among Tannaim in our Talmud in the chapter Mi Shemeiso. If he referred to the first clause, that one puts his head outside the window, meaning where his heart does not see the ervah, as the Magen Avraham explains afterward, that permission is not established in the Yerushalmi. On the contrary, perhaps that is precisely the Yerushalmi's uncertainty, as the Magen Avraham indeed understands it. We must therefore say that Rabbeinu Yerucham also explained the Yerushalmi as the Magen Avraham does. Why, then, did Rabbeinu Yerucham need to cite the Yerushalmi rather than the passage in Sukkah cited by the Magen Avraham, where the Gemara plainly permits it? One may answer that the latter version there does not state the law of a canopy concerning Krias Shema at all. It discusses only one who sleeps beneath a canopy in a sukkah, whether he is considered to be sleeping within the sukkah and the canopy is not an interruption. Since the latter version is primary, the law concerning Krias Shema is not established there, and Rabbeinu Yerucham therefore cites the Yerushalmi. Alternatively, Rabbeinu Yerucham may hold that a wooden cabinet is more fixed than a canopy. The Yerushalmi discusses a cabinet less than ten handbreadths high and does not disagree with our Talmud. Although a canopy less than ten handbreadths high is an interruption, it remained uncertain whether a cabinet, being more fixed, is not an interruption. Rabbeinu Yerucham therefore states the greater novelty, that even a wooden cabinet is an interruption, and all the more so a canopy. The law of a cabinet is not stated in Sukkah. His failure to distinguish between a cabinet lower than ten and one ten handbreadths high may be because an ordinary cabinet is lower than ten. Alternatively, Rabbeinu Yerucham permits it in every case, because he considers the latter version in Sukkah primary, so our Talmud never establishes the law of a canopy concerning Krias Shema. Since the Yerushalmi is simply uncertain about a cabinet, it implies both one ten handbreadths high and one lower than ten. He ruled the unresolved question leniently, and for this reason it is considered an interruption and is permitted in every case.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>It is possible that Rabbi Yirmeyah establishes it, etc.</b> The authorities who omitted this law evidently considered it forced to establish the case in this manner. Had the case involved one who covered his heart, the Yerushalmi should have stated so explicitly. Rather, those authorities understood that the Yerushalmi permits one's heart seeing ervah. Tosafos in Sukkah likewise state explicitly that the permission to put one's head outside a canopy and recite Krias Shema necessarily assumes that one's heart seeing ervah is permitted. They plainly considered it forced to establish the case as one who covered his heart. Since we rule that one's heart seeing ervah is forbidden, these authorities omitted the law. Although they could still have recorded that if he covers his heart, the canopy is an interruption when he puts out his head, this is no objection. The authorities are not required to record every novel law inferred through analysis when it is not stated explicitly in the Gemara. This has likewise been written many times regarding the Rambam. Rabbeinu Yerucham, however, according to the Magen Avraham, establishes both our Talmud and the Yerushalmi in precisely such a case, and therefore records the law.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>With his hands, etc.</b> “And he wishes to stand naked.” This refers to one who had Hashem's Name written on his skin and required immersion. He therefore had to remove his clothing. Since it is forbidden to stand naked before Hashem's Name, if he can cover the Name with a reed he should do so. It is not an intervening substance for immersion, because water enters around it. If he has no reed, the Sages maintain that he should not cover it with his hand, lest he forget and remove his hand from the Name, thereby standing naked before the Name. He should therefore not immerse. Rabbi Yose maintains that immersion at its proper time is a mitzvah. To avoid postponing immersion at its proper time, he holds that we do not issue this decree and that he should cover it with his hand. This is the substance of the Gemara's discussion.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Moreover, in the Gemara also, etc.</b> The wording requires explanation, because it implies that the Magen Avraham adds another reason to the Terumas HaDeshen, while one who examines the Terumas HaDeshen sees that the Magen Avraham necessarily disagrees with him. Terumas HaDeshen proves that a straw hat is considered a covering from the Gemara's statement that one covers Hashem's Name with a reed, for what difference is there between a reed and straw? At first he sought to reject the proof, because mentioning a blessing may be more stringent than standing naked before Hashem's Name. Regarding the Name, covering it with one's hand would have sufficed were it not for the decree lest he forget and remove his hand. Regarding a blessing, however, Rabbeinu Tam writes that this does not help. Placing one's hand on the heart so that the heart should not see the ervah is ineffective, and a garment is specifically required. It would therefore follow that mentioning a blessing is more stringent, and the effectiveness of a straw hat cannot be derived from there. He concludes, however, that mentioning Hashem's Name and standing naked before the Name are actually equivalent, and the law of a straw hat may indeed be derived from there. The reason a hand would suffice there absent the decree lest he forget, while it does not suffice for a blessing, is that a substance of the same kind does not intervene, as stated above. This is the substance of Terumas HaDeshen's words. The decree lest he forget was therefore fully considered by Terumas HaDeshen, yet he still raised his proof. He must hold that this decree does not apply to mentioning a blessing, as Peri Chadash also writes. The reason is that immersion continues for some time, so there is concern that he may forget. Mentioning a blessing is very brief, and we need not fear that during the short interval from beginning the blessing, after placing his hand on his heart, he will forget before finishing it and remove his hand. Alternatively, even if he forgets, he will presumably remember immediately and cover himself again. Regarding Hashem's Name, the moment he removes his hand he has already violated the prohibition by standing naked before the Name, and replacing his hand does not correct what he did. Regarding a blessing, however, the matter is different. In any event, Terumas HaDeshen holds that the decree lest he forget does not apply to a blessing. Since the Magen Avraham holds that it does apply even to a blessing, he necessarily disagrees with Terumas HaDeshen on this point.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Seif katan 6, “We treat it, etc.”</b> It nevertheless constitutes an interruption. Lechem Chamudos, in the chapter Mi Shemeiso, seif 113, writes regarding Beit Yosef's ruling: “This requires explanation. It therefore appears to me that it is considered an interruption because he performs an unusual act, since people do not ordinarily embrace themselves in this manner. By contrast, merely covering his heart with his hand is common and frequent, and therefore such covering is not considered a covering.” Peri Chadash disputes this ruling. See there.
