[ד] <b>“Between east and west,” etc.</b> <i>Rashi</i>: feet toward east and head toward west—or the reverse—but [the bed’s width lies] between north and south. The Bach and Taz cite the Zohar, <i>Parashat Bamidbar</i>: “between north and south” means head east and feet west—end of quotation. <i>Sheyarei Knesset HaGedolah</i> wrote that therefore people are not careful about this nowadays at all—end of quotation. <i>Olas Tamid</i> wrote: “some do this, some do that”—end of quotation; see <i>Tosafos Shabbat</i> 61a <i>s.v.</i> <i>dav</i>… I found in Responsa Menachem Azariah siman 3 that early authorities explained the bed’s <i>width</i> should run north–south so that conception resembles the formation of Adam; the reason is not the Shechinah—on the contrary, one should think holy thoughts at relations. Moreover, even in a privy the prohibition applies only where there is no fence; relations are permitted only where there is a fence and the whole body is covered. For practice he concluded the head of the bed should be west and the northwest corner highest—end of quotation. For <i>Rashi</i> and the plain <i>Shulchan Aruch</i> his head is north or south—so wrote Bach explicitly. In <i>Lechem Chamudos</i> I found he requires the head specifically toward north—hence he mentions north before south even though south is to one’s right—end of quotation. This needs reconciliation with <i>Tehillim</i> 89: “North and right You have created”—where north is listed first. Rabbenu Chananel explained: in the Gemara “north” is called cold and “south” heat—meaning one should use his bed about midnight, when the body has cooled from the food’s heat and temperament is calm: one born from a cold drop is a fool; from a hot drop, wrathful; from an average drop, wise and sociable—“You have given your maidservant men’s offspring.” The Levush wrote that even where there are partitions one must be strict—unlike relieving above, for it is more disgraceful to stretch one’s legs toward the Shechinah—and the Bach answered that at relations one must sanctify oneself more.

[6] <b>He sits with his face, etc.</b> This implies that having one's back toward the sacred place is forbidden, as the Beit Yosef wrote. It is possible to align the words of the Levush accordingly, or to say that he holds that the primary explanation is like the first explanation in the Beit Yosef, that it states a novelty: even facing it, where he exposes two tefachim, is permitted, and certainly with his back toward it. Nevertheless, the Levush overlooked the words of the Kesef Mishneh, chapter 4 of Hilchos Beis HaBechirah, that he retracted, because there is a scribal error, and the text in the Rambam and Shulchan Aruch should read: he should not sit with his face toward the sacred place, but rather to the north or to the south, or he should move the sacred place to the sides; end quote. The Olas Tamid wrote that even now there is a practical difference, for the sanctity remains in its place, as stated in Megillah. In the book Sheyarei Kenesses HaGedolah he wrote that this law was written unnecessarily and that he was not precise; end of his words. I do not know why he wrote this about the Sheyarei Kenesses HaGedolah, for he copied his words from the Bach; see there. Also, regarding what he challenged from the Gemara in Megillah, one must say that only regarding matters forbidden in the place of the Mikdash itself do we say that its sanctity remains in its place. Know this, for we say in Berachos 62 that nowadays there is no prohibition to act frivolously from Tzofim inward, and so it also implies in Lechem Chamudos, page 75; examine this carefully. Sefer Chasidim, siman 620, writes that when one leaves the privy he should look at the place where he sat, so that there should be no filth, lest his fellow come suddenly, or at night, and sit on it. A person should not make a privy aligned opposite a synagogue, so that the anal opening should not be exposed opposite the synagogue or beis midrash.

[8] <b>And not with the right hand, etc.</b> Likewise, not with the middle finger of the left hand, because one places tefillin on it; Shelah. All of this concerns wiping. But regarding rubbing away droplets, it is explained in Beit Yosef, siman 43, that it is permitted even with the right hand. It is possible that the same applies, that it is permitted to kill a louse with the right hand. The reason for the prohibition against wiping with the right hand is that he ties tefillin with the right hand. Another reason is that he shows the cantillation reasons of the Torah with it. According to these reasons, if he is left-handed, he should wipe with his right hand. But according to the reason that the Torah was given with the right hand, even for a left-handed person we follow the right hand of all people, and he must wipe with his left. It seems to me that even according to the reason that one shows the cantillation reasons of the Torah with them, it is permitted to show the cantillation reasons of the Torah with the left hand, even though it is designated for wiping, for a left-handed person wipes with his left hand even though he shows the cantillation reasons of the Torah with it. So it seems to me. However, in Beit Yosef, siman 43, he wrote that it is forbidden to wipe with the right hand; therefore one must say that a left-handed person also wipes with his own right hand, for his left hand is considered his right even according to the reason that the Torah was given with the right hand. So wrote the Taz. In Responsa Beis Yaakov, siman 149, he disagrees with what the Taz wrote, but regarding the reason, etc., and he did not examine the end of the Taz's words. However, this requires study, for one can say that what he wrote, that Beit Yosef holds it is forbidden to wipe with the right hand, refers only to an ordinary person, but a left-handed person is different.

[10] <b>And so they practiced.</b> See the Levush. The Malbushei Yom Tov wrote: it appears that the nature of lands or times changes, as he wrote in Even HaEzer, siman 156; end quote. In the Shelah, page 79, he wrote in Shabbos, chapter HaMotzi: there are three things which, if one does them, make him vulnerable to sorcery: first, one who pulls a vegetable from a bundle; second, one who kills a louse on his garments; third, one who wipes with earthenware. They further warned: three do not pass between others and others do not pass between them: a dog, a palm tree, and a woman; some say also a pig. If this occurred, Rav Pappa said: begin with a Divine Name and conclude with a Divine Name, 'God brings them out,' etc., until 'what has God wrought.'
