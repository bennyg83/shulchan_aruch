<b>“Exposed”</b> (no partitions): The Tur used this wording only for relieving in a place that is actually exposed; where there are partitions—such as an indoor privy—Beit Yosef says one need not be finicky. He cites Avohu that the Tur’s example “indoor privy” means the partitions are not as far apart as in a huge courtyard. The Taz argues Beit Yosef’s reading is weak: wherever partitions exist, even very distant ones, it should be permitted; “indoor privy” teaches that any walled area is like a house—the Tur opened with “only in an exposed place,” meaning no partitions at all, as the Yerushalmi says “provided there is no wall” (Rosh); hence our privies that have partitions require no stringency whether partitions are close or far. Rashba is explicit: only with no wall at all—i.e. an open field—does “no wall” exclude the presence of some wall; our master’s hints forbid east/west in a field with no partitions but not indoors—implying field privies had no partitions. Yet Tosafos (<i>Sukkah</i> 36) infer from “entering a privy” that there were partitions; “privy” suggests a structure—Beit Yosef cites this later (siman 12); the Gemara in Berachot (“I followed Rabbi into a privy…”) implies partitions—yet east/west is still forbidden. If Beit Yosef meant city walls make the whole town permitted, that cannot be: a wall adjacent to where one relieves is required; Avohu’s reading is primary—one must relieve next to a partition. The Yerushalmi/Rashba “field” language supports requiring adjacency to a wall; a courtyard without sitting near a wall is forbidden like exposure; Rashba limits strict “field” to no fence at all; with partitions in a courtyard there is permission at the wall. Rosh (“our privies with partitions—no concern”) shows intent to exclude large field privies of the Talmud where partitions were far from the spot; ours are permitted because partitions adjoin the place of relief. The Tur’s “field vs house” wording should have been “with vs without partitions,” teaching the field case needs adjacency (=indoors-like). The Tur’s “only in an exposed place” means the relieving spot is exposed even if distant walls exist—heter only when like indoors; practically one should relieve adjacent to a wall in a large roofless courtyard; a roofed courtyard has no east/west issue. Beit Yosef: even one wall suffices per Yerushalmi—when one sits adjacent.

<b>“But,” etc.</b> — One reason is the Divine Presence toward the west, so one may not face or turn one’s back there. Another is orientation toward Jerusalem. The Gemara (<i>Berachot</i> 61a) teaches: one who relieves himself in Judea should not do so east–west—meaning neither back nor front toward either—because Jerusalem is in Judea toward the north of Judah, and Judea runs the length of Israel east–west; relieving east–west would face Jerusalem. North–south is permitted provided one does not face Jerusalem directly (south of Judah). In Galilee (north of Israel) one should not turn north–south but may east–west. Rabbi Yossi forbids only where one can see the Temple Mount; the Sages forbid more broadly—these dispute the corners of Judah/Galilee not toward Jerusalem. A later baraita: Rabbi Akiva forbids everywhere outside the Land; we rule like Rabbi Akiva. Beit Yosef asks why the Tur states simply “east–west is forbidden”—that applies only in lands east or west of Israel (e.g. Bavel); in Judah or Galilee the axes invert. Tosafos resolve Rabbi Akiva’s concern is the Shechinah in the west (Yerushalmi), not Jerusalem; hence outside Israel only east–west is forbidden everywhere. The Tur’s wording follows that dispute. Terumah/Israel directions differ from <i>chu"l</i> because most of the world is not toward Jerusalem; Galilee’s south is still “Land of Israel,” so north–south there honors all Israel. Magen Avraham’s difficulty dissolves if the reason in <i>chu"l</i> is Shechinah in the west, not Jerusalem—Tosafos meant Rabbi Akiva forbids east–west even in <i>chu"l</i>, and the Gemara’s later clause explains his ruling there.

<b>“Even when his wife is not with him.”</b> — The Tur inferred this from Rambam; Tosafos in Beit Yosef suggest even with one’s wife, without relations, it is permitted—because the Shechinah “dwells” along the east–west axis and lying with one’s wife for relations would be unseemly. The Taz asks why the Tur did not cite Rambam’s prohibition of sleeping with one’s wife along that axis. Berachot: Abaye ben Menasseh was distressed that his bed lay north–south—apparently a praiseworthy orientation, not forbidden; Beit Yosef answered he feared a different prohibition (distant). The Taz resolves: the Tur permits relieving with partitions even east–west per Yerushalmi/Tosafos (R. Akiva) when there is no wall—relations are no stricter than bodily needs; in a privy one may not think Torah, and at relations one should cleave to holy thought (R. Chaim Vital, citing Ramban)—so relations do not apply in an exposed privy and the Tur need not mention them. Rambam does not prove he ignores “wall vs no wall”—Beit Yosef says our Bavli argues on Yerushalmi; we do not rule like Rabbi Yossi’s “wall” leniency but like R. Akiva, equating relations and bodily needs: both are forbidden east–west even with partitions. Abaye ben Menasseh is then mere extra piety (Rashi). The Shechinah is toward west or east, so one should rotate the bed if easy (Maharshal)—not that east–west sleeping is forbidden <i>per se</i>. Maharshal’s gloss about disgrace next to one’s wife explains Abaye’s wording as custom, not prohibition—even for Rambam who forbids relations in a room along east–west if Rabbi Yossi’s “fence” view were accepted; we rule like R. Akiva, so Rambam would forbid; practically we permit bodily needs with partitions per Shulchan Aruch, hence relations with partitions too—yet Shulchan Aruch forbids relations like Rambam: needs study. Maharshal then suggests relations require greater sanctity—yet it seems odd to permit filth-relieving without Shechinah concern but restrict marital holiness; the Taz thinks the Tur fully permits and omitted prohibition; one who cannot easily rotate north–south is not forbidden east–west. Maharshal’s Zohar-based note (head east, feet west for sons) is not Shulchan Aruch’s plain sense—so at minimum there is no clear prohibition.
