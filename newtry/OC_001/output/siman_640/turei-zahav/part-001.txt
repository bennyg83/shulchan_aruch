<b>Like a five-year-old, like a six-year-old.</b> Meaning, each according to his sharpness. In Perek Keitzad Mishtatfin, Rav Asi said: a six-year-old minor goes out with his mother's eruv. Meaning, if she made an eruv for herself and did not acquire a share in the eruv for him, she brings him along with her, for he is like her body, since presumably her mind was on the minor, who cannot manage without his mother. They challenged: a minor who needs his mother goes out with his mother's eruv, and one who does not need his mother does not go out with his mother's eruv. We also learned similarly regarding sukkah: a minor who does not need his mother is obligated in sukkah. The school of Rabbi Yannai and Reish Lakish said: how much is this? Like a four-year-old, like a five-year-old. Rav Yehoshua tried to answer this and remained refuted. Afterward the Gemara asks against Rav Asi from what was taught: a minor who needs his mother goes out with his mother's eruv until age six. It answers for Rav Asi: until, and including. Shall we say this refutes Rabbi Yannai and Reish Lakish? It is not difficult: that case is where his father is in town, and the child goes in and out with him, so from four and above he is not called one who needs his mother. The baraisa is where his father is not in town, and the measure is until age six. The Rif and Rambam wrote only the words of Rav Asi, because we do not rely on that answer of whether his father is present, for the Gemara did not answer this way for Rav Asi, but instead answered that until includes the endpoint. It is Rabbi Yannai and Reish Lakish who answer this way, because they do not hold that until includes the endpoint. We hold like Rav Asi, since the Gemara brings him at the end of Perek Af Al Pi, and so ruled Tosafos and the Rosh. The Tur ruled in eruvin, above in siman 414, like that distinction whether his father is in town or not, but here he did not mention this distinction; rather, the Tur ruled 'like a six-year-old, like a seven-year-old,' which is like Rav Asi. Rav Asi's 'six-year-old' means a full six, and the Tur's 'seven-year-old' means the beginning of the seventh. It is certainly difficult why he did not rule here as he wrote in Hilchos Eruvin, end of siman 414. The Beis Yosef explained the Tur's words here, that 'like a six-year-old, like a seven-year-old' means that if his father is in town, the measure is like age six, and if his father is not in town, the measure is like age seven. In my humble opinion, it is unlikely that the Tur would write in such a hinting way. Rather, it appears to me that the Rosh and Tur also hold that Rav Asi, Rabbi Yannai, and Reish Lakish disagree: according to Rav Asi, both in eruv and in sukkah the measure is a full six-year-old, with no distinction whether his father is present; according to Rabbi Yannai and Reish Lakish there is a distinction. The Rosh and Tur apparently ruled like Rav Asi regarding eruv, namely stringently, so his mother should not perform a prohibited act of bringing her son along when he has a father in town, since according to Rabbi Yannai and Reish Lakish this is prohibited. But here, regarding sukkah, where the obligation of education is rabbinic and passive, for according to Rabbi Yannai and Reish Lakish there is no obligation on him even rabbinically, while according to Rav Asi there is an obligation, since it is rabbinic we follow leniently and do not obligate one to exert himself to educate him unless he is a full six and at the beginning of seven. This is what our teacher the Tur intended here when he wrote that one is obligated rabbinically to educate him. Why did he need to mention that the obligation is rabbinic? It was for the intent we mentioned: since this is only rabbinic, they did not impose the obligation until he is age seven; rather, he remains passive. This is unlike eruv, where his mother wants to perform an action by taking him along, and she should not do so until it is permitted according to all opinions, namely at five years. This appears to me correct and clear in the Tur's words. What the Shulchan Aruch wrote here, 'like a five-year-old, like a six-year-old,' means complete years, according to the Tur's view that we mentioned.

<b>One who bloodlets is obligated in sukkah.</b> In Hagahos Ashiri he concluded there that he is not sick; on the contrary, he is happy and increases eating and drinking. Further, he should not have bloodlet on the festival but before or afterward. These are his words. It seems to me from this that it speaks only of one who bloodlets merely to preserve health and feels no illness. But if he feels ill and there is some chill, it is obvious that he is exempt from sukkah. Even regarding a cooked dish that spoils because of cold, the Levush wrote that one is exempt from sukkah, all the more so where there is bodily harm because of cold.

<b>One who cannot sleep, etc.</b> This matter is a question in Terumas HaDeshen, siman 92, and he resolved it from the fact that a small sukkah, whose measure is seven by seven tefachim and which has no room to lie down unless one bends his legs, is nevertheless valid. I am astonished about this, for certainly it is great distress to lie that way all night in a narrow place. What question is there, and why did the Rema need to write this? If he comes to say that a person cannot escape sleeping in the sukkah because of cramped space, we should say to him: since you made a sukkah in a way that you cannot lie down, you did not even fulfill eating, for you knew that you could not lie down and would be distressed by lying, and making it initially in this manner does not help even for eating, as the Rema wrote nearby, based on the Mordechai. And if it speaks of a case where initially it was fit for lying down and afterward something happened that made it distant or narrow to lie down, then it is fitting to be uncertain whether he is exempt from sleep because he is distressed; but he answered nothing from the proof of a small sukkah. In a small sukkah, he made it from the outset in such a way that he would tolerate cramped lying, and with that in mind he made the sukkah. But here, where it had been fit and the distress of lying arose afterward, this certainly has the law of one who is distressed and is exempt from sleeping; how is it different from any other distress that arose? And if he teaches that it is permitted to make a sukkah in a way that he will suffer distress when lying down, that is obvious, for they explicitly said that it is permitted to make a small sukkah seven by seven, and all the more so if it is a little larger. I do not know how to resolve why Terumas HaDeshen and the Rema entered into saying that this is not called distress. Certainly it is called distress, and he is exempt from sleeping when this matter arose after it had not been so initially. He is exempt from sleeping unless he made it from the beginning with that in mind. This appears clear to me.

<b>Only if he saves himself from the distress.</b> For we require something like dwelling, and people do not remain in their dwelling in distress. So wrote the Mordechai in the name of Re'em. It is difficult: this is obvious. Why should he be exempt when he has no more distress in the sukkah than in his house? It should have said this in a case where he has no distress in either place: why should he cancel the mitzvah of sukkah for nothing? One can say that one might have thought that the reason one who is distressed is exempt is because the essence of the mitzvah of sukkah is to recall the matter of the sukkos, as it says, 'for I caused them to dwell in sukkos,' and this distressed person is occupied with his distress and does not put his heart to this, and therefore he is exempt. It therefore teaches that the reason is not this, but that we require something like dwelling, and a person does not live in a place of distress whenever he can be saved from it by leaving. So too with sukkah.

<b>But he is obligated in sukkah.</b> In the Gemara they ask: this is obvious. One might have said that since Rava said one who is distressed is exempt from sukkah, this one too is distressed; it teaches that only distress that comes on its own exempts, but here he is distressing himself and should settle his mind. The Rosh explained that one might have thought the mourner is more distressed in the sukkah than in the house, for a mourner wants to be alone and to sit in a place of distress and darkness in order to be occupied with his grief; it teaches otherwise. This is difficult: from where would we say such a poor reasoning, that for a mourner it would be considered distress not to be able to distress himself? If so, it would be forbidden to comfort mourners, since one distresses him by preventing him from being distressed. Furthermore, from where do we know this reasoning at all? Why not answer that with a mourner he has no advantage in the house more than in the sukkah, but one might have thought that since he is preoccupied he is exempt from sukkah; it teaches that we require something like dwelling, and he is not exempt unless he is saved from distress, as the Mordechai wrote nearby. Why did it need to distinguish between distress that comes on its own or not? And if you say it was obvious to the Talmud that the reason is not preoccupation, as it says there in the sugya regarding Shema for a mourner, 'if so, one whose ship sank at sea should also be exempt,' and one who is distressed is exempt only because we require something like dwelling, then the Gemara's question here about a mourner is obvious: he is obligated, since it is impossible for him to escape the distress. The answerer should have answered that there is a way to escape here, because he prefers being alone, like the Rosh's words. If so, why distinguish between distress that comes on its own or not? It is unnecessary; he should have answered briefly that there is a distinction with a mourner, who can be saved from distress, for that is the essential answer. If we say, on the contrary, that the truth is that the exemption of one who is distressed does not depend on being able to be saved, but he is exempt because of preoccupation, and although with other mitzvos we do not care that one is preoccupied with distress, as the Gemara says that a mourner is obligated in all mitzvos except tefillin, which are called glory, sukkah is different because it requires intent, as I wrote, then the answer works well: since the reason is preoccupation, one might have thought that a mourner too has the preoccupation of distress; it teaches that only distress that comes on its own, etc., but a mourner is not included in the distressed person exempt from sukkah. Yet this is difficult according to the Mordechai in the name of Re'em, who holds the matter depends on whether he can be saved. It appears to me that both are true, for there are two kinds of distress. One is when he is distressed specifically because of the sukkah, meaning the sukkah causes him distress when he sits there. Then exemption because of preoccupation does not apply; on the contrary, in this he remembers the mitzvah of sukkah more, since he is forced to sit in a place of distress for him, as stated in siman 625, that for this reason Hashem commanded to make sukkah in Tishrei and not in Nisan, because in Tishrei other people leave the sukkah, etc. In this case we say that he is exempt only if he has a place where he can be saved. But other preoccupation, where distress comes to a person from another side, such as illness or other bodily distress, where he has no more comfort in the house than in the sukkah, nevertheless he is exempt because of preoccupation, for he cannot have intent for sukkah. If so, one might have thought that a mourner too is exempt although there is no difference between house and sukkah; it teaches that this is not called preoccupation at all, for he should settle his mind and become obligated in the mitzvos of Hashem. And if you ask: if so, what does this teach? There is no distressed person exempt because of preoccupation except a sick person, and we already heard from the Mishnah that sick people and their attendants are exempt. One can answer that this itself is what it teaches: although it is impossible to be saved, and from the Mishnah I might have thought specifically with a sick person who has a difference between house and sukkah, but if he has no difference he is obligated, it teaches that nevertheless he is exempt since he is distressed and preoccupied. If so, we do not need that forced reasoning mentioned by the Rosh, that a mourner has distress in not being able to be distressed. What we have written is correct, with Heaven's help.

<b>A groom and his attendants, etc.</b> This is the Rambam's language. He holds that what is stated in the Yerushalmi, that many acted stringently, means a measure of piety. The Tur concluded: my father, the Rosh, of blessed memory, wrote that the attendants are not called engaged in a mitzvah, and they are obligated in all mitzvos except prayer and tefillin; and the groom, although he is exempt from all mitzvos, is obligated in sukkah, because he can rejoice in the sukkah. These are his words. The Beis Yosef wondered why he needed to say that the attendants are not called engaged in a mitzvah, since the groom himself is engaged in a mitzvah and nevertheless obligated in sukkah, as he concludes. It appears to me that there is no difficulty. The Rosh wrote in this language: it appears that one should act like the Yerushalmi incident. This language implies that initially everyone should do this, meaning where it is easy to fulfill both mitzvos, sukkah and chuppah. But if it is not so easy, he is exempt. The attendants, however, are obligated in every case, since they are not so engaged in a mitzvah. The reason they are exempt from prayer is because joy commonly brings lightheadedness. Therefore the Tur concluded regarding the groom that he can rejoice, meaning that when he can do so easily, why should he not fulfill both? Terumas HaDeshen, siman 7, wrote: I remember in my youth at a wedding of the daughter of a great elderly Torah scholar, he and several Torah scholars were reclining at that meal, and the time for Minchah arrived at the end of its time. They had their meal before them and did not pray; only one stood to pray, and the others were somewhat astonished at him. These are his words. This is unlike the Rosh. The Beis Yosef in siman 232 brings this and writes about it: there is no substance to this reasoning. These are his words.
