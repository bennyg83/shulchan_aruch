<b>In Taz, se'if katan 3: The Gemara answers that he projects the bulge outward.</b> Taz understands 'projects the bulge outward' to mean that the tefillin's protrusion lies away from the area opposite his head; otherwise it would be forbidden. The entire case with the tefillin, however, remains beneath the pillow. The questioner thought that opposite one's head they are permitted even without a case. The answer teaches that opposite the head they are permitted in every manner, while away from the head they require a separate case whose bulge projects away from the head; nevertheless, the case remains beneath the pillow, so there are two coverings. Taz rejects this. On the contrary, the passage proves that away from the head two coverings are unnecessary. The questioner necessarily held so. It is unreasonable that the questioner inferred from the omission of a case that none is needed, while the answerer silently disagreed and still required one. Rather, the answerer agrees that Rav Yosef discusses tefillin without a case. When Rav Chiya mentions a case, he does not mean that both case and tefillin remain beneath the pillow away from the head. He means that the bulge projects completely outside the pillow. As Magen Avraham likewise writes, only a small portion of the case remains under the pillow. Taz's statement that 'the tefillin lie in a case beneath his head area' means that the tefillin themselves are not placed beneath the pillow; they lie independently in a case, and that case is positioned by his head area. 'Beneath' does not mean below the point where his head rests, toward the ground, which would indeed place them under the pillow. It means outside his head area: The edge of the case is under the pillow, while the tefillin bulge hangs outside it. A position outside the head area is also called 'beneath his head area.' Thus Rav Chiya's phrase means that the bulge projects outward. Perhaps he holds that only this is considered away from the head. If the tefillin are beneath the pillow but not presently opposite his head, he may turn over involuntarily in his sleep, as often explained, and place his head directly upon them. When the tefillin protrude beyond the pillow, there is no such concern.

<b>Se'if katan 4: The same may be learned from Rambam and Semag.</b> This is the correct text. In my humble opinion, however, his proof requires study. If he infers from their phrase 'his wife is with him in bed' that he does not intend marital relations, yet Rambam and Semag still require two coverings, this is no proof. According to Rabbeinu Yonah's understanding of Rambam, Rambam makes no distinction based on whether one's wife is present; even without her, two coverings are required when the tefillin are placed opposite his head. Beit Yosef cites him this way, leaving no proof. Even according to Beit Yosef's explanation that Rambam mentions two coverings because he is discussing one's wife, there is no difficulty. Rambam and Semag, who prohibit when one's wife is with him in bed, discuss an ordinary case that may include an intention for marital relations. Beit Yosef infers from the wording 'it is forbidden to have relations, etc.' that if two coverings were required even without such intent, the law would not depend upon relations and the source should simply have said, 'his wife is with him.' Rambam's phrase 'even when his wife is with him,' however, certainly can include intent for relations. Proof is that he does not state the greater novelty explicitly; 'even to have relations.' In truth, Rambam is not presently discussing the law of relations, which he wrote earlier, but placement beneath the head area. He teaches that with two coverings it is permitted in every case: Even if one's wife is ordinarily with him in bed and he may intend relations, it is permitted. This must be Rambam's meaning, for otherwise what source establishes a need for two coverings? The Gemara there does not mention placing the tefillin between mattress and cushion. Rambam must reason that otherwise a mattress and cushion would be superior to two coverings, as Kesef Mishnah explains. This follows because the case includes intent for relations, which requires two coverings. Rambam holds that the tefillin should be placed in one vessel within another between mattress and cushion, as the incident involving Rava implies. Alternatively, perhaps the phrase 'even when his wife is with him in bed' means even when he does not intend relations, and in that case two coverings truly are unnecessary. Since the principal ruling concerns intent for relations, however, Rambam should have stated the greater novelty; that even when he intends relations, two coverings permit it. It must therefore be that whenever his wife is with him, the situation is treated as an intention for relations.
