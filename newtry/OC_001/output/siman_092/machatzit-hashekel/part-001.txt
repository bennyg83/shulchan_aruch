<b>(ס"ק א) וצריך כו'</b> כ"מ בגמ' דף כ"ג דת"ר היה עומד בתפלה ומים שותתים על בדכיו יפסיק עד שיוכלו המים ואמרי' כשחוזר לאיזה מקום צריך לחזור. ר"ה ור"ח חד אמר למקום שפסק וח"א חוזר לראש התפלה ומסקי' בשהה כדי לגמור כולה דכ"ע חוזר לראש ולא פליגי אלא בלא שהה דמר סבר גברא דחוי הוא (פירש"י כשהתחיל להתפלל והיה צריך לנקביו דחוי הוא מלהתפלל הלכך מה שהתפלל אינה תפלה ויחזור לראש עכ"ל) ומר סבר גברא חזיא ותפלתו תפלה (ופירש"י אע"פ שאינו יכול להמתין (מלהשתין) עד שיסיים תפלתו תפלה ראוי' היא מה שהתפלל קודם שתיתה עכ"ל) וקי"ל כמ"ד חוזר למקום שפסק לעיל בסי' ע"ח הרי דאפי' הרגיש קודם תפלה שמצטרך לשתיתת מ"ר ומה"ט ס"ל לאידך חוזר לראש אפ"ה קי"ל דאינו חוזר אלא למקום שפסק ומה שהתפלל תחלה עלה לו. על הרי"ף דגרס הכי בש"ס עיין בב"י:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>(Seif katan 1) And he must, etc.</b> This is evident from the Gemara, page 23: The Sages taught that if one was standing in prayer and urine began flowing down his knees, he must interrupt until the flow ceases. The Gemara asks to what point he returns when he resumes. Rav Hisda and Rav Hamnuna disagree: one says that he returns to the place where he stopped, and the other says that he returns to the beginning of the prayer. The Gemara concludes that if he paused long enough to complete the entire prayer, all agree that he returns to the beginning. They disagree only where he did not pause that long. One Sage holds that the person was disqualified. Rashi explains that when he began praying he already needed to relieve himself and was therefore disqualified from prayer; consequently, what he prayed was not prayer and he must return to the beginning. The other Sage holds that the person was fit and his prayer was valid. Rashi explains that although he could not refrain from urinating until he finished, what he prayed before the flow began was a valid prayer. We rule like the opinion that he returns to the place where he stopped, as stated above in siman 78. Thus, even if he sensed before prayer that he would need to urinate, which is why the other opinion requires him to return to the beginning, we nevertheless rule that he returns only to the place where he stopped, and what he prayed initially counts. Regarding the Rif's version of the Gemara in this form, see Beit Yosef.

<b>וכ"מ סי' פ' כצ"ל.</b> והוא שכ' שם מי שברי לו שאינו יכול להעמיד עצמו מלהפיח עד שיגמר ק"ש ותפלה מוטב שיעבור זמן ק"ש ותפלה ולא יתפלל כו' ואם יראה לו שיכול לעמוד על עצמו בשעת ק"ש יניח כו' עכ"ל הרי דביכול לעמוד עצמו עד שיקרא ק"ש די בזה ומותר לקרות ק"ש עם תפילין וה"ה הכא אם יכול לעמוד עצמו שיעור פרסה דהוא שיעור שיכול לגמור התפלה וכמו שכתב הב"ח דשיעור פרסה הוא קרוב לשיעור שעה ושיעור תפלה אמרינן בברכות דהוא שיעור שעה וכיון שאם ימתין יעבור זמן תפלה מותר להתפלל כהאי גונא ומדמה מ"א גדולים לקטנים לדין הפחה ובפרט בהיות תפילין בראשו:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And this is evident from siman 80; this is the correct reading.</b> There it states that if one knows with certainty that he cannot restrain himself from passing gas until he finishes Krias Shema and the Amidah, it is better that he allow the time for Krias Shema and prayer to pass rather than pray. If it appears to him that he can restrain himself during Krias Shema, he should put on, etc. Thus, if he can restrain himself until he reads Krias Shema, that suffices, and he may read Krias Shema while wearing tefillin. The same applies here: if he can restrain himself for the time required to walk a parsah, which is the time in which he can finish the Amidah, it suffices. Bach writes that the time required to walk a parsah is close to one hour, and the Sages state in Berachos that the measure for prayer is one hour. Therefore, if waiting will cause the prayer time to pass, he may pray in this manner. Magen Avraham compares defecation to urination and to the law of passing gas, especially where tefillin are on his head.

<b>It appears to me that one may rely upon the Rashba.</b> Meaning, since according to Magen Avraham the Rashba and Terumas HaDeshen disagree, and the Rashba maintains that even where bal teshaktzu applies he should not interrupt, whereas Terumas HaDeshen maintains that he should interrupt, Magen Avraham writes that one may rely upon the Rashba and not interrupt. “It is possible that even Terumas HaDeshen prohibited only regarding passing gas, where it is permitted, etc.” Meaning, passing gas does not constitute a complete interruption, but relieving oneself constitutes a complete interruption, and Terumas HaDeshen may therefore agree with the Rashba that one should not interrupt. If so, the converse may also be true: regarding passing gas, the Rashba may agree with Terumas HaDeshen. Magen Avraham did not write this in siman 103. See there for what I will write, with Hashem's help. This is the structure of Magen Avraham's words. At first glance, however, Terumas HaDeshen explicitly indicates the opposite. I likewise found that Eliyah Rabbah writes that Magen Avraham's statement that Terumas HaDeshen also agrees does not appear from Terumas HaDeshen, responsum 57. Terumas HaDeshen addresses the practice, even among meticulous individuals, not to walk four amos and recite the Master of the Universe prayer when one feels the need to pass gas during the Amidah. He reports that one of the great authorities said that when he felt the need to pass gas during prayer, he restrained himself. Terumas HaDeshen asks that one who delays relieving himself violates bal teshaktzu. He answers from Rambam's wording: if one sought to release gas from below and cannot restrain himself, he walks four amos, etc. This explicitly indicates that if he can restrain himself, he acts properly. Perhaps the reason is that bal teshaktzu applies only when one delays defecation or urination, but the Sages did not decree or prohibit merely delaying gas. However, regarding one who cannot restrain himself, Terumas HaDeshen did not find a satisfactory basis for permitting him not to walk four amos, except that he would be disgraced and embarrassed before the congregation when they notice. Accordingly, the Sages' requirement to move four amos and recite the prayer applies only to one praying privately. There is also some proof that for degradation and embarrassment of this kind we do not heed a Rabbinic prohibition, since several Amoraim delayed relieving themselves while sitting before their teacher. Because it would be degrading to leave and relieve themselves in their teacher's presence, they did not heed bal teshaktzu. The same applies here. Nevertheless, Terumas HaDeshen concludes that it is very difficult to invent distinctions based on reasoning when they are not indicated in the Talmud or rulings of the Geonim. These are his words, and I have copied most of them. Beit Yosef below in siman 103 also quotes them briefly. At the beginning, after citing Rambam as proof that one may restrain himself from passing gas, Terumas HaDeshen suggests that bal teshaktzu may apply only to delaying defecation or urination, but not merely to gas. He thus writes that regarding passing gas there is no bal teshaktzu, whereas defecation or urination certainly does involve it. How then can Magen Avraham write the opposite, that Terumas HaDeshen agrees regarding defecation and urination that he should not interrupt and should restrain himself, and prohibited restraint only regarding gas? In my humble opinion, Magen Avraham understood Terumas HaDeshen as follows. Although delaying defecation or urination certainly violates bal teshaktzu, Terumas HaDeshen nevertheless agrees with the Rashba that during the Amidah it is permitted to delay them. Relieving oneself would entail a major interruption, and the prohibition against interrupting the Amidah is more severe than the prohibition against delay, as Magen Avraham writes from Semag. It is therefore preferable to set aside the prohibition of delay rather than the prohibition against interruption. By contrast, when restraining gas, according to Terumas HaDeshen's initial assumption that bal teshaktzu applies even to gas, there is greater reason to be stringent and forbid restraint during the Amidah, because no competing prohibition is involved. Releasing gas during prayer does not constitute a substantial interruption, and walking four amos likewise does not constitute such an interruption. Why then should one be permitted to restrain himself and violate bal teshaktzu? Terumas HaDeshen therefore needed to prove from Rambam's language that delaying gas does not involve bal teshaktzu at all, and he may consequently restrain himself during the Amidah even though releasing it would not constitute a substantial interruption. Magen Avraham's statement that Terumas HaDeshen prohibited only regarding gas, etc., is difficult because this was only Terumas HaDeshen's initial possibility, whereas his conclusion permits even delaying gas based upon Rambam. It must be explained from Terumas HaDeshen's conclusion that there is some proof that for degradation, etc., one may be lenient, followed by his statement that it remains difficult to invent distinctions. Since Terumas HaDeshen records the practice not to move away in such a case, he did not challenge the practice as forbidden. Practice is decisive, and as the Yerushalmi states, when the law is uncertain, observe what the people do. The great authority's personal practice of restraining himself, however, was not established communal practice. Terumas HaDeshen therefore brought proof from Rambam, but did not regard it as conclusive, and added some further proof from the allowance to delay defecation or urination for human dignity. Certainly, then, one may delay gas. His concluding difficulty concerns this permission to restrain gas. Rama likewise appears to have understood him this way, since below in siman 103 Rama rules that in public one need not walk four amos. Had Terumas HaDeshen remained doubtful even about that, Rama should not have stated an unequivocal permission. Necessarily, Terumas HaDeshen's doubt concerned only the great authority's practice of restraining gas. Magen Avraham below in siman 103 also appears to understand the matter this way. Accordingly, Magen Avraham's words here are resolved: even in Terumas HaDeshen's conclusion, the permission to restrain gas remained doubtful. See below, siman 103.
