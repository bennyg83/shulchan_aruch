<b>(Seif 6 in the gloss) </b>Three blessings, and so forth. Note: See Magen Avraham, seif katan 9, who writes that if one erred in the middle, he does not repeat anything. In siman 119, seif katan 5, however, he writes regarding one who erred or omitted, meaning that he omitted the blessing entirely or erred by not reciting it in the formulation established by Chazal. At first glance these statements appear contradictory, but they are not. In siman 119 the intention is that he failed to mention the subject of the opening near the conclusion, or that he concluded or opened improperly. If, however, he opened and concluded properly and mentioned the subject of the opening near the conclusion, then even if he omitted material in the middle he need not repeat it. The Magen Avraham therefore directs one to siman 59, where it is explained that even if one said the words 'Who brings on evenings' in the middle, he has fulfilled his obligation. See there. This is obvious, unlike the Eliyah Rabbah, who writes that the matter requires study. See there, and examine this carefully.

<b>(Seif 9) </b>If, on the first day of Pesach, one recites the blessing from 'You are mighty' through 'Who causes the dew to descend' ninety times, and so forth. Note: The Tur writes as follows: Maharam of Rothenburg was accustomed on Shemini Atzeres to recite the blessing from 'You are mighty,' and so forth. His proof was from the chapter Keitzad HaRegel, where the Gemara states regarding an ox that becomes forewarned: If spaced-out gorings establish liability, then certainly closely spaced gorings do so. Rabbeinu Peretz, however, wrote that he had not seen the elder scholars of France act in this way, because the case is not comparable to the proof. There the reason is that the ox has acquired an established propensity to gore. If three spaced-out gorings establish it, then certainly three closely spaced gorings do. The mention of rain, however, was instituted within prayer and depends upon one's habitual speech, so this inference does not apply. At first glance, it appeared to me that Maharam of Rothenburg intended the Gemara's inquiry there whether the warning is meant to establish the ox's status or to warn its owner. The practical difference is where three groups of witnesses came on one day. If the purpose is to establish the ox, it becomes forewarned; if it is to warn the owner, it does not, for he can say that only now are they testifying to him. The difficulty is this: How could the Gemara consider that the purpose is to warn the owner, when Rabbi Meir disagrees and argues by a kal vachomer that if spaced-out gorings establish liability, certainly closely spaced gorings do? If the purpose is to establish the ox, the kal vachomer is sound. But if the purpose is to warn the owner, it is no kal vachomer. When he is warned on three separate days, the fact that the ox is forewarned becomes fixed in his mind and he should guard it. When all warnings occur on one day, however, he may forget and fail to guard it. It would therefore seem that even with respect to a matter dependent upon memory, one remembers repeated events occurring close together better than events spread farther apart. The same should apply to the mention of Mashiv haruach. Upon closer analysis, however, the words of Rabbeinu Peretz should be upheld. Why does the Gemara identify the practical difference only as three groups of witnesses arriving on one day? It could have stated the reverse case: three groups of witnesses arrive on three days, but all testify about three gorings that took place on one day. If the purpose is to establish the ox, this would not suffice; if the purpose is to warn the owner, it would. This shows that there is no possibility that warning the owner alone suffices. Rather, it is certain that the gorings must occur on three days. The Gemara's question is only whether warning the owner is also required, or whether establishing the ox alone suffices. Accordingly, Rabbi Yehudah's view that warning the owner requires three days follows because establishing the ox itself requires three days. The law governing the owner follows the law governing the ox, and the end of the process follows its beginning. If the beginning did not require three days, then certainly warning the owner at the end would not require three days. Thus Rabbi Meir's argument plainly concerns establishing the ox. The Gemara's uncertainty regarding Rabbi Yehudah is only whether he requires establishment of the ox alone or also warning of the owner, and no proof may be brought from this for Maharam. Indeed, this analysis may prove the opposite. Why does the Gemara not entertain the reverse possibility, that Rabbi Yehudah requires three days only to warn the owner but does not care whether the ox itself was established through three days? It must be because then Rabbi Meir's kal vachomer would fail. With respect to warning the owner, which depends upon memory, there is no kal vachomer, since something repeated day after day over a longer period becomes more firmly fixed in his mind than many repetitions in a short period. Necessarily, Rabbi Meir's kal vachomer concerns only establishment of the ox. It follows that Rabbi Yehudah certainly requires establishment of the ox, and the only question is whether warning the owner is also required. This supports Rabbeinu Peretz and not Maharam of Rothenburg. According to Maharam, it remains difficult why the Gemara did not also inquire whether warning the owner alone might suffice. Examine this well. I further propose to resolve the later authorities' objection to Maharam of Rothenburg, who cited Rabbi Meir's kal vachomer even though the halachah follows Rabbi Yehudah rather than Rabbi Meir. The dispute between Rabbi Meir and Rabbi Yehudah may depend upon the dispute among the poskim whether forgetfulness is considered unavoidable or negligent. Rabbi Meir's reasoning appears compelling: if spaced-out events establish a chazakah, certainly closely spaced events do. Yet in the third chapter of Kesubos the Gemara states that warning cannot readily be given to conspiring witnesses, because if they were warned beforehand they could say that they forgot. Likewise, the Torah makes liability for a forewarned ox depend upon 'its owner was warned and did not guard it.' The owner could argue that although he had been warned, he later forgot that the ox had become forewarned and therefore failed to guard it. This defense works only if forgetfulness is considered unavoidable. If forgetfulness is negligence, he is liable for that very negligence. In the case of conspiring witnesses, even negligence would not render them liable to lashes, because lashes require deliberate false testimony, not mere negligence. Monetary liability, however, can arise through negligence. The dispute may therefore be understood as follows. Regarding establishment of the ox's propensity to gore, Rabbi Meir's kal vachomer is certainly correct: if spaced-out gorings suffice, closely spaced gorings certainly suffice. The owner's ability to claim forgetfulness works in the opposite direction. If he was warned on three successive days, he is unlikely to forget. If all the warnings came on one day, he might forget and say that it slipped his mind. Rabbi Meir holds that forgetfulness is negligence, so the decisive question is whether the ox itself acquired an established propensity, and his kal vachomer applies. Rabbi Yehudah holds that forgetfulness is unavoidable. Although the ox became prone to gore, the owner may say that since the Torah requires that he be warned and fail to guard it, he later forgot. Three separate days are therefore required, because forgetting after repeated warnings over several days is certainly negligent. Even if the witnesses informed him on three days about gorings that all occurred on one day, this would not help. Testimony must be fit to be given immediately. If it was not fit at the outset, later testimony does not cure it, because its beginning was invalid even if its end became valid. If all gorings occurred on one day, testimony given that day would not make the ox forewarned. Since the witnesses were not fit to establish that status immediately, their later testimony over several days cannot do so. Thus, for warning the owner, three days are superior to one day because he might otherwise claim forgetfulness. But when establishing a person's habitual recitation of Mashiv haruach, the person himself is being trained to say what his mouth has become accustomed to say. This does not depend upon conscious recollection, but upon verbal habit. It resembles the rule regarding uncertainty in Krias Shema: if one finds himself at 'so that your days may be prolonged,' he certainly completed the paragraph of 'And it shall be if you listen,' because he followed the flow of his accustomed wording. We therefore rely upon verbal habit. In such a case, Rabbi Meir's kal vachomer from the ox is valid: if spaced-out repetition establishes a habit, certainly closely spaced repetition does. Maharam of Rothenburg's ruling is therefore well founded, with Hashem's help. Even if Maharam did not intend this explanation, the Urim VeTumim already wrote in Kitzur Tekafo Kohen, siman 25, that the spirit of Hashem rested upon the words of the early authorities. See there and understand, and examine this carefully.
