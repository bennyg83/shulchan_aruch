[3] <b>He should wash his hands, etc.</b> This is because of doubt. So writes Rokeach, siman 328, since this appears to follow from Chullin 107. Nachalas Tzvi questions Rokeach from the Gemara's inquiry whether one may eat using a cloth. The Gemara brings proof from the incident in which Rabbi Tzadok was given less than an egg's volume of food, wrapped it in a cloth, and ate it outside the sukkah. The Gemara asks whether this implies that an egg's volume requires washing, and rejects the proof by saying that perhaps an egg's volume requires a sukkah. Even according to this rejection, less than an egg's volume would not require washing. If the fact that he ate through a cloth itself implied that washing was required, Rokeach's ruling in siman 283 would be difficult, for there he writes that less than an egg's volume does not require washing, as stated in Sukkah. Yet there too the Gemara cites Rabbi Tzadok's use of a cloth. Moreover, Rokeach appears self-contradictory, ruling in siman 283 that washing is unnecessary and in siman 328 that one should wash because of doubt. In my humble opinion, all of this may be resolved. Rokeach precisely inferred that according to the rejection in Chullin, even for more than an egg's volume a cloth would suffice, because using a cloth is equivalent to washing. Otherwise, what does the Mishnah teach by saying that Rabbi Tzadok used a cloth? Surely it did not merely teach that he acted cleanly. Rather, by recording the cloth it teaches incidentally that even less than an egg's volume requires washing, since a cloth and washing serve the same function. In Sukkah 27, however, where the Gemara says that perhaps less than an egg's volume does not require washing, the use of a cloth cannot substitute for washing, as Tosafos explain there, s.v. ha. The Mishnah therefore teaches that less than an egg's volume does not require washing, as shown by his use of a cloth. Examine this carefully. Nachalas Tzvi's claim that Rokeach contradicts himself also overlooks Lechem Chamudos to Chullin, page 222, who explains that in siman 283 Rokeach discusses whether there is an obligation carrying a blessing of Al Netilas Yadayim. There remains a difficulty, however, because Rokeach writes there that Chullin implies that even one eating with a cloth requires washing. If so, Rabbi Tzadok's use of a cloth for less than an egg's volume would indicate that washing itself was unnecessary. Perhaps Rokeach means that because of doubt one must wash even when using a cloth, and here he rules stringently. Yet if so, he should have ruled that no blessing be recited. Moreover, the conclusion of Chullin implies that washing is certainly required, as appears below in siman 163 in Beit Yosef. It may be that even if less than an egg's volume requires washing, Rabbi Tzadok used a cloth because he was a kohen, and the Sages permitted a cloth for those eating terumah, as Tosafos write there. The mention of less than an egg's volume would then relate only to sukkah and the blessing. Although Tosafos in Yoma 79, s.v. ha, write that the allowance applies only to terumah and not to ordinary food, that is according to the Gemara's necessity in Sukkah, in the discussion of Abaye and Rav Yosef, to interpret less than an egg's volume as referring to washing. Where it is possible to say that the passage does not refer to washing, it is preferable to say that kohanim were permitted to use a cloth even for ordinary food. This also resolves the apparent contradiction between Tosafos in Chullin, s.v. mai, and Tosafos in Yoma. Examine this carefully.

[4] <b>He does not need, etc.</b> According to all opinions, he does not need to wash. Levush. So writes Sheyarei Knesses HaGedolah. Lechem Chamudos writes there that a copying error in Beit Yosef caused this error in the Shulchan Aruch. The correct wording of Rokeach, siman 283, is that less than an egg's volume does not require washing. According to this, less than an olive's volume has the same law as less than an egg's volume: he should wash without a blessing. So write Nachalas Tzvi and Magen Avraham. In my humble opinion, the Shulchan Aruch did not intend this at all. Rather, he inferred from the earlier ruling of Rokeach, that for less than an egg's volume one washes without a blessing, that the novelty regarding less than an olive's volume is not merely that one washes without a blessing, but that no washing is required at all. This also appears from Bach, who evidently understood the Shulchan Aruch this way. Nevertheless, this is not a decisive proof for practical halachah, because had Rokeach taught the law only for less than an olive's volume, one might have thought that less than an egg's volume requires a blessing. I saw that Taz writes that Sukkah explicitly states that washing is unnecessary. This is astonishing, for it is not stated there, and all the great authorities cited above make clear that this is not explicit in the Gemara. There is also some proof from the fact that Rambam and the poskim link the law of washing to the law of the blessing Hamotzi, and below in siman 168 we rule that Hamotzi is recited even on less than an olive's volume. One should therefore be stringent and wash without a blessing. This also provides some support for Rama's responsum, siman 8, ruling that one who eats bread merely to sweeten a drink need not wash, since he does not recite Hamotzi, as explained below in siman 212. Mateh Moshe, siman 327, and Shelah, however, rule that washing is required, and this also appears to be the view of Sheyarei Knesses HaGedolah.

[5] <b>Something dipped in liquid, etc.</b> If an item is not ordinarily dipped or does not ordinarily have liquid upon it, then even if he dips it, his practice is disregarded relative to ordinary people. Likewise, one who inserts his finger into oil or honey and sucks it need not wash. Radbaz, siman 78. Taz, however, writes that one who eats honey must wash because he is likely to touch the honey with his hand. Shelah, page 81, writes that a confection made with honey requires washing. Bircas HaZevach to Menachos, page 31, writes that washing is unnecessary once the honey has congealed, and Radbaz, siman 101, rules likewise even when it congealed from cold. If it has thickened only slightly and still flows, it remains a liquid. Magen Avraham permits it because it is intended for eating. Taz rejects this, since an act rendered the honey into a liquid and mere intention to eat cannot override an act. Knesses HaGedolah and Sheyarei Knesses HaGedolah cite authorities who hold that something dipped in a cooked liquid does not require washing, unless the liquid was not present while raw and emerged only through cooking. He concludes that when asked about cooked liquid he would say: I cannot decide; do as your heart desires. Olas Tamid writes that pickling is equivalent to cooking for this purpose. It is clear that liquid emerging after cooking is regarded as liquid according to all opinions, unlike Maharshal as cited by Taz and Magen Avraham in seif katan 10. This applies only if the liquid is one of the seven liquids. Derishah writes that even when the food is no longer in water, if it remains moist from the soaking water, washing is required. According to this, one would need to wash for cucumbers or salad even when they are no longer in water or vinegar. For plums and cooked fruits, however, it appears that even while they remain in their own broth no washing is required, since the blessing over the broth is Borei Peri HaEtz, showing that it does not have the status of a liquid. The same applies to confections fried in honey, where the honey is subordinate to the confection and the blessing is Borei Peri HaAdamah. Just as it is subordinate for the blessing, perhaps it is subordinate for washing as well, unless the confection is soaking in honey and one removes it from the honey by hand. If he takes it with a knife or utensil, there is no need to be stringent. Sheyarei Knesses HaGedolah cites Radbaz that even when one eats something dipped in liquid with a utensil, washing is required. With a cooked liquid, however, even one who is stringent may be lenient when using a utensil, which somewhat accords with Derishah. Magen Avraham writes that Kesef Mishneh, chapter 1 of Hilchos Tumas Ochlin, implies that oil, blood, and milk that congealed are no longer liquids even if they later melt, with the exception of wine. This also appears at the beginning of chapter 3 of Taharos. Rambam and Bartenura there, however, make clear that melted oil again becomes a liquid, and Rambam writes likewise in chapter 9 of Hilchos Tumas Ochlin. See Bircas HaZevach to Menachos there. Rambam writes in chapter 1 that snow is neither food nor liquid; if one designates it as liquid, it contracts the impurity of liquids. Rash writes in chapter 3 of Taharos that frozen water is not a liquid, but when it melts it returns to the status of a liquid.
