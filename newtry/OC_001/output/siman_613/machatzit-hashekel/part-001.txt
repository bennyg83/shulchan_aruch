**** ENGLISH ****
<b>(Seif katan 4) Some medicinal benefit.</b> For complete medical treatment, however, it is permitted, etc. This likewise appears from Beis Yosef in the name of Semak. Beis Yosef writes: “Semak writes: Maharam says that a person may wash his eyes with water on Yom Kippur because it is medicinal for the eyes, as stated in the chapter Shemonah Sheratzim, meaning Shabbos 108b, where Shmuel says that a drop of cold water upon the eyes in the morning is better than all eye salves; and medical treatment is permitted on Yom Kippur.” End quote. I do not understand this, for Semak did not write that medical treatment is permitted on Yom Kippur, but only that there is no prohibition of washing when it is not for pleasure and his intent is solely medicinal. In my humble opinion, this applies specifically to a healthy person who does not require actual treatment. If he requires actual treatment because his eyes hurt, it is forbidden even on Yom Tov and Shabbos because of the decree against grinding medicinal ingredients. Siman 328, seifim 19 and 20, permits it only where the act does not appear medicinal but merely resembles washing. On Yom Kippur, when washing itself is forbidden, it should therefore be prohibited. We must consequently say that the case is one in which his entire body became ill, for whom a rabbinic medical act is permitted, as stated in siman 328, seif 17. One might have thought that on Yom Kippur it should nevertheless be forbidden because washing is according to the Torah; therefore he teaches that when done for treatment there is no prohibition of washing.

<b>Rabbeinu Yitzchak explains that the reason is swimming.</b> Meaning, this excludes the other authorities who give different reasons, as explained in siman 248. Although it appears somewhat there that Baal Halachos Gedolos disagrees with Rabbeinu Yitzchak and maintains that the reason is techum, and that the “some say that it is forbidden” cited here refers to Baal Halachos Gedolos, Magen Avraham nevertheless understands that the Tur does not intend merely to challenge Baal Halachos Gedolos according to his own position. Rather, the Tur also disputes him as a matter of practical law, and the Tur’s rulings consequently contradict one another. This likewise appears from Magen Avraham’s wording. It also appears from Magen Avraham in siman 248, seif katan 10, that he understands that Baal Halachos Gedolos also considers the reason to be swimming, as Rabbeinu Yitzchak writes; see Responsa Shav Yaakov. It is therefore evident that even for a mitzvah one must specifically stipulate that the ship will rest on Shabbos. How, then, did the Tur permit one initially to cross on Shabbos for a mitzvah? The same appears from Yoma 77b: Rabbi Yitzchak and Rav Shmuel bar Rav Yitzchak were standing at the bank of the River Pappa, at the ferry of Chatzdad. Rami bar Pappa stood on the opposite side and called to them: “May I cross to come to you and ask a halachic teaching?” Rabbi Yitzchak answered that Rav and Shmuel both said that he may cross, provided that he does not remove his hand from beneath the edge of his garment. Nevertheless, they did not wish him to travel by boat. Otherwise, Rabbi Yitzchak should have told him to cross by boat, first because according to the Tur that is a more lenient prohibition than crossing through water, and second because it was certainly easier for them to travel by boat than through water. One cannot answer that Rabbi Yitzchak wished to teach them the novelty that even crossing through water is permitted, for their question was not whether crossing water was permitted as an abstract law. They wished to ask a halachic teaching and did not know by what route they could reach the others. They asked whether they could cross through the water. Moreover, why did they not ask whether they could travel by boat to ask the halachic teaching, which would have involved the more lenient prohibition?

<b>(Seif katan 10) To minimize, etc.</b> “In practice it appears to me that one should be stringent,” etc.; “I do not know what stringency there is,” etc.; and see siman 498, etc. Meaning, Magen Avraham certainly agrees with Bach as a matter of law that if one can go around, it is preferable to do so rather than cross through water, even though this increases his walking. Magen Avraham’s difficulty concerns Bach’s wording, “It appears to me that one should be stringent,” calling the requirement to go around rather than cross a stringency. Had Rama’s ruling left him a choice to go around or cross, it would be appropriate to call the requirement to go around a stringency. Since according to Rama the law is the reverse, that he should specifically cross and not go around, Bach’s ruling cannot properly be described as a stringency. Nevertheless, Magen Avraham brings support for Bach’s actual ruling, that it is preferable to go around, from simanim 499 and 510.

He had a cloth on the eve of Yom Kippur, soaked it in water, and made it like dried utensils; the next day he wiped his face, hands, and feet with it. On the eve of Tishah B’Av he soaked it in water,” without requiring that it be made like dried utensils, “and the next day passed it over his eyes,” etc. Rabbi Yaakov said to Rabbi Yirmiyah bar Tachlifa: “You told us the reverse,” meaning that regarding Tishah B’Av you said he made it like dried utensils, but regarding Yom Kippur you did not say so, “and we challenged you from wringing.” That is to say, on Yom Kippur, since wringing is forbidden, if the cloth was not made like dried utensils and still contained water, wiping his face, hands, and feet could lead to wringing. According to this version, if it was made like dried utensils, although it remained somewhat damp, there was no longer concern for wringing and it was permitted. Rashi records another version that he heard: On the eve of Yom Kippur they brought him a cloth and he wiped his hands with it, and the next day passed it over his eyes. According to this, wiping with a cloth soaked in water was not permitted at all, only with a cloth slightly soiled from wiping his hands. On the eve of Tishah B’Av, however, he soaked it and made it like dried utensils, and the next day, etc. Rabbi Yaakov then said that the reverse had been taught: on the eve of Yom Kippur he soaked a cloth and made it like dried utensils, etc., and we challenged from wringing. According to this version, even soaking it and making it like dried utensils is forbidden because of wringing. Wiping his hands on a dry cloth on the eve of Yom Kippur and passing it over his eyes on Yom Kippur, however, is permitted even according to this version. On the eve of Yom Kippur he wipes his face, hands, and feet with it, and the next day passes it over his eyes. He may not initially soak it in water, because of concern that he may wring it while passing it over his face. This is the halachah.” End quote. Magen Avraham copied the Rif’s conclusion. This wording of the Rif is difficult to reconcile. I found in Lechem Mishneh, chapter 3 of Hilchos Shevisas Asor, halachah 5: “Although the Venice edition of the Rif reads, ‘We concluded that on the eve of Tishah B’Av he soaks it in water,’ etc., this certainly is not the Rif’s text. The phrase ‘We concluded’ therefore contradicts the version previously recorded by the Rif and certainly is not his. It is absent from the Constantinople edition. The Rif’s view is surely that on the eve of Tishah B’Av he soaks it in water without making it like dried utensils, whereas on the eve of Yom Kippur he makes it like dried utensils.” End quote. Magen Avraham’s words are nevertheless correct according to the version Rashi heard. Still, none of this proves that one may be lenient against Hagahos Maimoniyos and Semak. Even if the strict law permits it, their words impose a stringency for us beyond the law of the Gemara, as appears from their language cited by Beis Yosef. Logically, however, one may say that although they were stringent when one soaks a cloth on the eve of Yom Kippur and makes it like dried utensils, if he merely wipes his hands with it on the eve of Yom Kippur, when there is not such a concern of wringing, they concede that it is permitted even for us. Perhaps Magen Avraham means that he too was troubled by the Rif’s phrase “We concluded,” since according to the Rif’s version even soaking the cloth on the eve of Yom Kippur and making it like dried utensils is permitted. He therefore explains that “We concluded” does not mean that this was the Gemara’s conclusion. According to the strict law of the Gemara, it is certainly permitted on the eve of Yom Kippur to soak it and make it like dried utensils. Rather, later Sages, after the completion of the Gemara, concluded that one should be stringent, in accordance with Hagahos Maimoniyos and Semak.

<b>(Seif katan 12) A bride, etc.</b> “This requires great study, for Rashi in Kesubos wrote,” etc. The Mishnah in Yoma teaches that Yom Kippur is forbidden, etc., and regarding washing, etc., a king and a bride may wash their faces. On 78b the Gemara asks: Why may a bride do so? So that she not become repulsive to her husband. Rav asked Rav Chiya: Until when is she considered a bride? He answered: As we learned, ornaments are not withheld from a bride for thirty days. Rashi explains: “Ornaments are not withheld, etc. If one had prepared the slaughtered animal, etc., and the groom’s father or the bride’s mother died, the deceased is placed in a room, the groom and bride are brought to the chuppah, he consummates the marriage and separates, and afterward the deceased is buried. Ornaments are not withheld from the bride because of the mourning for all thirty days. This proves that she requires adornment throughout the thirty days.” End quote. It is clear from this that the teaching “ornaments are not withheld” does not refer back to the preceding case, but is an independent statement, as Rosh explains in Kesubos 4a: “It is an independent statement. Its meaning is that if mourning occurred to a bride during the thirty days of her wedding celebration, ornaments are not withheld from her. She adorns herself as a bride even during the seven days of mourning, so that she not become repulsive to her husband. All thirty days of her wedding are a time of joy for groom and bride, and she must not make herself unattractive. Mourning does not apply to her regarding these matters at all.” According to this, Rav Chiya properly proved that she is called a bride for thirty days and may wash on Yom Kippur, just as during mourning, so that she not become repulsive. After thirty days she is like other women. If a different relative dies after thirty days from her wedding, she must observe mourning, and we are not concerned that she may make herself unattractive. Rashi in Kesubos 4a, however, writes: “Ornaments are not withheld, etc., throughout the thirty days, although these are days of mourning for others, and all the more so after thirty days.” See Rosh there. This implies that Rashi understands the statement to refer back to the case in which the slaughtered animal was prepared, etc.: even after burial, when mourning begins, ornaments are not withheld from the bride for thirty days, so that she not become repulsive; all the more so after thirty days, when the mourning is lighter. According to this, the phrase “ornaments are not withheld for thirty days” means thirty days from the burial. Magen Avraham therefore asks: What proof can be brought from there that she is called a bride only until thirty days? On the contrary, there ornaments are permitted even after thirty days. I saw in a Shulchan Aruch with Baer Heitev that it writes regarding Magen Avraham: See Mordechai in Moed Katan, who raises this question according to Rashi and answers it; see there. Mordechai, Moed Katan, siman 524, writes: “We learned in the first chapter of Kesubos: If one had prepared the slaughtered animal, etc. Rashi explains: all the more so after thirty days. ‘Ornaments’ implies even removal of hair, as taught in the first chapter of Moed Katan: A woman may perform her adornments on Chol HaMoed, etc., braid her hair, apply cosmetics, remove her hair and nails. One may ask from Yoma, etc., where it explains: Until when is she a bride? As we learned, ornaments are not withheld, etc. This implies that until thirty days she is permitted, but afterward she is no longer called a bride and is like all other women. One may answer that this applies regarding Yom Kippur. Regarding mourning, however, since she is permitted during the first thirty days, which are the principal days of mourning, all the more so is she permitted after thirty days. This is how Rashi may be explained.” End quote. Further clarification is still needed. Although Mordechai resolves the contradiction from Kesubos, whence did Rav Chiya know that specifically until thirty days she is called a bride and no longer? Perhaps Rav Chiya regarded it as obvious that after thirty days she is like all other women and no leniency was granted to her on Yom Kippur. He needed proof only that at least until thirty days she is called a bride and there is concern that she not become unattractive. This likewise appears from Rashi in Yoma, who concludes: “This proves that she requires adornment throughout the thirty days.” His proof concerns permission throughout the first thirty days. He therefore properly cites evidence from mourning. Although mourning is lighter, as she is permitted even after thirty days, this is because mourning itself is then lighter. During the first thirty days, which are the principal period of mourning, they nevertheless were lenient for her, proving that she requires greater adornment. Thus, although mourning is D’Rabbanan, it can teach the law of Yom Kippur, because whatever the Sages instituted, they instituted in the pattern of Torah law.
