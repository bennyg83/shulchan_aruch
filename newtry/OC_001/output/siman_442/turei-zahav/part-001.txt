**** ENGLISH ****
<b>A mixture of chametz.</b> The Tur wrote that Rabbi Eliezer makes a chametz mixture subject to a negative commandment but not kareis, and according to him it is forbidden to keep on Pesach; according to the Sages it has no negative commandment, only a lesser prohibition, and according to them it may be kept. The Beis Yosef understood that even according to the Sages there is a Torah prohibition in eating, and therefore wondered why the Tur permits keeping it. But the Tur's words later show that he means only a rabbinic prohibition: the Rif rules like the Sages that there is no negative commandment in mixtures or in chametz nukshah, and therefore it may be mixed and kept. Thus the Beis Yosef's difficulty is not correct. The Taz explains that the main dispute is whether keeping a mixture follows eating. If eating is not forbidden by Torah law, the Tur holds that keeping is permitted, whereas Tosafos require destruction rabbinically because the Sages did not distinguish in chametz and because one may come to eat it. The Mishnah's statement that 'these transgress' can be understood either as removal from the table or as actual bal yera'eh, depending on the view. The Rambam's formulation indicates that for bal yera'eh he rules stringently in mixtures, while for eating the liability depends on the usual eating measures. The distinction also depends on whether the mixture has actual chametz taste. If the chametz was inserted only to sour or prepare the mixture and there is no taste sufficient to create liability, it is less severe; but if the taste of chametz is present, taste is like substance in unlike mixtures and the mixture is forbidden. A thing intentionally made in that manner is not treated like an accidental mixture that becomes nullified, as the Rashba writes regarding drinks made from vinegar and honey and as is seen from Babylonian kutach. Even if according to the Torah the mixture might be permitted without a kezayis within the time of eating a peras, rabbinically it may still be forbidden and may require destruction. The Taz further analyzes chametz nukshah: it is not full chametz, such as scribes' paste or dough that has begun but not completed leavening. If it was once full edible chametz, it would remain forbidden until unfit for a dog; therefore the cases of chametz nukshah are either never fully edible or only somewhat fit. Nonetheless the Sages were concerned one may come to eat it. He also distinguishes keeping before Pesach, mixing during the permitted time, and the penalty after Pesach: where one did not transgress bal yera'eh, the item is not forbidden after Pesach as a penalty, though eating may remain forbidden. The conclusion is that the Mechaber follows the stricter approach for mixtures of chametz, while the Tur's leniency applies only according to the view that the mixture is not Torah-prohibited and only before Pesach or for keeping where no bal yera'eh applies.

<b>And likewise papers, etc.</b> The Rambam's ruling is difficult from the Mishnah's prohibition of scribes' paste. The Ravyah, cited by the Tur, distinguishes between the scribes' paste itself, where the chametz remains recognizable, and our case where parchment or paper is pasted onto a board and the chametz is no longer recognizable. The Maggid Mishneh explains that before the papers are pasted the chametz remains as a hard substance and is forbidden to keep, but once pasted, the form of chametz is gone, similar to hides placed in a vat. The Beis Yosef therefore says that the Ravyah's words are unnecessary. In my humble opinion, the Ravyah's distinction is difficult, for if it is chametz, hiding it beneath paper should not help, since bal yera'eh applies even to hidden chametz. Terumas HaDeshen therefore required that one who pastes windows within thirty days of Pesach should use very soft dough and press it fully between the joints so that nothing protrudes; a stringent person should plaster it with clay. This is the stringency cited by the Rema. But where the dough is made so thin that after drying no substance remains, there is no difference between what is visible outside and what is inside, and this is the correct practice.

<b>And likewise if he set cheeses, etc.</b> Since the forbidden matter acts as the setting agent, it is considered as though it is present in its own substance. Therefore if one set mead, honey-water, with beer yeast, all the mead is forbidden on Pesach. This is comparable to Yoreh De'ah siman 123, where a whole dough is forbidden when set by forbidden matter. If it was set also with non-chametz yeast, it may be a case of this and this causing and be permitted, as in Yoreh De'ah siman 87 regarding stomach lining. One might ask that in Yoreh De'ah 87, cheese set with kosher stomach lining is nullified in sixty, so why should it not be nullified before Pesach? The answer is that chametz is more severe, for the power of the setting agent continues into Pesach, and regarding a setting agent we say that the prohibition reawakens. Regarding mead made during the year, it appears to be complete chametz because it is cooked in kettles used for beer and because it is often fermented with beer yeast. If the kettle was not used within twenty-four hours, its taste may be impaired and permitted before Pesach, but there remains concern that the yeast itself came from beer. Therefore one who prepares mead for Pesach must use yeast that is not chametz, and must kasher or properly prepare the vessel. The custom is to be careful about this. Some are lenient on the last day of Yom Tov with year-round mead, based on the Rema's leniency for foods cooked in chametz vessels, but that leniency depends on doubt whether the vessel was ben yomo. Where there is concern that it was fermented with beer yeast, or cooked in vessels used for sharp distilled spirits that improve the taste, one should not be lenient even on the last day, and certainly should not buy mead from a non-Jew then, lest beer was mixed into it.
