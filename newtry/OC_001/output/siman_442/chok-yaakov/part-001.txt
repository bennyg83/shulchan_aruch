<b>A mixture containing chametz.</b> The agreement of the later authorities is in accordance with those poskim that even if there is not an olive's bulk within the time of eating a half-loaf, one violates the prohibitions of chametz being seen and found. This applies specifically to a mixture of one type with another, which according to those who hold that taste is primary according to the Torah is not nullified according to the Torah by a majority. But with the same type mixed into the same type, since according to the Torah it is nullified by a majority and one may eat it, certainly according to the Torah one may keep it. D'Rabbanan, however, it is forbidden to keep it, lest he come to eat from it on Pesach, and he must destroy it. Nevertheless, if he transgressed and kept it, it is permitted after Pesach, since he did not violate the prohibitions of chametz being seen and found, as is explained below in simanim 447 and 466. The same law applies where there is sixty even in a mixture of one type with another. Where there is no actual substance of chametz, but only chametz taste, the Olas Shabbos also wrote that one violates the prohibitions of chametz being seen and found. In my view, this law requires great doubt, for the implication of the poskim is not so. So too wrote the Minchas Kohen in Sefer HaTaaruvos. It appears that even according to the view that taste is primary according to the Torah, even without substance, that is only regarding eating, which is derived from the law of nazirite soaking. But regarding violation of the prohibition of chametz being seen, since there is no form of chametz here at all, this is not comparable, even though Beis Hillel derives destruction from eating at the beginning of Beitzah. They are not equated for every matter. See the responsum of the Rashba, siman 30, where the questioner wanted to permit such a case by sixty even for eating. This is also clear from Rav Natronai, cited by the Tur in this siman, and so it appears from the responsa of Maharit. When the Tur writes that if it has the taste of chametz one is liable for it, because taste is primary according to the Torah, that liability refers to eating, not to keeping it. Alternatively, it refers to all those items in which the body of the chametz is mixed but there is not an olive's bulk within the time of eating a half-loaf, unlike what the Bach wrote. The Bach and Rosh Yosef wrote similarly, and the words of the Beit Yosef are difficult. Greater than this, the Olas Shabbos wrote that even if it was cooked in a vessel used that day, one should be stringent not to keep it. I do not know the source of this stringency. On the contrary, the Agudah at the beginning of Pesachim explicitly wrote that because of absorption from vessels it does not require destruction. This is especially so since many poskim hold that one does not violate the prohibitions of chametz being seen and found with any mixture of chametz, and even according to Rashi and those with him, taste is primary is not according to the Torah. Some also say that before Pesach, when it is still permitted, vessel absorption is considered a secondary taste of a secondary taste. All the more so according to what is explained below in siman 447, according to Rashi and Tosafot in the chapter Kol Sha'ah, that absorption from vessels is considered only a minute prohibition that was nullified before Pesach. Therefore even if we say that taste without substance entails violation of the prohibitions of chametz being seen and found, with vessel absorption one should not be stringent at all, and it is permitted to keep it. Where its taste remains but not its substance, it nevertheless appears to me that it is forbidden to keep it D'Rabbanan and it must be destroyed if found during Pesach. But if one transgressed and kept it, one should not forbid it, and at least it should be permitted for benefit. The prohibition of eating in this case is only a mere stringency. The same law applies to all cases that according to the Torah are permitted to keep but D'Rabbanan are forbidden to keep initially: he should burn them during Pesach D'Rabbanan, but if he transgressed and kept them, they are permitted, as is explained below in siman 447. So wrote the Magen Avraham. For this reason, the famous gaon, my wife's grandfather, ruled, as cited by my brother-in-law in Eliyah Zuta, siman 447, seif katan 21, regarding butter in which bread and flour had soaked and which one transgressed and kept during Pesach, that he should cast the benefit value of the bread or flour into the sea, and he permitted the butter for benefit. It appears to me that this speaks where the flour was fine flour, or where water was mixed with the butter, for otherwise fruit juice alone does not become chametz. Our beer, over which one violates the prohibitions of chametz being seen and found, as the Rosh wrote, is because its essence is barley, so it has both taste and substance, and it is also made for its taste, as the Rosh wrote in klal 2 regarding the prohibition of new grain. See there.

<b>עוברין עליו. </b> לשון הש"ע הוא כלשון הרמב"ם דפסק כר' אליעזר דעל תערובות חמץ בלאו ועל חמץ נוקשה בלא כלום לכן לא כת' גם כן דעוברין על חמץ נוקשה כסתם מתני' דפ' אלו עוברין וכן מבואר לקמן סי' תמ"ז סעיף י"א ולכתחלה מ"מ אסור להשהות ועיין מ"ש שם:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>One violates it.</b> The language of the Shulchan Aruch is like the language of the Rambam, who rules like Rabbi Eliezer, that for a mixture containing chametz there is a negative commandment, while for defective chametz there is no such liability. Therefore he also did not write that one violates regarding defective chametz, unlike the plain Mishnah in the chapter Eilu Ovrin. This is also explained below in siman 447, seif 11. Nevertheless, initially it is forbidden to keep it. See what I wrote there.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And Median beer.</b> All of them involve a mixture with water, for otherwise fruit juice does not cause leavening. So it is in the Yerushalmi, and so wrote the Rosh. Although water together with fruit juice produces defective chametz, Tosafot already noted this in the chapter Kol Sha'ah, Pesachim 35b, s.v. mei peiros, and answered that the wheat or barley is first soaked in water, which causes it to become chametz. See there. These Tosafot escaped the Rosh Yosef.

<b>And anything similar to these items.</b> This refers to Edomite vinegar, Egyptian olives, and all those listed in the Mishnah at the beginning of the chapter Eilu Ovrin. Brandy made from soured grain, called malt in the vernacular, was written in responsa Masas Binyamin, siman 58, to be actual chametz and worse than a mixture containing chametz. See there, where the matter is reasoned. So too wrote Rav Menachem of Tiktin in a responsum printed at the beginning of the Rif, Krakow edition, Seder Nashim. There is no distinction between brandy made from soured grain and brandy made from beer dregs, unlike what one scholar thought, whose question to me I will quote. He asked about Reuven, who had brandy made from the dregs of barley beer, forgot to sell it before Pesach, and remembered during Chol HaMoed. The brandy was in the house of a non-Jew, in a room that belonged to the Jew, and the loss was great. At first glance, he wrote, it appears simple that it is chametz that passed over Pesach and must be destroyed. The Mishnah lists Median beer among the items over which one violates on Pesach, and Rashi and Riva explain that this refers to the prohibitions of chametz being seen and found. The Rosh cited Rabbeinu Tam that Median beer is specifically where barley is placed in it, whereas our beer in Ashkenaz, whose essence is barley, is actual chametz and has an olive's bulk within the time of eating a half-loaf. So wrote the Tur and all the later authorities. The same should apply to brandy made from grain. Nevertheless, the questioner sought grounds for leniency, distinguishing between brandy made from grain and brandy made from dregs. Since dregs are merely acidity and not true wine, as is stated in Bava Basra regarding wine dregs, perhaps beer dregs are likewise mere acidity. He also argued that this is not a case of a fermenting agent, since the dregs did not cause another item to stand or ferment, but only produced an exudation. He added that the owner was inadvertent, had nullified his chametz, the item was not in a Jewish house, and there was great loss. He therefore inclined to permit benefit from it, provided another decisor agreed. My response is as follows. Although I do not presume to decide easily in the severe matter of chametz, I will not leave the question unanswered. First, the assumption that there is no prohibition of chametz being seen and found with a mixture containing chametz is not simple, for the Shulchan Aruch at the beginning of this siman rules explicitly that one violates a negative commandment over a mixture containing chametz even when there is not an olive's bulk within the time of eating a half-loaf, and this is the consensus of the later authorities. The Taz's difficulty on the Beit Yosef in siman 442 is not a difficulty, for the Mishnah itself states that these are in the warning of a prohibition, proving that it is not merely removal from the world. Furthermore, even if one were to require an olive's bulk within the time of eating a half-loaf for a mixture, brandy is not called a mixture containing chametz but actual chametz, for vapor that emerges from a liquid has the status of that liquid, as is proved from several Mishnayos in Machshirin and from the responsa of the Rosh and the Rivash. The Masas Binyamin and Rav Menachem of Tiktin wrote plainly that brandy made from chametz is actual chametz, not merely vapor, and is treated like the prohibited substance itself or like an agent that causes standing. As for distinguishing beer dregs from wine dregs, this comparison has no basis. Sense and reason testify that wine dregs whose wine has been removed may be only acidity, but beer dregs still contain the strength and substance of the grain and make a stronger drink when boiled and distilled. The proofs from wine dregs therefore do not apply to beer dregs. Nor does the fact that there is no intent to use them as a fermenting agent help, for the product itself comes from the chametz dregs and is made for taste and strength. The additional arguments also do not suffice. Forgetfulness after Pesach does not remove the fine of chametz that passed over Pesach where the chametz was owned by a Jew. Nullification does not help once the Sages required removal and sale, especially for a significant item that a person intends to preserve, and keeping it in a non-Jew's house does not help where it remains the Jew's property. Therefore the ruling is that brandy made from beer dregs is forbidden after Pesach as chametz that passed over Pesach, and certainly one should not rely on the proposed leniencies. The printed responsum of Rav Menachem of Tiktin that forbids both kinds is correct. Only where the case is of something permitted according to the Torah and forbidden only because of a D'Rabbanan concern may one discuss benefit after Pesach, as explained in the relevant simanim. But in this case, where the essence of the product is from chametz grain or its dregs and is made for taste and strength, it is treated as actual chametz. It follows that the gloss later in seif 4, beginning with the words 'and below,' also refers to all of these cases taught here.

<b>And likewise papers.</b> The Maggid Mishneh wrote that this is not the scribes' paste mentioned in the Gemara, which is forbidden to keep; that case is before the papers were pasted with it, when it is defective chametz by itself and forbidden to keep. Rather, the Rambam speaks after the papers were pasted with it, when it is like a kneading bowl into which hides were placed. The Tur cited another distinction in the name of the Raavyah: scribes' paste means that the paper itself is improved by it, like tanning, and the chametz is recognizable; but when we use it merely to paste parchment onto a board and it is not recognizable, it is permitted. Scribes' paste is defective chametz, and even when the chametz is recognizable one does not violate chametz being seen, as we concluded above that defective chametz by itself entails no such violation. Nevertheless, it is forbidden to keep it lest one come to eat from it. This concern applies only where the chametz is recognizable. But where parchment is pasted onto a board and the chametz is not recognizable, there is no concern that one will eat from it, since there is no prohibition of chametz being seen. This is clearly the Raavyah's intent. The Taz was troubled only because he understood that according to the Raavyah there is a prohibition of chametz being seen, but in truth this is not so.

<b>If he curdled cheeses with vinegar.</b> The same applies if they pour chametz, beer, or brandy into it in order to cause it to stand; it is also considered an agent that causes standing. In responsa Nachalas Shivah, siman 35, he also wrote that if one did not sell before Pesach cheese into which brandy had been poured, it should be forbidden because it is made for taste and is not nullified. So too ruled responsa Maharam Lublin regarding dough that became leavened with beer dregs that passed over Pesach. This is also the agreement of the later authorities in siman 447, seif 11. The same appears in the gloss of the Netz in this siman in the name of Rav Nosson Katz regarding mead that was set with beer dregs. So too wrote the Rashba in responsum 850, even if one set mead with mead dregs that had themselves already been set with beer dregs. See the Taz, who discussed at length how to conduct oneself with mead. The conclusion is that some are lenient to drink on the last day of Pesach mead from the whole year, as the Rama wrote in siman 447. The same applies to anything cooked in a chametz vessel, where one may be lenient on the last day of Yom Tov. It appears that this is because there is a doubt whether the vessel had been used that day, and we rule that ordinary vessels are presumed not to have been used that day, and on the last day of Yom Tov we say this. But if there is doubt whether it was fermented with beer dregs, there is no room to be lenient. Certainly in places where they cook mead all year in cauldrons used for making brandy, which is sharp and strong and gives a favorable taste to the mead, as is known, the fact that the vessel was not used that day does not help, and there is no doubt at all. Therefore one who guards his soul should distance himself from this even on the last day of Yom Tov, and all the more from buying mead from a non-Jew on the last day of Yom Tov, lest he mix beer into it. Although an agent that causes standing and something made for taste are not nullified only D'Rabbanan, as Tosafot in the chapter Kol HaZevachim and the Issur VeHeter wrote, nevertheless, since an agent that causes standing is like something present in its own substance, it is forbidden even after the fact if he kept it until after Pesach. It is possible that this is the view of the Tur. The Magen Avraham wrote that in the Tur's view an agent that causes standing is not nullified according to the Torah, but his words are not necessary. In Sefer Beis Hillel he wrote about a vessel designated for kneading dough, in which sourdough was placed and bread baked before Pesach; a little dough remained stuck to it, they forgot to clean it well and did not sell it, Pesach passed, and after Pesach they forgot and kneaded dough in it and baked bread. He answered that the bread is permitted for eating, since it is nullified in sixty, and if the stuck dough is less than sixty he should cast its value into the river. He further wrote that although sourdough is made for its taste and is not nullified even in a thousand, the dough nullifies the sourdough, citing Terumas HaDeshen. These words are very difficult. First, they contradict themselves, for initially he wrote to nullify in sixty and then wrote that it is nullified in a thousand. Second, his proof from Terumas HaDeshen is against the Gemara and all the poskim, for something that leavens or seasons forbids in any amount, as the Rambam wrote and as all early and later poskim agree, even by D'Rabbanan prohibitions. His proof from Terumas HaDeshen falls away, for that source only wrote that the dough is not nullified to the sourdough with respect to the obligation of challah, but certainly the sourdough itself is not nullified. However, if the sourdough did not have enough strength by itself to leaven, then it is nullified, as is explicit in Yoreh De'ah, siman 98, seif 1, and at the end of siman 87, that where this and that together cause the effect, it is permitted if the prohibition alone does not have enough strength to leaven. So too regarding items made for taste, they forbid only as long as their taste is sensed. Nevertheless, it appears to me that perhaps all these cases in which we say that something made for taste is not nullified, or that an agent that causes standing is not nullified, are no worse than a mixture of less than sixty that passed over Pesach, where it suffices to cast the benefit of the prohibited part into the Dead Sea, as explained below in siman 447, seif 11 in the gloss. However, from some later authorities that I cited it is clear that the entire item is forbidden, because it is like the prohibited substance itself. Still, in a case of great loss, one may perhaps permit benefit from it by casting the benefit of the standing agent into the Dead Sea. The later authorities wrote in the name of responsa Radbaz that cheese set with a stomach that had absorbed from a chametz vessel is forbidden to eat but permitted to keep.

<b>או ששרפו באש </b> וכתב הרא"ש ואלמודי שעושין מורייס ומשימין בו לחם קלוי י"א שמותר אחר פסח שחמץ ע"י תערובת שעבר עליו הפסח מותר ועוד שהרי חרכו קודם זמנו ומותר בהנא' לאחר זמנו ויש מרבוות' אומרים דלא קי"ל הכי דלא שרי ר"ש אלא כגון דערוב שלא בכוונ' אבל לערובי ולשהוי' לא ומשום שחרכו נמי אימר לא נחרכו יפה יפה דלא אסיק אדעתיה פסח בשעת עשייתו עוד דלא אמרו חרכו קודם זמנו מותר באכילתו לאחר זמנו אלא מותר בהנא' קאמר דלאו אורחי' למיכל פת חרוך כל צרכו אבל גבי מורייס אורחיה למיכל הכי אסור עכ"ל הרא"ש ומתמיה המ"א על הטור שמשמע מדבריו שפסק כדעת היש אומרים ובאמת לדעת היש אומרים שכתב הרא"ש אסור אפילו בהנא' ולמה פסק הטור דאסור באכילה והניח בתימה ולי יותר תימא לדעתיה בכוונת הטור דפסק כי"א דאסור לערובי ולשהויי דהרי הטור דפסק להדיא להיפך בב' מקומות דלא כוותיה חדא שבסי' זה כתב להדיא שאין לאו בתערובת חמץ רק איסור דרבנן ולפ"ז מותר לערבו לכתחל' לפני פסח ולשהותה (אף שהטור סיים ודוק' שאין בתערובת טעם חמץ כגון אלו שאין נותנים בו אלא לקיוה' בעלמא אבל אם יש בו טעם חמץ מחויבין עליו דטעם כעיקר דאורייתא וכן אם יש בו כזית בכדי אכילת פרס מ"מ ע"כ צריך לומר דהאי מורייס לית ביה טעם כעיקר ולא כזית בכדא"פ דאל"כ אפילו נתערב ממילא ג"כ אסור לדעת הטור כמ"ש להדיא בס"ס תמ"ז) ועוד שבס"ס תמ"ז חולק הטור להדיא אמאן דפסק דאסור לערב לכתחלה כדי להשהותה לכן נ"ל דבחדא מתרצה חבירתה דדעת הטור לפסוק כדעה ראשונה דמותר בהנאה ואסור לאכול וכן משמע מדבריו בקיצור פסקי הרא"ש ע"ש ריש פ' כל שעה אך קשה דלמא באמת לדעה ראשונה אסור באכילה דהא חמץ ע"י תערובת מותר אף באכילה לאחר פסח כמו שפסק הטור עצמו ס"ס תמ"ז ואפשר לומר דאף דעה ראשונה עצמו מסתפק אי מיקרי חמץ ע"י תערובת דלמ' יש בו טעם כעיקר או כזית בכדי אכילת פרס. והש"ע השמיט לגמרי דין זה ואפשר לפי שסובר הרא"ש וסייעתו אזלי לשיטתייהו שפסקו דתערובת חמץ אין עוברין בבל יראה. אכן המחבר בש"ע סתם בר"ס זה לשון הרמב"ם דמורייס הוא בכלל תערובת' חמץ דעוברין עליה בב"י וב"י וזה המורייס ג"כ בכלל והוי כשאר חמץ שעבר עליו פסח וע' מ"ש ר"ס זה כל הדינים אלו:

<b>Or if he burned it in fire.</b> The Rosh wrote regarding elmodi, used to make muries, into which toasted bread is placed, that some say it is permitted after Pesach because chametz through a mixture that passed over Pesach is permitted, and furthermore it was charred before its time and is permitted for benefit after its time. But some great authorities say that we do not rule this way, for Rabbi Shimon permitted only a mixture that occurred unintentionally, but not to mix it deliberately and keep it. Also, because it was charred one cannot rely on it, for perhaps it was not charred thoroughly, since Pesach was not in his mind when he made it. Furthermore, when they said that if one charred it before its time it is permitted after its time, they meant permitted for benefit, because fully charred bread is not normally eaten. But with muries, where this is the normal way to eat it, it is forbidden. The Magen Avraham wondered at the Tur, whose words imply that he ruled like the stringent view, whereas according to that view it should be forbidden even for benefit. He left it difficult. In my view, the greater difficulty is with his understanding of the Tur, for the Tur rules explicitly in two places against the view that one may not mix and keep it. First, in this siman the Tur writes explicitly that a mixture containing chametz entails no negative commandment, only a D'Rabbanan prohibition, and according to this it is permitted initially to mix it before Pesach in order to keep it, provided there is no chametz taste and no olive's bulk within the time of eating a half-loaf. Second, at the end of siman 447 the Tur explicitly disagrees with the one who forbids mixing initially in order to keep it. Therefore it appears to me that one resolves the other: the Tur's view is to rule like the first opinion, that it is permitted for benefit but forbidden to eat. This is also implied in his abridged rulings on the Rosh at the beginning of the chapter Kol Sha'ah. One might ask that according to the first opinion it should be permitted even for eating, for chametz through a mixture is permitted after Pesach, as the Tur himself rules at the end of siman 447. Perhaps the first opinion itself was uncertain whether this is called chametz through a mixture, or perhaps it has taste that is primary or an olive's bulk within the time of eating a half-loaf. The Shulchan Aruch entirely omitted this law, perhaps because it holds that the Rosh and those with him follow their view that one does not violate chametz being seen with a chametz mixture. But the Mechaber at the beginning of this siman states without qualification the Rambam's language that muries is included in chametz mixtures over which one violates the prohibitions of chametz being seen and found, and this muries is also included. It is therefore like other chametz that passed over Pesach. See what I wrote at the beginning of this siman regarding all these laws.

<b>מותר לכתוב בו </b> וכ' שם בת"ה סי' קנ"ט ולא חיישינן שיתן קולמסו לתוך פיו וחמץ שנפסל מאכילת כלב אסור באכילה עכ"פ כיון שנותן שלא בכוונה ליכא קפידא דדוקא לאכלו בכוונה אסור דאחשביה לאכילה עכ"ל ונ"ל לפ"ז פשוט דאם נפל דיו או שאר חמץ שנפסל מאכילת כלב לתוך המאכל אפי' ברובא בעלמא בטל כיון דעפרא בעלמא הוא ולא אחשביה באכילתו בזה כי אין כוונתו רק לאכול המאכל וזו פגום לגמרי הוא מעיקרו וע"ל סי' תמ"ז. נראה דה"ה לדיו שמדפיסין בו שנעשית ע"י פתיתי חמץ ופגום מאוד הוא ודיו שבישל עכו"ם בפסח אסור לכתוב בו כיון שלא נפסל מאכילת לכלב קודם זמנו כ"כ בת"ה שם וע' בי"ד סי' קל"ד:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>It is permitted to write with it.</b> Terumas HaDeshen, siman 159, wrote that we are not concerned lest he put his pen into his mouth. Although chametz that became unfit for a dog to eat is still forbidden to eat, since he puts it in unintentionally there is no concern; only intentional eating is forbidden, because by doing so he gives it significance as food. According to this, it appears simple that if ink or other chametz that became unfit for a dog to eat fell into food, it is nullified even by a mere majority, since it is like dust and he does not give it significance by eating it, for his intention is only to eat the food, and this is fundamentally entirely spoiled. See below, siman 447. It appears that the same applies to printing ink made with chametz crumbs, which is very spoiled. Ink cooked by a non-Jew on Pesach is forbidden for writing, since it did not become unfit for a dog to eat before its time. So wrote Terumas HaDeshen there. See Yoreh De'ah, siman 134.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Because it is impossible to clean them out.</b> At first glance this implies that specifically washing with hot water and picking them clean do not help, but scraping would help. This could also resolve the Taz's difficulty about an apparent contradiction in the Tur. However, from the end of the Tur's wording, where he concludes like the Mechaber that one must give them as a gift to a non-Jew and does not mention a remedy of scraping, it somewhat implies that scraping does not help. So too it is explained below in siman 451 regarding hagalah. See the Taz. The Magen Avraham distinguishes between kneading troughs in which bread is kneaded, which have cracks between board and board, and small kneading troughs made from one piece with no crack at all, which certainly can be cleaned out. Mahari Weil, siman 123, and Maharil in the laws of Pesach wrote that the custom is to sell to a non-Jew, or to smear with clay, boards used for filled foods, rolling pins, and all utensils used with dough. Mahari Weil concludes that one should hide them in a room that is not needed on Pesach. The Magen Avraham wrote that one should not tell the non-Jew this condition, but should give them to him as a complete gift, and after Pesach take them back from him. See siman 478.
