**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Se'if 1.</b> A mixture of chametz is transgressed, etc. Note: Regarding the rule that permitted material combines with forbidden material, such as where a minority of permitted substance became mixed with a majority of forbidden substance, whether one is lashed for eating a kezayis made up of the permitted and forbidden together, see what I wrote in my novellae to Niddah 27b, on the question whether two clods of earth could combine to a clod with a large measure. There I proved that even the permitted minority joins the majority to convey impurity to the house, and the same applies to prohibitions. In that sugya the questioner and answerer dispute this; see there carefully. See also the language of the Beis Yosef in this siman, who cites the Maggid Mishneh on the Tur and says that the wording of the book seems to contradict itself from one passage to the other. Before answering, the wording itself is not understandable and the answer as printed is also difficult. It appears that there is a printing error: first it should say, 'Before I answer, I will introduce that the reason the author distinguished,' etc. With this introduction he resolves the first difficulty: although the Tur first brought that keeping is one level lighter than eating, he afterward brings the Rambam's language that one transgresses bal yera'eh on mixtures. At first the Tur is following the commentators who explain 'these transgress' to mean they must be removed from the table, and according to them one may say that keeping is lighter than eating in one respect. But the Rambam explains that 'these transgress' refers to bal yera'eh, and therefore he holds that destruction and eating are exactly parallel; the first wonder is removed. Afterward the text must continue that what may be said to permit the contradiction is the second point: the Rambam ruled like the Sages regarding eating but like Rabbi Eliezer regarding bal yera'eh, so according to him destruction is stricter than eating. This follows the Ramach's explanation: therefore, for bal yera'eh one transgresses even when there is no kezayis within the time of eating a peras, while for eating one is not lashed unless there is such a measure. With this the two contradictions he mentioned are resolved. This appears to be the intent of the Maggid Mishneh; otherwise his words are unintelligible. See Magen Avraham s.k. 1, who wrote that in a mixture of chametz the Kesef Mishneh agreed with the second view. The Beis Yosef explains the matter further: regarding bal yera'eh, the Rambam ruled like Rabbi Eliezer because the anonymous Mishnah follows him, since at least the chametz is in his house; but regarding eating he ruled like the Sages. I was asked: if so, why did the Gemara need to say that the Mishnah follows Rabbi Eliezer? It could be even according to the Sages, for they disagree only regarding eating and concede regarding destruction. I answered that although the beginning of the Mishnah may be explained as referring to bal yera'eh, as Rashi explains, the end which says, 'these are under a warning and there is no kareis for them,' necessarily speaks of eating, as Rashi himself explains. If it referred to bal yera'eh, what novelty is there that there is no kareis, since even actual chametz has no kareis for bal yera'eh? Therefore the end proves that the Mishnah follows Rabbi Eliezer regarding eating as well. Only the beginning can be considered anonymous; the end may be part of Rabbi Eliezer's statement and is not anonymous. Therefore the Rambam could rule like the Sages for eating. Regarding the Magen Avraham's citation of the Rashba that anything made intentionally in that way is not nullified, see what I wrote in my Orach Chayim work from 5601, in the Pesachim sugya 'one may not cook the Pesach offering in water,' where I raised many difficulties with it. I also wrote on it in my later responsum to the community of Stanislav, printed in the omissions to Sefer HaChayim in the pamphlet Minei Mat'amim. See there. The Magen Avraham quotes the Maggid Mishneh that only where there is a kezayis of chametz within the time of eating a peras is one liable, and this is primary; some say that even if one does not transgress by eating it, he must destroy it because there is a kezayis of chametz there, and the Kesef Mishneh agreed. In my opinion the Maggid Mishneh's view can be decided from the Yerushalmi, Beitzah chapter 1, halachah 2, on the Mishnah that Beis Shammai say the measure of se'or is a kezayis. Rabbi Abahu said in the name of Rabbi Yochanan that for both destruction and eating the measure is a kezayis. Rabbi Mana asked Rabbi Chizkiyah whether he heard this from Rav, and he answered that he heard it from Rabbi Abahu; he too said in the name of Rabbi Yochanan that both for destruction and eating the measure is a kezayis, for otherwise the Torah should have taught thirty-seven kareis liabilities. This Yerushalmi is like a sealed book, and Korban HaEidah emended it, but in my view the printed reading should stand. The explanation is that Beis Hillel hold that for se'or the measure is a koseves and for chametz a kezayis. Their reasoning is not a mere analogy from eating to destruction; rather, since all eating measures are a kezayis through halachah leMoshe miSinai, and where the Torah does not write eating one might have thought the measure is even smaller, we learn leniently from eating that destruction too needs a measure, and the principle of 'it is enough for the derived law to be like the source' makes it a kezayis. This is precisely the dispute between the Maggid Mishneh and the Beis Meir: if we learn destruction from eating by a simple 'what we find' analogy, one could say that eating is limited by the time of eating a peras but destruction is not, because the chametz is ultimately in his house. But if it is learned as a kal vachomer, then the derived law cannot be stricter than its source, and just as eating requires a kezayis within the time of eating a peras, so too destruction. The Yerushalmi asks: if we learn destruction from eating, perhaps we should also learn kareis for bal yera'eh. The answer is that the Mishnah itself says these are under a warning but have no kareis. Yet this alone would not prove the point, for one might say that the Mishnah deals where there is no kezayis within the time of eating a peras. Rather, the ending forces us to read the Mishnah as Rabbi Eliezer regarding eating, and then the beginning is anonymous only in part. The matter is clear in my humble opinion. The later discussion of se'or and chametz measures likewise supports that the comparison of destruction to eating is not open-ended; the derived measure is limited to what eating itself would obligate. Therefore, where eating would not be liable because the kezayis is not within the time of eating a peras, destruction according to this analysis is not stricter.

<b>And likewise papers, etc.</b> The Rambam ruled this way, and one may ask: from the paste of scribes the Mishnah forbids. The Ravyah, brought by the Tur, answers that the paste of scribes means the paper itself was processed with chametz in a recognizable form, but where we paste parchment onto a board and the chametz is not recognizable, it is permitted. The Maggid Mishneh explained differently: the paste of scribes refers to before the papers were pasted, when the chametz remained a hard substance in itself and was forbidden to keep; the Rambam speaks after the papers were pasted, similar to a vat in which hides were placed, where he says the form of chametz no longer remains. It is clear that after pasting it is permitted to keep. The Beis Yosef therefore wrote that we do not need the Ravyah's words. In my humble opinion, the Ravyah's distinction is surprising: if there is a chametz prohibition, what does covering it with paper help, since bal yera'eh applies even to chametz hidden in a pit where one does not see it? Terumas HaDeshen, siman 117, therefore wrote that one who wishes to paste windows within thirty days before Pesach should paste with very soft dough and insert all the dough between the joints so that none protrudes outward; this is the Rambam's case, where the form of chametz does not remain. One who is stringent should plaster it with clay. This is the stringency brought here by the Rema, following the Ravyah, but it is difficult for the reason I wrote. Also, even with garments laundered many times in starch-water, some substance may appear somewhat on the outside, yet it certainly does not forbid, because no chametz remains there at all, as the Maggid Mishneh explained. Therefore one who wishes to be stringent should remove even what the paper covers, for there is no distinction. What helps in se'if 6, plastering with clay, is only a stringency, because even without plastering it is permitted there since one cannot dig after it and it is like chametz covered by a collapse. But here, where one is trying to repair the prohibition through plastering, that is not reasonable unless the plaster itself nullifies the form and taste of the chametz. Regarding paper pasted to windows, one should follow Terumas HaDeshen's first instruction: cover it with extremely thin dough so that after drying there is no substance at all; there is no distinction between what is visible outside and what is inside.

<b>And below it will be explained, etc.</b> In my humble opinion, even according to those who disagree, one should forbid eating this theriac, lest the chametz in it is what sets it, like the case of setting cheeses nearby, where nullification before Pesach does not help. <b>And likewise if he set cheeses, etc.</b> The reason is that since it is the setting agent, a thing that sets is considered as if it is present in its own substance. Therefore, if he set honey-water called mead with beer yeast, all the mead is forbidden on Pesach, just as with wine of non-Jews in Yoreh De'ah siman 123, se'if 15, where the whole dough is forbidden. If he also set it with yeast that is not chametz, this is a case of this and this causing, and is permitted according to the law stated in Yoreh De'ah siman 87 regarding the stomach lining. But it is difficult: in Yoreh De'ah siman 87 we say that if one set cheese with a kosher stomach lining, it is nullified in sixty; why not say the same here, that before Pesach it is nullified in sixty? One must say that chametz is stricter here: because the power of the setting agent is active during Pesach, we say that it reawakens, even though with ordinary chametz we do not always say it reawakens. Regarding our mead during the rest of the year, it appears to be complete chametz for two reasons. First, it is cooked in a kettle in which beer is cooked; if the beer cooking was within twenty-four hours, the mead absorbs chametz taste, though if it was not within twenty-four hours the taste is impaired and is permitted before the first night of Pesach. Second, it is fermented with beer yeast, which makes all the mead forbidden, just like a whole dough set by forbidden matter in Yoreh De'ah siman 123. Therefore anyone cooking mead for Pesach must be careful to ferment it with yeast that is not from chametz. Even yeast of mead from the rest of the year may itself be chametz, because that mead may have been fermented with beer yeast. If it is fermented in the special vessel called a kvasnik in Russian, which leavens all mead poured into it, the absorbed leavening power in that vessel came from earlier chametz mead. There is some leniency regarding the kettle where there is doubt whether the beer was cooked within twenty-four hours, because we generally say that ordinary utensils are not assumed to have been used within twenty-four hours, even Jewish utensils, because of a double doubt, and impaired taste is permitted after the fact. One might therefore permit if he fermented the mead with yeast from ordinary mead. But in siman 447, se'if 5, it will be explained that regarding chametz we do not necessarily say that ordinary utensils are not ben yomo. More importantly, the second concern remains: ordinary mead may sometimes have been fermented with beer yeast, and then all its yeast is chametz. Since this is a Torah doubt, one should be stringent even on the last day of Yom Tov. The custom is already to be careful not to ferment Pesach mead with year-round mead, and to use a kashered utensil, or to fill it three times, each time for a full day, with fresh water, as in Yoreh De'ah siman 135, se'if 12, regarding wine vessels of non-Jews. Some are lenient to drink year-round mead on the last day of Yom Tov, as the Rema in siman 447, se'if 5, is lenient on the last day regarding foods cooked in chametz vessels. That leniency seems to rest on doubt whether the vessel was ben yomo, but where there is doubt that it was fermented with beer yeast there is no basis to be lenient, and certainly not where the mead is cooked in kettles used all year for distilled spirits, which are sharp and strong and improve the mead's taste, so the lack of ben yomo does not help. Therefore one who guards his soul should distance himself from it even on the last day of Yom Tov, and all the more so from buying mead from a non-Jew on the last day, lest he mixed beer into it.
