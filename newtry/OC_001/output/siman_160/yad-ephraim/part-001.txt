<b>In Taz, seif katan 10,</b> the correct reading is that initially it is forbidden to wash with wine. His words are astonishing. What he writes, that Rashba and the Shulchan Aruch in the second opinion rule like Rabbi Eliezer, is incorrect. On the contrary, they rule like the Sages, and therefore even before water has been added it is initially forbidden to wash with the wine, because according to the Sages it already independently requires the blessing Borei Peri HaGefen before water is added. As for what Taz cites from Rashba in Mishmeres HaBayis, one who examines that passage will see that Rashba is responding to Raah in Bedek HaBayis, who explained the prohibition as arising from wasting food. Rashba objects that, according to that reason, there would be no basis to distinguish between wine before and after water is added. He means this only within Rabbi Eliezer’s position. In truth, Raah’s text had Rabbi Eliezer permitting washing in both cases, whereas Rashba apparently did not have that reading. Rashba therefore writes that the proper explanation is that the concern is treating food disrespectfully. Accordingly, Rabbi Eliezer can indeed distinguish between the cases: after water has been added, it is forbidden to treat the wine disrespectfully because it independently requires a blessing. See there. But as practical halachah Rashba certainly rules like the Sages, and according to them the concern for disrespect applies both before and after water is added. It is therefore initially forbidden in either case. Taz’s concluding question, why there should be no concern for wasting food before water is added, is itself astonishing, because this is precisely Rashba’s question in Mishmeres HaBayis against Raah. Rashba therefore writes that the reason should instead be disrespect, and the distinction is then understandable. Likewise, what Taz writes in Maharshal’s name and then questions him is not correct. One who examines Maharshal will see that he had not seen Toras HaBayis and assumed that Rabbi Eliezer and the Sages certainly dispute the validity of the washing after the fact. He understood Rashba to rule like Rabbi Eliezer regarding that validity, and therefore asked why Rashba ruled like Rabbi Eliezer. Maharshal answered that the Sages too agree that the washing is valid, and their dispute concerns only wasting food. Taz correctly notes that Maharshal had not seen Mishmeres HaBayis. Nevertheless, Maharshal accurately understood Rashba’s practical view, that there is no difference between before and after water is added. Only his phrase “because of wasting food” does not reflect Rashba’s precise intent, which is disrespect rather than waste. This distinction is necessary to resolve the difficulty within Rabbi Eliezer’s position. See there. However, so that Taz’s words should not be like an unreadable letter, it appears to me that his understanding was as follows. Taz assumed that Rashba’s reason, that one may not treat disrespectfully an important item that independently requires a blessing, does not fit the position of the Sages. If so, how can the Gemara say that Shmuel’s ruling, that one may use bread for all his needs, follows Rabbi Eliezer? According to Rashba, Rabbi Eliezer and the Sages both speak only of disrespecting food. They differ merely over whether wine before dilution has already changed for the better. If bread independently requires a blessing, Shmuel would not accord even with Rabbi Eliezer, for Rabbi Eliezer also prohibits washing after water has been added because the wine then requires its own blessing. One would therefore need to distinguish between a use that makes the food repulsive and one that does not. But if so, Shmuel’s ruling could also accord with the Sages. Rather, even according to the Sages, although one recites Borei Peri HaGefen before adding water, the Gemara says that the Sages agree that for a cup of blessing one should not recite the blessing until water is added, as a more optimal fulfillment of the mitzvah. It also states that even according to the Sages, undiluted wine is fit only for a medicinal mixture, such as karyati, meaning spiced wine or aluntis, but is not fit for ordinary drinking. Thus, even according to the Sages, although one who drinks it recites Borei Peri HaGefen, it is not considered to have changed for the better with respect to disrespect, since it is unfit for drinking and unfit for a cup of blessing. For that reason it would be proper to permit washing with it. The Sages nevertheless prohibit it because of wasting food, since even before dilution it has potential value once water is added. According to this, the Gemara’s statement that Shmuel follows Rabbi Eliezer must be understood as follows. Rashba’s explanation implies that Rabbi Eliezer too forbids the use only where there is disrespect to an item that requires its own blessing. Bread also requires its own blessing, so why does Shmuel permit its use? One must distinguish between something that becomes repulsive and something that does not. But if so, perhaps the Sages too would agree with Shmuel. Tosafos ask this very question. According to Rashba one cannot answer, as Tosafos do, that Rabbi Eliezer’s position is like Shmuel’s and even broader, permitting use even where the food becomes repulsive, because Rabbi Eliezer forbids washing with diluted wine. Rather, one must say that if the prohibition is due to disrespect, it applies specifically where an item that independently requires a blessing is made repulsive. When it is not made repulsive, there is no disrespect. If the prohibition is due to wasting food, however, there is no distinction between an item that independently requires a blessing and one that does not, or between making it repulsive and not making it repulsive. Even if one burns it or otherwise destroys it without making it repulsive, it is forbidden. The Gemara therefore says that Shmuel, who permits all uses of bread and thus apparently permits even a use involving some loss, follows Rabbi Eliezer, who is concerned only with disrespect, not with waste. With bread that does not become repulsive, there is no disrespect. Shmuel must be discussing a case involving some loss, for if there were neither loss nor repulsiveness, his ruling would teach nothing. According to the Sages, however, who are concerned for loss even without repulsiveness, such use would be forbidden and Shmuel would not accord with them. This is how Taz understood the passage according to his emendation and Rashba’s explanation. Accordingly, once Rashba adopts the reason of disrespecting something that independently requires a blessing, it must be that he rules like Rabbi Eliezer, who distinguishes between wine before and after water is added, as Rashba writes in Mishmeres HaBayis. Taz understood that Rashba wrote this there as practical halachah. Rashba’s statement that “it is reasonable according to both views” refers only to the end of the matter, namely that according to both positions the prohibition applies only initially, while after the fact the washing is valid. Their dispute is not about the validity of washing but whether the concern is disrespect or wasting food. Because Rashba appeared to rule like Rabbi Eliezer, who distinguishes between wine before and after dilution, he chose the reason of disrespect rather than waste. The phrase is not exact for both views; rather, Rabbi Eliezer follows his reason and the Sages follow theirs, and Rashba mentioned the reason of disrespect because he viewed Rabbi Eliezer’s position as halachah. Taz therefore asks why Rashba ruled like Rabbi Eliezer. One cannot answer that Rashba agrees with Shmuel, for below in siman 171 we do not rule like Shmuel where the food becomes repulsive, and Rashba himself rules likewise. His rulings would therefore contradict one another. Taz means that there we rule that anything made repulsive is forbidden even if it does not independently require a blessing. The reason must be that once it is made repulsive, there is a loss of food. This is unlike Rabbi Eliezer, who, on Taz’s understanding, forbids only where the item independently requires a blessing and the concern is disrespect, not loss at all. Taz resolves this by saying that Rashba mentioned the reason of disrespect only to explain Rabbi Eliezer. Even according to Rabbi Eliezer, the concern is not the validity of the washing but the loss of the beverage. To explain his distinction between wine before and after dilution, Rashba writes that Rabbi Eliezer is not concerned with loss as such, but only with disrespect to something that independently requires a blessing. According to the Sages, however, there is no distinction and initially every case is forbidden because of wasting the beverage. But the Shulchan Aruch records the reason of disrespecting an important item that independently requires a blessing as practical law. Its phrase, “except that initially it is forbidden to do so,” therefore refers only to wine after water has been added. This requires study, for why did the Shulchan Aruch not rule like the Sages and prohibit every case because of wasting food where the liquid becomes repulsive, even if it does not independently require a blessing, as ruled below in siman 171? Taz then questions Maharshal, who writes that Rashba in fact rules like the Sages because of wasting food. Taz objects that Rashba gave the reason of disrespecting an item that independently requires a blessing, and this applies specifically after water is added, since before dilution the wine is not regarded as independently requiring the blessing in the full sense explained above, even according to the Sages. Taz therefore writes that Maharshal had not seen Toras HaBayis or Mishmeres HaBayis and consequently understood Rashba to prohibit all wine, even before water is added, because of wasting food. Taz finally adds that, nevertheless, Rashba’s own explanation of Rabbi Eliezer remains difficult. If the distinction turns on independent blessing status and concern for loss or disrespect, why should there be no concern before water is added? Even if the wine is not presently fit for Borei Peri HaGefen in the optimal sense, water can be added without impairing its proper mixture. Why, then, should Rabbi Eliezer disagree with the Sages? According to Rashi, however, the matter is clear, because before water is added the wine itself is valid for washing.
