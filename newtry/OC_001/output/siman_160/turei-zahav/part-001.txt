<b>Except with water.</b> In the third chapter of Berachos it is taught: The Sages taught that, before water has been added to wine, one does not recite Borei Peri HaGefen over it but Borei Peri HaEtz, and one may wash his hands with it. After water has been added, one recites Borei Peri HaGefen over it and may not wash his hands with it. This is the view of Rabbi Eliezer. The Sages say that in either case one recites Borei Peri HaGefen over it and may not wash his hands with it. Terumas HaDeshen writes: “It is reasonable that, according to both views, they said that one may not wash his hands with it only because it is forbidden to use wine and treat it disrespectfully once it has attained its elevated status and is fit for the blessing Borei Peri HaGefen. This applies specifically initially, but after the fact, in either case, the washing is valid. Rashi, however, explains that before water is added it has not yet changed for the better and is regarded merely as fruit juice; once water is added it acquires the status of wine, and washing is valid only with water. The first explanation appears primary. Raavad explains that the statement that one may not wash with it does not refer to washing that prepares one for eating, for that washing requires water, but rather to washing for cleanliness. Yet in the laws of handwashing that he composed, he explains it as referring to washing for eating, and this is primary.” The Gemara further states: In accordance with whom is Shmuel’s ruling that a person may use bread for all his needs? In accordance with Rabbi Eliezer. Now, the first opinion cited here, that washing is valid only with water, is Rashi’s view, which follows the Sages. Yet I am perplexed by what is written afterward in the second opinion, which is Rashba’s view, that initially it is forbidden to wash with wine because it is important enough to require its own blessing. This refers to wine after water has been added, as Rashba explicitly writes in Mishmeres HaBayis, and thus follows Rabbi Eliezer, whereas according to the Sages one may not wash with it in either case, as Rashba himself writes there when explaining their position. Why, then, did he rule in accordance with Rabbi Eliezer? One cannot answer that he did so because Shmuel agrees with Rabbi Eliezer that one may use bread for his needs, for we do not rule like Shmuel regarding something that becomes repulsive, but rather like the Sages, as the Tur writes below in siman 171. Moreover, Beit Yosef writes there that Rashba also agrees with this. His rulings would therefore contradict one another. It appears to me that Rashba’s distinction concerning something that requires its own blessing was not written as a practical ruling, but only to explain Rabbi Eliezer’s position. His point is that there is no intrinsic invalidity in washing with wine according to anyone, and the prohibition is only because of wasting a beverage. To answer why Rabbi Eliezer distinguishes between wine before and after water is added, Rashba explains that after water is added it is fit for drinking and requires its own blessing, and one therefore may not wash with it. According to the Sages, however, we are concerned for wasting the beverage even before water is added, and this is the practical halachah. But I cannot reconcile the Shulchan Aruch, which records this distinction as law, and this requires extensive study. <b>I later</b> saw that Maharshal also asked why Rashba did not rule like the Sages. He answered that Rashba holds that the Sages disagree with Rabbi Eliezer only regarding wasting food, but agree that the washing itself is valid. This does not suffice, for concerning wasting food Rashba himself distinguishes that there is a prohibition only where the wine requires a blessing of its own, namely after water was added. Perhaps Maharshal did not understand Rashba’s statement that it requires its own blessing as referring specifically to wine after water was added, because he had not seen Mishmeres HaBayis. Even Terumas HaDeshen himself had not seen it, for in chapter Kol HaBasar he writes: “But Rabbi Yosef Karo wrote that he saw that Rashba wrote in Toras HaBayis,” and so forth, which shows that he himself had not seen it. Nevertheless, I am astonished why there should be no concern for wasting food before water is added. It can be made fit by adding water, without diminishing its proper mixture. Why, then, may it be wasted at that stage? Is pouring water into it such a great burden?
