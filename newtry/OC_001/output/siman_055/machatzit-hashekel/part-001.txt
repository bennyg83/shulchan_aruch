**** ENGLISH ****
The Tur writes in siman 236: The custom to interrupt with the verses of “May our eyes behold” and Kaddish arose because in earlier times their synagogues were in the fields, and they feared remaining there until after the time for Maariv. They therefore instituted the recital of these verses, which contain eighteen mentions of Hashem corresponding to the eighteen blessings of the Maariv Amidah, and they discharged their obligation through this Kaddish. The Tur likewise writes there in the name of Rav Natronai Gaon that the Kaddish marks the conclusion of the prayer, meaning that one who wishes to leave may leave. Now that they have returned to praying Maariv in the synagogue within the city, the original custom was not abolished. These are the words of the Tur. Accordingly, since we recite these verses and Kaddish only because of the earlier custom, and at that time the Kaddish before the Amidah belonged to the verses and Krias Shema, after which one who wished to leave would leave, even now this Kaddish remains connected to the verses and Krias Shema. Therefore, if some of the minyan left before the verses and Kaddish, the Kaddish before the Amidah may still be recited.

<b>ע"י חומש.</b> וב"ח כ' דאפי' למ"ד דמהני ע"י חומש בעי' שיהיה כתוב בגלילה כס"ת כמ"ש התו' משא"כ בחומשים שלנו. וא"כ היה צריך שיהא ס"ת ביד התינוק. אבל בס' א"ר בשם זקנו מהרר"ש כתב דאפי' בשעת הדחק לא רצה לסמוך ע"ז. וכ"כ בתשו' שב יעקב סי' ו'. וכ' דגם רמ"א אין כוונתו לסמוך ע"ז אע"ג שכ' יש נוהגים כו'. דהא בסי' קצ"ט ס"ו כ' הרב"י דקטן כו' מזמנין עליו ומצטרף בין לג' ובין ליו"ד. וכ' רמ"א וי"א דאיו מצטרפים כו' עד שיהיה בן י"ג כו' וכן נוהגים ואין לשנות עכ"ל. הרי דכ' רמ"א גופי' לענין ברכת המזון אין לצרפו בין לג' בין ליו"ד ואין לשנות. וא"ל דס"ל דתפלה קילא מבהמ"ז הא אדרבה הרב"י ס"ל איפכא דבהמ"ז קילא דהא לענין תפלה כ' הרב"י סתם דאין לצרפו ולענין בהמ"ז כתב דמצטרף בין לג' ובין ליו"ד ואיך נאמר דרמ"א יהיה סברת הפוכות עם הרב"י אע"כ דרמ"א ס"ל דשוה תפלה ובהמ"ז ובשניהם אין מצטרפין. וז"ל תשו' שב יעקב דגם בסי' נ"ה מסכים כו' דאין לצרף כו' אפי' ע"י חומש רק כ' מיהו יש נוהגים כו' לו' דאותן הנוהגים כן סומכים אאותו דעה שהביא המחבר בשעת הדחק אבל לא כ' רמ"א דשפי' עבדי הנוהגים כן וסמך עצמו על סי' קצ"פ ב' עכ"ל והאריך וקצרתי וע"ל מ"ש הט"ז סק"ג ע' במ"א ר"ס רל"ד שחולק:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>By means of a Chumash.</b> Bach writes that even according to the view that holding a Chumash is effective, it must be written as a scroll like a Sefer Torah, as Tosafos writes, unlike our printed chumashim. Accordingly, the child would need to hold a Sefer Torah. Eliyah Rabbah, however, writes in the name of his grandfather Rabbi Shmuel that even in pressing circumstances he was unwilling to rely upon this. Responsa Shav Yaakov, siman 6, rules likewise. He writes that Rama also did not intend that one should rely upon this, even though he writes that some are accustomed to do so. For in siman 199, seif 6, Beit Yosef writes that a minor may be included for zimun, whether with three or with ten. Rama writes there: “Some say that he is not included until he is thirteen years old, and this is the custom and should not be changed.” Thus Rama himself rules regarding Bircas HaMazon that a minor may not be included, whether with three or with ten, and that the custom should not be changed. One cannot say that Rama considers prayer more lenient than Bircas HaMazon. On the contrary, Beit Yosef holds the reverse, for regarding prayer he rules without qualification that a minor is not included, while regarding Bircas HaMazon he writes that a minor is included both with three and with ten. How could we say that Rama holds views exactly opposite to Beit Yosef? Rather, Rama holds that prayer and Bircas HaMazon are equal, and that in neither case is a minor included. Responsa Shav Yaakov writes that in siman 55 Rama likewise agrees that a minor is not included even when holding a Chumash. He merely writes that some are accustomed to be lenient, meaning that those who follow this custom rely, in pressing circumstances, upon the view cited by the Mechaber. Rama does not write that those who act this way are acting properly, and he relies upon what he wrote in siman 199, seif 6. He discusses this at length, and I have abbreviated it. See also what Taz writes in seif katan 3, and Magen Avraham at the beginning of siman 234, who disagrees.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>(Seif katan 10) And the thirteenth year, etc.</b> This also appears from the end of Eruvin, where a complete year is required. Meaning, it appears there in accordance with Rama's gloss, from which Magen Avraham raises his question. The Torah permits one who sold a house in a walled city to redeem it within a year. If he does not redeem it within a year, it becomes permanently vested in the buyer and never returns to the seller. If the year is intercalated, the seller has thirteen months. The same applies here. Although one might distinguish that there the verse says “complete,” thereby including the added month, nevertheless Beit Yosef derives our case from there. See there. Magen Avraham's statement concerning the thirteenth year is an additional proof, for during thirteen years there were several leap years, and in each leap year the child is given thirteen months for that year. His statement that in the thirteenth year he becomes bar mitzvah in Adar I follows from this reasoning.

<b>And one who was born, etc.</b> This is implied in Nedarim, where the Mishnah teaches: If one says, “This item is forbidden to me for this month,” when Rosh Chodesh of the following month arrives, it becomes permitted. The Gemara asks that this is obvious, and answers that the ruling was needed for a full month. Meaning, the month during which the vow was made had thirty days, and therefore the following Rosh Chodesh had two days. <b>(Seif katan 11) The threshold, etc.</b> And Sefer Tanya, etc., and so it appears from the Gemara. See Beit Yosef. Meaning, the source of this ruling is Pesachim 85b. We learned in a Mishnah: From the doorway inward is considered inward, both regarding lesser sanctities, which may be eaten throughout Yerushalayim but not outside Yerushalayim, and regarding the most sacred offerings, which may be eaten only within the Azarah. From the doorway outward is considered outward. Rav said: The same applies to prayer. The Gemara then raises a contradiction between the implications of the Mishnah. The first clause says that from the doorway inward is inward, implying that the doorway itself is outward. The latter clause says that from the doorway outward is outward, implying that the doorway itself is inward. It answers that in the gates of the Azarah, the doorway itself is regarded as inward, because all the gates of the Azarah were sanctified with the sanctity of the Azarah except the eastern gate, namely the Gate of Nikanor. This was for the sake of a metzora who had healed and had acted in accordance with the Torah and the mitzvah. On the eighth day he had not yet completed his atonement and was forbidden to enter the Azarah, yet he needed to insert his hands into the Azarah so that blood from his guilt offering could be placed upon his thumbs. Since the Gate of Nikanor was not sanctified, he could stand in the gateway and insert his hands. In doing so he did not violate the prohibition of partial entry, because inserting part of his body into the Azarah is not called entry. The other gates of the Azarah, however, were sanctified, and therefore the doorway itself is regarded as inward. By contrast, none of the gates of Yerushalayim were sanctified, so that metzoraim, who were sent outside Yerushalayim and forbidden to enter the city, could stand in the gateways to protect themselves from the sun and rain. Beit Yosef writes: “It would appear at first glance that regarding combining for a davar shebikdushah, the doorway itself is regarded as inward. For if, generally, gateways were inward regarding sacrificial law, the gates of the Azarah would not have required sanctification. Likewise, regarding the gates of Yerushalayim, were it not for the metzoraim they would have been sanctified for this reason. Rabbeinu Yerucham, however, writes regarding prayer that the doorway itself is regarded as outward. The Rambam likewise writes in chapter 9 of Hilchos Korban Pesach, regarding the prohibition against taking the Korban Pesach outside its company during its consumption, that the doorway itself is regarded as outward. It appears that we derive prayer from there, in accordance with Rabbeinu Yerucham.” These are the words of Beit Yosef. Therefore Shulchan Aruch writes here, without citing any dissenting view, that the doorway is regarded as outward. Although Beit Yosef found it more logical that the doorway should be regarded as inward for prayer, since the gates of the Azarah were sanctified and because a reason was needed for why the gates of Yerushalayim were not sanctified, as explained above, he nevertheless set aside his own view in favor of Rabbeinu Yerucham, especially since he found support from the Rambam. Had Beit Yosef seen Sefer Tanya, which disagrees, he would certainly have cited its view, and perhaps would have ruled accordingly, since that is what appears from the Gemara.
