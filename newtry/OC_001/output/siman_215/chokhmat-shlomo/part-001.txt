<b>There, seif 2.</b> One who hears a Jew reciting a blessing, etc. Note: This is indeed Rambam's view. The Tur's language in siman 215 is as follows: “It appears to me that when it says that one answers amen, this is not an obligation but optional,” etc. “Rambam, however, writes that everyone who hears is obligated to answer amen,” etc. I am astonished how the Tur could have entertained the possibility that answering amen is merely optional. The Gemara explicitly states in Shevuos 36a that Rabbi Yose bar Chanina said: “Amen contains an oath,” etc.; “it contains affirmation of words, as it is written, ‘Yirmiyahu said to Chananyah: Amen, so may Hashem do,’” etc. Rashi explains: “Amen contains affirmation of words, and it is proper to answer amen to a prayer or supplication, for it means that the words should be confirmed, that it should be Hashem's will that they be true.” This appears to prove that answering amen is obligatory. Perhaps the Tur explains the Gemara unlike Rashi and understands that it merely teaches that one may answer amen and that this is not an orphaned amen. There may be support for this explanation, for why did the Gemara bring proof from Yirmiyahu rather than from an earlier verse in Tehillim 72, “Blessed is His glorious name forever, and may His glory fill the entire earth, amen and amen”; or Tehillim 89, “Blessed is Hashem forever, amen and amen”; or Tehillim 106, “Blessed is Hashem, the God of Israel, from eternity to eternity, and all the people shall say amen”? All these verses already show that amen affirms words. Why, then, was the verse from Yirmiyahu necessary? According to the above, however, it is understandable. The Gemara came to teach that this was not an orphaned amen, for even when Chananyah, who was a false prophet, spoke words of blessing to Israel, answering amen was not considered an orphaned amen. Nevertheless, the Tur remains difficult. How can he say that answering amen is optional, when Scripture says, “And all the people shall say amen,” implying an obligation? One cannot explain that the verse merely teaches that they are permitted to answer amen, for this was already known from Tehillim 72 and 89. It must therefore teach that answering amen is obligatory, supporting Rambam. This requires extensive study. See Taz, seif katan 4, regarding his explanation of the Tur's words, “one answers,” etc. See also what I wrote in my work on Shulchan Aruch, Orach Chayim, from the year 5609, in a responsum to the holy community of Pomerania, placed in Hilchos Tefillah, siman 124. See there and examine it carefully.

<b>There, seif 4.</b> Whoever recites an unnecessary blessing bears the Name of Heaven in vain; he is like one who swears falsely, and it is forbidden to answer amen after him. Note: A copy of Chayei Adam recently came into my hands, and I saw that in Nishmas Adam he asks why a blessing recited in vain should be prohibited by Torah law when it is not said in mockery or jest. After all, if a person says a thousand times, “You are Hashem, King of the universe, Who creates the fruit of the tree,” or similar words, it is not prohibited; why should it become prohibited merely because he says, “Blessed are You,” etc.? On this basis he emboldened himself to dispute all the poskim and permit reciting a blessing in a case of doubt. I say that this view should be forgotten and never stated. The words of the poskim are certainly correct. The very greatness of a blessing over other songs and praises causes its misuse to be objectionable and prohibited when it is said without cause.

A blessing serves as payment for the benefit we have received from Hashem, whether a physical benefit through the blessings over enjoyment, or a spiritual benefit through a mitzvah He gave us. Every blessing is recompense for recompense. Chazal explicitly state in the sixth chapter of Berachos that one who enjoys this world without a blessing is as though he robs the Holy One, blessed is He, and the congregation of Israel; they also say that he is as though he misuses sacred property. All this demonstrates that a blessing is payment to Hashem for the benefit received. If a person says, “You, Hashem, King of the universe, created,” etc., he has not thereby discharged this payment. Only by saying, “Blessed are You,” does he pay it. A blessing therefore possesses a quality far beyond ordinary song and praise. For this very reason it is prohibited when said in vain.

By way of analogy, if a person says to a distinguished man, “I am grateful to you for the favor you performed for me,” and that man knows that he performed no favor, he will be angry and say, “You are mocking me. I did nothing for you; why are you thanking me falsely?” “One who speaks falsehood shall not stand before My eyes.” How much more so regarding Hashem. Chazal said that the prophets initially omitted the descriptions “mighty” and “awesome” because, knowing that Hashem is truthful, they would not speak falsely about Him. Later they restored the crown to its former place because they explained that these very events displayed His might. Had that explanation not applied, they would not have said those words, because He is truthful. The same applies to the language of blessing, which is payment to Hashem. Such payment is appropriate only when one has presently benefited from that particular gift, whether spiritual or physical. If he has not presently benefited from it, the payment is false, and “one who speaks falsehood shall not stand before” Hashem. An unnecessary blessing is therefore forbidden. But if he does not use the formula “Blessed are You,” and says only, “You are Hashem,” then, since this formula does not discharge the obligation of blessing, it is clearly not payment but merely praise and song. Therefore, even when he has not presently benefited, it is not false and may be said. For the same reason one may not recite a blessing even in a case of doubt, since doubtful falsehood is also forbidden. With Hashem's help, the words of the early poskim are thereby upheld. This is clear and true in my humble opinion.

Regarding the question raised in Chayei Adam whether one recites a blessing in a case of a double doubt, see what I wrote in my work on Shulchan Aruch, Orach Chayim, from the year 5607, pages 64 and 105, in a responsum to the holy community of Schwabach. See there. Regarding whether an unnecessary blessing involves a Torah prohibition, see my work on Shulchan Aruch, Yoreh Deah, fifth edition, siman 14, in a responsum to the holy community of Rytin, where I discussed incidentally a matter relevant here.

Magen Avraham cites here the dispute: according to Tosafos, an unnecessary blessing is prohibited only D'Rabbanan, whereas according to Rambam it is prohibited by Torah law. It now appears to me, with Hashem's help, that there is clear proof that the prohibition is only D'Rabbanan. Pesachim 115 states that even according to the view that mitzvos do not nullify one another, this applies only when both are D'Oraisa, but a D'Oraisa mitzvah and a D'Rabbanan mitzvah do nullify one another. One may ask why maror is regarded as merely D'Rabbanan. Although the obligation to eat it is only D'Rabbanan, if one recited the blessing “concerning eating maror,” then if he does not eat any maror he has recited a blessing in vain and violated a Torah prohibition. Once he has recited the blessing, the matter should therefore acquire a Torah-level dimension, and why should the maror nullify the matzah? It must be that an unnecessary blessing is prohibited only D'Rabbanan. Even if he does not eat the maror, his blessing in vain remains only D'Rabbanan, so the maror remains D'Rabbanan.

It is difficult to answer that there is a distinction between one Torah obligation and two Torah obligations, because matzah also has a blessing and would therefore involve two Torah matters. Such a distinction may perhaps apply within Rabbinic law, between one Rabbinic factor and two, but not regarding Torah law. Yet this proof can be rejected. If one eats matzah and maror together, he cannot first recite the blessing over eating matzah and then a separate blessing over maror, for the blessing over maror, which is only D'Rabbanan, would interrupt between the blessing over matzah and eating it. It cannot be regarded as necessary for the eating. He must therefore recite one blessing over the mitzvos, or over matzah and maror together. Since he in fact eats the matzah, the blessing is not in vain even though maror was also mentioned. According to all opinions this is not an unnecessary blessing. A practical consequence arises according to Rashi's view concerning tefillin, that if one did not interrupt he recites one blessing, namely “concerning the mitzvos.” If he recited “concerning the mitzvos” but possessed only one of the tefillin, the blessing would nevertheless not be in vain, since he fulfilled at least one mitzvah. Examine this carefully.

Regarding the Shulchan Aruch's statement that whoever recites an unnecessary blessing bears the Name of Heaven in vain, is like one who swears falsely, and may not be answered with amen, see Magen Avraham, who writes that Yoma explicitly teaches that even one who causes an unnecessary blessing violates “You shall not bear.” From this one may prove the view of Tosafos in Rosh Hashanah 33 that when the Name is used in the form of a blessing, the prohibition of “You shall not bear” is only D'Rabbanan. Sotah 43b asks what practical difference exists between Rabbi Yose and Rabbi Yose HaGelili and answers that they differ regarding a Rabbinic transgression. It then asks: According to whom does the Baraisa follow that says one who spoke between the tefillin of the arm and the tefillin of the head has a transgression in his hand and returns from the battlefront? It answers that it follows Rabbi Yose HaGelili. Rashi explains, “A transgression is in his hand if he did not return and recite a blessing, for Menachos states that if he spoke he recites two blessings, and if he did not speak he recites one.”

Why did the Gemara assume that “a transgression is in his hand” means that he did not repeat the blessing? On the contrary, if it meant that he did repeat the blessing, then according to the view that even the formula of a blessing falls under the Torah prohibition of “You shall not bear,” he would have committed a Torah transgression by causing himself to recite an unnecessary blessing. The Sages required a second blessing only because his interruption created a Rabbinic obligation supported by “You shall not turn aside,” and such a required blessing is not in vain. But since, had he not spoken, he would not have been required even Rabbinically to recite another blessing, and through his own speech he brought himself into a new obligation, he caused an unnecessary blessing. As Magen Avraham proves from Yoma, even causing such a blessing is included in an unnecessary blessing. If the blessing formula were prohibited by Torah law, he would therefore violate “You shall not bear” by repeating it. The Baraisa could then follow Rabbi Yose HaGelili. Why was the Gemara forced to explain that he failed to repeat the blessing and that the Baraisa does not follow Rabbi Yose HaGelili? It must be that Tosafos is correct: any mention of Hashem in the form of a blessing violates “You shall not bear” only D'Rabbanan. Therefore, whether he repeated the blessing or failed to repeat it, the transgression is only Rabbinic, and the Baraisa necessarily does not follow Rabbi Yose HaGelili. This is a clear proof for Tosafos. Examine it carefully.
