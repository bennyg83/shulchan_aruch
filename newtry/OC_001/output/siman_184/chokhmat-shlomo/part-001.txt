**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Seif 1.</b> One who ate in another place must recite Bircas HaMazon before leaving his place, etc.; if he left intentionally, he must return to his place, etc. It appears to me, with Hashem’s help, that a novel ruling may be derived here. The Yerushalmi, Pesachim chapter 4, comments on the Mishnah concerning one who is going to slaughter his Pesach offering, and states: Rabbi Yudan said, as a mnemonic, this parallels the dispute in Berachos. One opinion gives the measure as a kezayis, while Rabbi Yehudah gives the measure as a kebeitzah. It was taught that for less than this amount, he is not burdened to return. What is the distinction between the two cases? This one can be nullified. Korban HaEdah, in Shirei Korban, wonders what is being taught by the statement that for less than this amount he is not burdened to return, since the Mishnah explicitly asks, “Until what amount must he return?” which implies that for less than that amount he need not return. He leaves this unresolved. In truth, his question is difficult to understand. The Yerushalmi may mean that for less than this amount he need not return even for his own sake, which is not explicit in the Mishnah. Alternatively, it may mean that for less than a kezayis he need not return even after the prohibition has taken effect, when it can no longer be nullified. See Magen Avraham in the name of Tosafos. This is straightforward. Nevertheless, in my humble opinion, the passage does not in fact refer to chametz. If it did, why does the Yerushalmi interrupt the discussion with the mnemonic from Berachos, and why is that mnemonic needed at all? At first glance, one might have said that this proves that the Yerushalmi follows the view stated in siman 206 that even a whole entity smaller than a kezayis requires an after-blessing, and also the view in siman 184 that all seven species require their blessing to be recited in the place where they were eaten. Accordingly, if one left intentionally, he must return. The Yerushalmi would then be teaching that the law of Berachos is learned from the law of chametz: just as for chametz one returns only for a kebeitzah or a kezayis, so too for Bircas HaMazon one returns only for a kezayis. For less than a kezayis, even if it was a whole entity, he does not return but blesses where he is. Yet according to those who hold that a whole entity smaller than a kezayis requires no after-blessing, the passage may also be explained. Regarding chametz, we rule like the Sages that the measure is a kebeitzah, whereas regarding Bircas HaMazon we rule like Rabbi Meir that the measure is a kezayis. It is true that in the chametz Mishnah Rabbi formulated the Sages’ view in the plural, indicating that he agreed with it, and therefore the halachah follows them. But why, then, do we rule like Rabbi Meir regarding Bircas HaMazon and like Rabbi Yehudah regarding chametz? Necessarily, this supports the view of the poskim that one may not recite Bircas HaMazon unless he ate the required measure, because we rule like Rabbi Meir that eating is a kezayis and satisfaction refers to drinking. Since Bircas HaMazon is Torah-level, we follow Rabbi Meir stringently. Returning to the place of eating, however, and likewise returning because of chametz, are only Rabbinic obligations, so a kebeitzah suffices. According to this, where one left without reciting Bircas HaMazon in his place, the obligation to return is likewise only Rabbinic, and the measure should therefore be a kebeitzah. This is the Yerushalmi’s meaning when it says that the dispute in Bircas HaMazon serves as a mnemonic. The opinions follow their respective positions there. The difference between the cases is that chametz can be nullified, and therefore for less than the required amount one is not burdened to return. The same applies to Bircas HaMazon: since the obligation to return is Rabbinic, for less than a kebeitzah he does not return. I later remembered that what I wrote, that Rabbi formulated Rabbi Yehudah’s view as the Sages’ view, was mistaken. The Mishnah explicitly states Rabbi Yehudah’s position and then the Sages’ position. Nevertheless, the question remains why we rule like Rabbi Meir regarding Bircas HaMazon but like the Sages here, if they follow parallel approaches. Necessarily, Bircas HaMazon itself is Torah-level, while the requirement to return is Rabbinic. The Yerushalmi’s conclusion, however, requires explanation. It asks: Until now we have discussed consecrated meat; from where do we know the law of chametz? Rabbi Yosi bar Bun answers from the Mishnah, “and likewise one who left Jerusalem,” which shows that what falls under one law falls under the other. Korban HaEdah’s explanation is difficult. It appears instead that the Yerushalmi means as I wrote initially: even after the prohibition has taken effect and chametz can no longer be nullified, one is not burdened to return for less than the stated measure. The Yerushalmi then asks what distinction remains between chametz and consecrated meat, since after the prohibition begins neither has a remedy. It answers that chametz is still lighter because at an earlier time it could have been nullified, whereas consecrated meat never had such a remedy. One might challenge that perhaps the Mishnah’s measure applies only while chametz can still be nullified, whereas after that time everyone would require return even for less. The Yerushalmi therefore cites the parallel case of one who left Jerusalem, teaching that the laws are truly equated. Since consecrated meat can no longer be corrected except by returning, the same must be true of chametz once it can no longer be nullified; nevertheless, the measure remains a kezayis or kebeitzah according to each opinion. In any event, although this second explanation is more compelling, the first ruling also appears correct: for less than a kebeitzah one need not return to his original place in order to recite Bircas HaMazon. This may be learned from chametz, since both obligations to return are Rabbinic, and it is reasonable to be lenient in a Rabbinic matter. Examine this carefully.

<b>Seif 4.</b> One who is uncertain whether he recited Bircas HaMazon, etc. See Magen Avraham, seif katan 7, who writes that he must also recite the fourth blessing, so that people should not come to treat it lightly. A clear proof may be brought from Pesachim 39b, where Rami bar Chama asks whether one may fulfill the mitzvah of maror with maaser sheni. Perhaps one cannot fulfill the Torah-level mitzvah of matzah with it, but may fulfill the Rabbinic mitzvah of maror; or perhaps there is no distinction. Rava says, “It is reasonable that matzah and maror are alike.” Rashi explains that they are juxtaposed, but this is difficult, because juxtaposition applies only to Torah law, not to a Rabbinic enactment. According to the principle above, however, the meaning is clear. If matzah and maror were entirely separate matters, one could say that although maaser sheni is invalid for Torah-level matzah, it is valid for Rabbinic maror. But since matzah and maror are performed together, if one were invalid for matzah but valid for maror, people might belittle the Rabbinic mitzvah. Rava therefore says that since matzah and maror are paired, maror too is not fulfilled with maaser sheni. This is a proper explanation, with Hashem’s help. After writing this, I saw that Pnei Yehoshua also noticed the difficulty in Rashi’s words and explained that since matzah is juxtaposed to maror and maror to matzah, maror at the Torah level would likewise be invalid with maaser sheni. Therefore, even now that maror is Rabbinic, the Sages enacted it in the pattern of Torah law. This is difficult, for what did Rava add? Rami bar Chama himself had already framed the doubt as whether the distinction follows from maror being Rabbinic, which necessarily assumes that if it were Torah-level it would certainly be invalid. If Rava’s point were merely that every Rabbinic enactment follows the pattern of Torah law, he should have stated that principle explicitly. It appears to me that Rashi’s intention may instead be explained in light of Gittin 65a, where the Gemara says that the principle that the Sages enacted Rabbinic law in the pattern of Torah law applies only where the Rabbinic law has a Torah root, but not where it has no Torah root. Later the Gemara proves that maaser nowadays is considered to have no Torah root, even though in the Temple era it was Torah-level. Accordingly, Rami bar Chama’s doubt may be whether maror nowadays, since it has no present Torah obligation, was not enacted in the pattern of Torah law, or whether even such a Rabbinic mitzvah follows Torah law. Rava answers that matzah and maror are parallel and interconnected, so maror is considered to have a Torah root. Where there is a Torah root, the Sages certainly enacted it in the pattern of Torah law. This is very correct. Regarding the ruling in Shulchan Aruch, siman 184, seif 4, that one who is uncertain whether he recited Bircas HaMazon must repeat it because it is Torah-level, see Magen Avraham, who writes that he should repeat even the fourth blessing so that people not belittle it. At first glance this is difficult from siman 67, where one uncertain whether he recited Krias Shema repeats it together with its blessings. Taz explains that one also recites blessings in a case of doubt concerning a Torah obligation, while Magen Avraham, citing Rashba and Kesef Mishneh, explains that the Sages enacted the blessings in that manner. Why did Magen Avraham not explain there as he does here, that omitting the Rabbinic blessings in a case of doubt would cause people to treat them lightly? It must be that this concern applies specifically to the fourth blessing of Bircas HaMazon, because the Sages already treated it more leniently regarding laborers, and therefore also enacted that amen should not be answered aloud after it, lest people belittle it. Since it already had a lenient status, they were concerned that if people saw that it was not repeated in a case of doubt, they would belittle it further. Bach writes that today, when laborers recite it like everyone else, amen may be answered aloud because there is no longer such a concern. According to this, today one uncertain whether he recited Bircas HaMazon should not repeat the fourth blessing, since the concern no longer applies. Rabbi Shimon Chayon and Magen Avraham may disagree with Bach and hold that once Chazal were concerned for belittlement under Talmudic conditions, the enactment remains even today. In any event, since Bach holds that there is no present concern, the matter is doubtful, and one should refrain from reciting the fourth blessing in a case of doubt. This appears correct. In my work Sefer HaChaim I offered another explanation. At present, however, what I have written here appears more correct. There I raised a difficulty from Tosafos regarding one who repeats Emes Veyatziv but not Krias Shema, and my earlier answer resolves that issue. Here, however, the language of the poskim and Rashba cannot be reconciled through that answer. Necessarily, the concern of belittling the Rabbinic enactment applies only in this specific case and not generally. This is clear.

<b>There, seif 6.</b> The minimum amount of food that requires Bircas HaMazon is a kezayis. The Gemara records a dispute whether this obligation is Torah-level or Rabbinic. See my work on Megillas Rus, in the additions from the year 5611, on the verse, “She ate, was satisfied, and left over,” where I explained the basis of their dispute and connected it to the uncertainty raised by Mishneh LaMelech, Hilchos Chametz chapter 1, regarding the principle that eating is measured by a kezayis. Examine it carefully. Regarding the ruling that the minimum amount requiring Bircas HaMazon is a kezayis, were I not hesitant I would suggest a novel explanation. Berachos 20b states that Rabbi Avira expounded: Hashem said, “I wrote in the Torah, ‘You shall eat, be satisfied, and bless,’ yet Israel is stringent with itself to recite Bircas HaMazon even for a kezayis or a kebeitzah.” Rashi explains that a kezayis follows Rabbi Meir and a kebeitzah follows Rabbi Yehudah. This is difficult, for we rule that a kezayis suffices, so why mention the rejected view? Based on what I wrote in Sefer HaChaim, siman 486, that food lodged between the teeth does not combine for a prohibition but does combine for a mitzvah, one might say as follows. Rabbi Meir’s measure of a kezayis applies to mitzvah eating, such as matzah, because even what remains between the teeth combines to complete the mitzvah measure and is therefore also sufficient for Bircas HaMazon. Rabbi Yehudah would require a kebeitzah even there. Rabbi Meir, however, may agree that with ordinary, non-mitzvah eating, food between the teeth does not combine, and one therefore needs a full kezayis apart from what remains between the teeth. Since more than a kezayis is then required, the measure is set at a kebeitzah, which certainly leaves a kezayis actually swallowed. The Gemara could thus mean that Israel is stringent to recite Bircas HaMazon for a kezayis when eating matzah and for a kebeitzah when eating voluntarily. Yet the poskim do not rule this way. It therefore appears that the Gemara means something else. Elsewhere the Gemara says regarding the response Baruch Hashem HaMevorach that one need not place himself in a dispute. This shows that even where the halachah follows one view, it is proper, where possible, to satisfy all opinions. See Taz, siman 167. Accordingly, regarding the wording of the blessing HaMotzi, the halachic novelty is evident from the blessing itself, and it is appropriate to demonstrate that novelty. But regarding the amount eaten, the blessing does not reveal how much one consumed, so there is no gain in displaying a novelty. It is therefore preferable to satisfy all opinions. Thus, although a kezayis technically requires Bircas HaMazon, the scrupulous eat a kebeitzah so as to satisfy Rabbi Yehudah as well. The Gemara describes two forms of scrupulousness. First, although the Torah says, “You shall eat, be satisfied, and bless,” they recite Bircas HaMazon even for a kezayis. Second, even though a kezayis would suffice, they are stringent to eat a kebeitzah so as to satisfy all views. This is correct. The matter may be explained further by noting the precise wording, “May Hashem lift His face toward you.” If it meant simply that Hashem should show favor, it should have said, “May Hashem lift your face,” as it says regarding Lot, “I have lifted your face,” and regarding Iyov, “Hashem lifted the face of Iyov.” Why does it say “His face”? Even when one shows favor, the degree of favor shown to a poor person dressed in torn clothing is not the same as that shown to a wealthy person dressed in fine garments. The favor corresponds to the dignity of the petitioner. Likewise, when a person merits, he bears the Divine image, and when he does not merit, he lacks it. The blessing therefore asks for two favors. First, “May Hashem lift His face toward you,” meaning that He should grant you a dignified countenance and the Divine image. Then He should lift your own face and show you favor fitting for one whose countenance is honored. The ministering angels therefore asked: Hashem wrote in the Torah that He does not show favor, implying that He grants not even one form of favor, yet the verse asks that He perform two forms of favor. Hashem answered that Israel is stringent to the level of both a kezayis and a kebeitzah, thus performing two forms of self-imposed favor, and therefore He too performs two forms for them. This also reconciles the passage with Niddah 70b, which answers differently, distinguishing between Israel and the nations. Both answers are needed: one form of favor derives from the distinction between Israel and the nations, and the second from their stringency regarding a kezayis and a kebeitzah. According to this, the phrase “a kezayis and a kebeitzah” may indeed be understood as Rashi explains, a kezayis according to Rabbi Meir and a kebeitzah according to Rabbi Yehudah. Examine this carefully.
