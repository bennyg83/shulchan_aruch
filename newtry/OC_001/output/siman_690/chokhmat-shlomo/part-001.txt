<b>סעיף ג'  </b>צריך לקרותה כולה וכו'. נ"ב גרסינן בש"ס דמגלה דף י"ח אר"ח וכו' הלכה כדברי האומר צריך לקרותה כולה ואפילו למ"ד מאיש יהודי צריכה שתהיה כתובה כולה וקשה לי למה לא קמ"ל רבותא טפי אפילו למ"ד מאחר הדברים האלה צריכה שתהיה כתובה כולה דהרי במשנה יש שם ג' דיעות דת"ק ס"ל כולה ור' יהודה ס"ל מאיש יהודי ורבי יוסי ס"ל מאחר הדברים האלה. ואם כן אם רצה לאשמעינן רבותא הו"ל לאשמועינן רבותא אפילו למ"ד מאחר הדברים האלה צריכה שתהא כתובה כולה. ואין לו' לדידיה כיון דמיקל כולי האי דדי מאחר הדברים האלה לא בעינן שתהא כתובה כולה דא"כ כדפריך לעיל בדף הקודם שם גבי היה כותבה דורשה ומגיה וכו' ה"ד וכו' אלא היכי דכתב פסוקא פסוקא וקרי ליה ומי יצא והאר"ח וכו' ואפילו למ"ד מאיש יהודי צריכה שתהיה כתובה כולה ומה פריך נימא דאתיא כרבי יוסי דס"ל מאחר הדברים האלה ולכך לדידיה לא בעינן שתהא כתובה כולה ומשכחת לה דהיה כותבה פסוקא פסוקא ותני לה ומה פריך. אלא וודאי דלכ"ע בעינן שתהיה כתובה כולה ואם כן למה לא קמ"ל דאף לרבי יוסי דס"ל מאחר הדברים האלה בעינן שתהא כתובה כולה וצ"ע מיהו י"ל דקמ"ל אף בנחסר רק עד איש יהודי פסולה ומכ"ש למ"ד אחר הדברים האלה דנחסר טובא דפסולה ואדרבא הוי למ"ד זה רבותא טפי ועיין בהר"ן וש"ע והבן זה ודוק. והנה מזה יהיה מוכח כדעת הר"ן באם השמיט ענין שלם אפילו בדיעבד פסול. ולכך י"ל שפיר דר"ח קמ"ל לענין דיעבד דאפילו בדיעבד צריכה שתהיה כתובה כולה לכך שפיר למ"ד מאיש יהודי ללמד אפילו אם נחסר עד איש יהודי פסולה. ולכך לא נקט למ"ד עד אחר הדברים האלה דבזה יותר פשיטא דפסול אם נחסר כ"כ. אבל אם נימא כדעת הרשב"א דבדיעבד כשר ור"ח קמ"ל רק לכתחלה א"כ לענין לכתחלה אפילו אם נחסר פסוק א' אסור לכתחלה. ובע"כ מה דנקט אפילו למ"ד מאיש יהודי הכוונה לענין אם נחסר עד איש יהודי דאפי' אם נחסר פסוק אחד אסור לכתחלה. ובע"כ הכוונה רק אף דמיקל בענין הקריאה מכל מקום לענין הכתיב' צריכה שתהא כתובה כולה אפילו בלי חסרון פסוק אחד א"כ בזה קשה שפיר לאשמעינן רבותא טפי אפילו למ"ד מאחר הדברים האלה צריכה שתהא כתובה כולה אפילו בלי חסרון פסוק אחד ולמה לא אשמעינן רבותא טפי. ובע"כ מוכח כדעת הר"ן לדינא ולהרשב"א צ"ע בזה ודוק היטב:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Section 3.</b> "He must read all of it, etc." Note: We learn in the Gemara of Megillah, page 18: Rav Chelbo said, etc., the halachah follows the one who says that one must read all of it; and even according to the one who says from "There was a Jewish man," it must be written in its entirety. This is difficult for me: why did it not teach us a greater novelty, that even according to the one who says from "After these things," it must be written in its entirety? For in the mishnah there are three opinions there: the first tanna holds all of it, Rabbi Yehudah holds from "There was a Jewish man," and Rabbi Yose holds from "After these things." If so, if it wanted to teach us a novelty, it should have taught us the novelty that even according to the one who says from "After these things," it must be written in its entirety. One cannot say that according to him, since he is so lenient that it is enough from "After these things," we do not require it to be written in its entirety. For if so, when the Gemara challenges above on the previous page regarding one who was writing it, expounding it, and correcting it, etc., asking, what are the circumstances, etc., and answering that it is where he wrote verse by verse and read it, and then asking, did he fulfill? But Rav Chelbo said, etc., and even according to the one who says from "There was a Jewish man," it must be written in its entirety. What is the challenge? Let it say that it follows Rabbi Yose, who holds from "After these things," and therefore according to him we do not require it to be written in its entirety; thus it is possible to find a case where he was writing it verse by verse and teaching it, and what is the challenge? Rather, certainly according to everyone we require it to be written in its entirety. If so, why did it not teach us that even according to Rabbi Yose, who holds from "After these things," we require it to be written in its entirety? This requires analysis. However, one can say that it teaches us that even if only until "There was a Jewish man" is missing, it is invalid, and all the more so according to the one who says from "After these things," where much is missing, it is invalid. On the contrary, for that opinion this is an even greater novelty. See the Ran and Shulchan Aruch, understand this, and analyze it carefully. From this there will be proof for the opinion of the Ran, that if one omitted a complete matter, even after the fact it is invalid. Therefore one can say well that Rav Chelbo teaches us regarding after the fact, that even after the fact it must be written in its entirety. Therefore it is correct to mention the one who says from "There was a Jewish man," to teach that even if it was missing until "There was a Jewish man," it is invalid. Therefore it did not mention the one who says until "After these things," because in that case it is more obvious that it is invalid if so much is missing. But if we say like the opinion of the Rashba, that after the fact it is valid and Rav Chelbo teaches only the initial law, then regarding the initial law even if one verse is missing it is forbidden initially. Perforce, what he mentioned, even according to the one who says from "There was a Jewish man," means regarding where it is missing until "There was a Jewish man," for even if one verse is missing it is forbidden initially. Perforce, the intent is only that although he is lenient regarding the reading, nevertheless regarding the writing it must be written in its entirety, even without the absence of one verse. If so, the difficulty is strong: it should have taught us a greater novelty, that even according to the one who says from "After these things," it must be written in its entirety, even without the absence of one verse. Why did it not teach us the greater novelty? Perforce, this proves the Ran's opinion as practical law, and according to the Rashba this requires analysis. Analyze this well.

בציבור לא מהני זה. והוא דזה דומה למ"ש בש"ס ובש"ע לקמן סי' תרצ"א דאין קורין בציבור במגלה הכתובה בין הכתובים ואם קרא לא יצא יי"ח אם לא שיש היכר שהיא ארוכה או קצרה. אם כן מוכח דבציבור לא מהני כוונת הלב לצאת רק שיהיה ניכר שקורא לשם מגלה וא"כ ה"נ אם כותבה דורשה ומגיה א"כ בפועל אינו ניכר אם קורא לשם מצוה או לכתוב ולדרוש ולהגיה א"כ לא עדיף ממגילה הכתובה בין הכתובים באינה ניכרת דבע"כ הטעם כיון דאינו ניכר לא נודע להם אם היא מגלה א"כ ה"ה נמי אם אינו ניכר אם קראה לשם מצוה דלא מהני בציבור ועיין במג"א סי' תרצ"א שכתב דכל עשרה מקרי ציבור ע"ש. ובזה הונח לנו על נכון דברי הכל בו שהביא הקרבן עדה בירושלמי פ"ב ניחא ליה בתירוץ הירושלמי דמיירי בי"ד בכרכים והניח בצ"ע. ולפמ"ש יהיה אתי שפיר דהנה דין הנ"ל נראה ברור למעיין בפירש"י והר"ן והב"י הביאם דהטעם דפסולה בכתובה בין הכתובים מכח דאינו ניכר אם הוי כקורא במקרא וליכא פרסומי ניסא וא"כ ה"נ אם כתבה דרשה ומגיה אינו ניכר שהוא לשם מצוה וליכא פרסומי ניסא. ולפי"ז אתי שפיר כיון דקיי"ל מגלה בזמנה ביחיד שלא בזמנה בעשרה ומוכח בש"ס דף ה' דאפי' בכרכים בע"ש נמי נחשב שלא בזמנה והוי בעשרה. וא"כ קשה היאך אפשר לומר דהיה כתבה בי"ד בכרכים הרי אז בעינן עשרה וא"כ לא מהני אם כוון לבו. אך הירושלמי לשיטתו אזיל דהמעיין בירושלמי פ"ק דמגלה הלכה ג' וז"ל ר' אבינא בשם ר' אסי כל שאמרו כותבה דיוצא אף ביחיד וכמ"ש אבל ר"א דס"ל דיוצא בציבור בכתוכה בין הכתובים א"כ אין ראיה מן המשנה דהי' כתבה דיוצא ביחיד לכך ס"ל דבעינן ציבור. ולדידן דקי"ל כר"י דאינו יוצא בכתובה בין הכתובים וקיי"ל שלא בזמנה אף בי"ד בעינן עשרה אי אפשר לתרץ כהירושלמי ואתי שפיר דברי הכל בו בעזה"י על נכון ודו"ק בזה:

<b>There, section 13.</b> "If he was writing it, meaning that he read a verse in the megillah from which he was copying and wrote it, if he directed his heart to fulfill his obligation, etc." Note: In my humble opinion a new law appears, and it is correct with Hashem's help: even if he directed his heart, this is only for an individual, where if he directed his heart to fulfill his obligation it helps; but in a congregation this does not help. This is because it is similar to what is written in the Gemara and in Shulchan Aruch below, siman 691, that we do not read publicly from a megillah written among the Writings, and if he read he did not fulfill his obligation unless there is a recognizable sign that it is longer or shorter. If so, it is proven that in a congregation intent of the heart to fulfill does not help; rather, it must be recognizable that he is reading for the sake of the megillah. If so, here too, when he writes it, expounds it, and corrects it, in actual practice it is not recognizable whether he is reading for the sake of the mitzvah or in order to write, expound, and correct. If so, this is no better than a megillah written among the Writings when it is not recognizable. Perforce, the reason there is that since it is not recognizable, it is not known to them whether it is a megillah. The same law applies if it is not recognizable whether he read it for the sake of the mitzvah; this does not help in a congregation. See Magen Avraham, siman 691, who wrote that any ten are called a congregation; see there. With this the words of the Kol Bo, brought by the Korban HaEdah on the Yerushalmi, chapter 2, halachah 2, are properly settled. Regarding "if he was writing it," the Yerushalmi says: this teaches that on Purim work is permitted. It answers: say that it was on the fourteenth in walled cities; end quote of the Yerushalmi. The Korban HaEdah brought in Shirei Korban the words of the Kol Bo, who cited the Yerushalmi's question and answered that it was a pressing circumstance. The Korban HaEdah wondered: why was he not satisfied with the Yerushalmi's answer that it speaks of the fourteenth in walled cities? He left this requiring analysis. According to what I wrote, it works well. This law appears clear to one who examines Rashi's explanation, the Ran, and Beis Yosef, who brought them, that the reason it is invalid when written among the Writings is because it is not recognizable and it is like reading Scripture, and there is no publicizing of the miracle. If so, here too, when he wrote it, expounded it, and corrected it, it is not recognizable that it is for the sake of the mitzvah, and there is no publicizing of the miracle. According to this it works well. Since we hold that a megillah in its proper time may be read by an individual, and not in its proper time requires ten, and it is proven in the Gemara, page 5, that even in walled cities on Friday it is also considered not in its proper time and requires ten, if so it is difficult: how can one say that he was writing it on the fourteenth in walled cities? At that time we require ten, and if so his directing his heart does not help. But the Yerushalmi follows its own view. One who examines the Yerushalmi in the first chapter of Megillah, halachah 3, will see this language: Rabbi Avina said in the name of Rabbi Asi: anything they said should be postponed to the day of assembly, and specifically with ten. If so, it is proven that anything not postponed to the day of assembly, even if postponed to Friday, nevertheless does not require ten. Therefore the Yerushalmi can answer that the mishnah speaks of the fourteenth in walled cities. But the Kol Bo follows the view of our Gemara, which holds on page 5 that even in walled cities on the fourteenth ten are required. Therefore it is impossible to explain that it speaks of the fourteenth, since ten are called a congregation, and therefore he was forced to explain it differently. Analyze this carefully: even if the others have already fulfilled their obligation, nevertheless, since ten are required and publicizing is required, the case of one who was writing it does not help. Analyze this. One should not challenge our words by asking: if so, according to Rabbi Asi, who holds that whether in its proper time or not in its proper time it requires ten, how will he explain the mishnah of "if he was writing it, etc."? This is not difficult. Rabbi Asi will hold that regarding a megillah written among the Writings as well, one fulfills in a congregation, and he disagrees with Rabbi Yochanan. For in any event he disagrees with Rabbi Yochanan: Rabbi Yochanan holds that it is permitted even for an individual, while Rabbi Asi holds that we require a congregation, as Tosafos wrote under "if so." So too, in this matter he disagrees with Rabbi Yochanan. On the contrary, according to this it works well, for this depends on that. Rabbi Yochanan follows his own view, that in a congregation one has not fulfilled with a megillah written among the Writings; perforce, according to him, it is proven from the mishnah of "if he was writing it" that one fulfills even individually, as I wrote. But Rabbi Asi, who holds that one fulfills in a congregation with a megillah written among the Writings, if so there is no proof from the mishnah of "if he was writing it" that one fulfills individually, and therefore he holds that we require a congregation. For us, since we rule like Rabbi Yochanan that one does not fulfill with a megillah written among the Writings, and we rule that not in its proper time, even on the fourteenth, requires ten, it is impossible to answer like the Yerushalmi. The words of the Kol Bo therefore work well, with Hashem's help, properly. Analyze this carefully.

<b>There, section 14.</b> "The one who reads the megillah must intend to discharge the listener, etc." Note: See Magen Avraham, seif katan 15, who proved that even if the listener did not hear one word, he has not fulfilled, and he wrote that this is not implied in the Bach. This implies that the Bach holds that if the listener did not hear one word, he has fulfilled. In my humble opinion there appears to be a proof for the Bach from the Yerushalmi, and this is its language: It was taught: we are not exacting regarding its errors. Rabbi Yitzchak bar Abba, etc., were sitting before Rav; one said one form of "Jews" and one said another form of "Jews," and he did not make either of them go back; end quote. Beis Yosef in this siman asked that it should have said that he did not make the one who read the longer form go back, but the one who read the regular form read correctly, so why would there be a need to make him go back? He forced his answer. The Korban HaEdah was also forced in this, and his words too are astonishing. Granted, there is a place in the megillah where the longer spelling is written; nevertheless, the reading in every place is the regular form, and if so he read properly, for even in the Torah we follow the read form. Therefore the intent of the Yerushalmi appears to be that it teaches two things: it speaks where the one who read the longer form had a valid megillah, and the one who read the regular form had a Chumash or an invalid megillah. It is explicit in Shulchan Aruch that it is forbidden to assist the cantor from a Chumash, and the later authorities, brought in Magen Avraham, wrote that perhaps the listener will set his mind on this one who reads from the Chumash. Magen Avraham wrote that the reader himself may listen even though he is assisting. It also appears that even regarding others, this is only a precaution initially; after the fact, since we hold that regarding megillah even ten may read at once, because it is beloved they set their minds to it and hear. Nevertheless, it appears that this is only if their reading is the same. But where it is not the same, it is forbidden. According to the initial assumption there in Rosh Hashanah that two voices from two people are not heard, and although the conclusion is that they are heard, that is specifically there, where the one who blows with the trumpets knows that the mitzvah of the day is with the shofar and sets his mind upon it. But this one who reads the regular form thinks that the reader of the longer form is reading in error; if so, one does not set his heart on an error, and he did not set his mind on it. Therefore even he himself has not fulfilled. Either way, he did not set his mind on the regular form of the second reader, and his own reading was from an invalid scroll. Therefore the Yerushalmi teaches that he did not make either of them go back, because the one who read the longer form, even though if the reader omitted a word he has not fulfilled, nevertheless in this case we are not exacting regarding an error; and the second, who read the regular form, even though it was from an invalid scroll, is only a listener, and regarding a listener, if he omitted a word there is no objection. It therefore teaches one case that contains two points, and it works well. From this there is proof for the opinion of the Bach. Analyze this well.<br><b>There, in that same section.</b> "We are not exacting regarding its errors, etc." Note: See what I wrote in my novellae in siman 687, for it appears to me that this is specifically for an individual, but in a congregation he goes back, and we require specifically that he read according to the accepted reading. See there for the reason, and analyze this well.

<b>There, section 17 in the gloss.</b> "They wrote further that the children had the custom to form an image of Haman on pieces of wood and stones, etc., and from this the custom spread that they strike Haman when the megillah is read in the synagogue, etc." Note: See Magen Avraham, seif katan 19, who wrote what he understood from Maharil, that he was not concerned to strike Haman, etc. In my humble opinion this has a source in the Gemara in the first chapter of Megillah, where Rav Nachman bar Yitzchak began that passage from here: "Had Hashem not been with us, let Israel now say; had Hashem not been with us when a man rose against us," a man and not a king. At first glance, what does he need with this, and who does not know that Haman was an ordinary person and not a king? But it appears based on the words of the Yerushalmi at the end of chapter 3 of Megillah, and this is its language there: Rav said: one must say, "Cursed is Haman, cursed are his sons." Rabbi Pinchas said: one must say also, "And Charvonah is remembered for good." Rav Berechyah, etc., when he would reach that verse, "whom Nevuchadnezzar exiled," would say, "May Nevuchadnezzar's bones be ground," for every mention of Nevuchadnezzar in Yirmiyah refers to when he was alive, but here he was dead; end quote of the Yerushalmi. Korban HaEdah in Shirei Korban brought in the name of some who explain that it does not distinguish between Haman before his hanging and afterward, and that it disagrees with Rabbi Yochanan nearby. He wrote that he did not know what forced him to this, for Rav's intent can also be after they read the megillah, after Haman was hanged; see there. I say that I do not know what forced both of them to this, for one case is not comparable to the other. Perforce the intent of Rabbi Yochanan is that he did not say this during Nevuchadnezzar's lifetime but after his death he would say it, because during his lifetime one must apportion honor to royalty, but after his death one need not apportion him honor. So it is in the Midrash on Parashas Vaera, on the verse, "and He commanded them to the children of Israel and to Pharaoh king of Egypt," to apportion honor to royalty; see there. If so, according to this it works well: this is reasonable for Nevuchadnezzar, who was a king, but Haman was not a king, so one need not apportion him honor. Therefore even during his lifetime it is permitted to say, "Cursed is Haman." This is what Rav Nachman bar Yitzchak teaches: "Had Hashem not been with us, etc., when a man rose against us," a man and not a king, and one need not apportion him honor. It is therefore proper to say immediately, "Cursed is Haman." But since it is forbidden to interrupt with speech during the megillah, they strike him, which is not an interruption. If so, this is based on the law of the Gemara and the Yerushalmi, to say even during his lifetime, "Cursed is Haman," and this striking is in place of saying it.
