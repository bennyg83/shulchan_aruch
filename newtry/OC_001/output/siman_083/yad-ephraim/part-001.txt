(In Magen Avraham, note 8) Opposite the seat area, etc. — it appears his intent is that he holds: certainly if moist it is forbidden only the seat area. If so, when one said plainly "this house for a bathroom" and did not designate the seat area, the house is not forbidden at all in such a case — there is no designation; therefore he designates the seat area and points to a place that this shall be the bathroom seat area. If so, when he said regarding the second "and this house" it is difficult: either he did not designate a place for it — then in any case it is not forbidden at all; or he points to a place and says "this place" — then one must again be uncertain: perhaps he meant for ordinary use; perhaps he saw this place clearly and said it is for another use, for the whole house is for another use and not a bathroom — rather necessarily what he points to in a special place in the house and says about it "and this place" is to designate it as the seat area. And again Magen Avraham wrote: he answered that with partitions it is forbidden to recite inside; if so, what is the question, etc. — meaning in Berachos he challenges from Ravina's question: if one designated it for a bathroom, what is its law, against R' Chanina who said a person stands in a bathroom and prays; and the Gemara answers there: to stand inside to pray, but opposite is permitted, as written in note 5. And for the questioner it must be that it seemed to him that Ravina's question too regarding opposite deals with inside — it seems obvious it is forbidden; rather necessarily "opposite" needs to be asked, and it is difficult for him from R' Chanina. And now it is difficult: what is the question — even if we say Ravina's question is not literally about the seat area, which is certainly forbidden, nevertheless one may well say Ravina: although he said plainly "designated for a bathroom," it deals when there are partitions, similar to designating a bathhouse, and he asks whether it is permitted to recite within the partitions opposite the seat area, or perhaps within partitions in such a case is forbidden; and R' Chanina who permits opposite deals when there are no partitions. With this there is no reason to say the questioner erred and thought this law obvious to forbid, and they answered that there it means to stand inside — and he needs to ask whether within partitions is like within the seat area, for why should this be obvious to the questioner? And also accordingly they should have answered: there it deals within partitions and opposite the seat area — but certainly it deals in an old one with partitions too, and there is no difference between inside partitions and outside the partition. And what Magen Avraham wrote further: if partitions help, etc. — at first glance requires study what this has to do with that; and it appears he means: granted, if partitions do not help in a new one regarding inside, then one could say in an old one it is permitted opposite, for the surrounding partitions are not part of the waste body; whereas if we say partitions help for the waste body to forbid all inside partitions — because partitions are made for their interior — whence do we lighten in an old one opposite, lest it be like a cesspool receptacle? And what Magen Avraham wrote: it should challenge the Beit Yosef's question, "and according to your view," etc. — at first glance requires study, for in truth the Gemara answered it deals so; and it appears Magen Avraham holds: granted, if a bathroom has partitions it is reasonable that the questioner deals when there is feces in it and nevertheless it is permitted opposite the partitions, and on this it answers it deals when there is no feces; whereas if we say the bathroom has no partitions, then necessarily the bathroom R' Chanina mentions is one without feces, for otherwise why mention bathroom at all, since the main stringency of a bathroom is because of feces; and if R' Chanina comes to permit when there is feces, it should have said a person stands opposite feces and prays — why mention bathroom? Rather necessarily the questioner too knew it deals when there is no feces; if so there is no question on Rava. And this is Magen Avraham's intent writing it should challenge, "and according to your view, perhaps even though it has no partitions nevertheless it deals when there is no feces" — meaning for him it is reasonable that the questioner thought a plain bathroom has feces, unlike Beit Yosef who necessarily deals when there is feces in it (and Magen Avraham's wording "perhaps" means: perhaps since it deals without feces therefore it is more lenient than feces itself). And one may also say Magen Avraham holds it is not reasonable to say the questioner did not know one could establish it without feces, for certainly the term bathroom applies also without feces (and the wording "perhaps" Magen Avraham wrote is literal); but certainly the questioner too knew this R' Chanina deals without feces, only he holds bathroom law and feces law are equivalent and he did not hold to distinguish between them, as Rav Yosef actually holds here; and he holds that R' Chanina mentioned bathroom to inform us that opposite is permitted but inside is forbidden even without feces, but opposite is at least permitted even though it has feces law; therefore he challenges Ravina and the answer holds there is a difference between a bathroom without feces and one with feces, and he challenges from Rav Yosef; if so, whence does the questioner hold reasonably that partitions too are like a cesspool receptacle and have feces law, and therefore partition interruption does not help.

(Magen Avraham, note 5) And one must say Ravina is uncertain — perhaps R' Perem deals with an old bathroom, etc. One should wonder: if so, why indeed did they not answer that Rav Yosef refers to the mishnah? And it appears because at first glance it is difficult: why did R' Perem not mention in his words that one must distance four amot — but per our way of learning it was reasonable that he thought it deals when there is feces in it; if so the law is obvious in the mishnah that one must distance four amot, and R' Perem as a leniency comes to inform us that even though one stands opposite it one may pray and need not distance as far as the eye can see. But per what they answer when there is no feces — then it is impossible to explain he meant distancing four amot, since nowhere yet was it mentioned to forbid when there is no feces to distance four amot, until one must say: at any rate through distancing it is permitted; and therefore R' Perem's intent is to inform that even without distancing it is also permitted. And as we actually hold in the conclusion: they establish it in a new one — inside specifically forbidden, opposite permitted — namely without distancing. If so, even per what they establish even without feces, it implies even adjacent to the bathroom itself is permitted; and on this he challenges from Rav Yosef. And one cannot say Rav Yosef refers to the mishnah (meaning on what was said that one distances four amot from feces — there it is found that the same law applies to a bathroom, and Rav Yosef says about it: a bathroom that they designated even though there is no feces in it) — that is through the measure of four amot; although one could say so, nevertheless per this answer R' Perem need not distance at all; and therefore Magen Avraham's question returns to its place: why is Ravina uncertain — let him resolve from R' Perem. However, it appears that otherwise it is difficult: let him resolve from Rav Yosef who said a bathroom that they designated even though there is no feces — implying there was feces in it but now there is not; and let him derive that even in a new one where there never was feces it is forbidden; and they answered that in an old one it is obvious to him and in a new one he is uncertain. If so, from R' Perem too one cannot resolve anything — perhaps opposite is obvious to him and inside he is uncertain. Rather certainly this is so, and as Magen Avraham wrote strained to say R' Perem himself is uncertain. If so, let him resolve from Rav Yosef; and one must say on that side of the question that a new one is forbidden: Rav Yosef actually refers to feces law before it too requiring as far as the eye can see, and on this he says the same for a bathroom without feces, unlike a new one where at least one need not distance as far as the eye can see — but always four amot are required. And therefore although we could say R' Perem holds like Rav Yosef's view that a bathroom with feces and without feces are equivalent and four amot are required, and therefore he did not mention four amot in his words — nevertheless it is difficult: for if we say R' Perem relied on this that without feces equals feces per Rav Yosef, then for as far as the eye can see the law is likewise as Rav Yosef — why does R' Perem permit opposite, for this view of Rav Yosef deals regarding as far as the eye can see, whereas for four amot one need not say in a new one too there is prohibition — Ravina's question should be on one side only; and necessarily it deals with as far as the eye can see, and therefore he properly challenges from Rav Yosef on R' Perem. But Ravina properly is uncertain that perhaps a new one is permitted and Rav Yosef refers to the mishnah that even though there is no feces one needs four amot, for it is equivalent for this matter to the law when there is feces — but a new one is entirely permitted, and R' Perem deals without feces. Nevertheless four amot are required — this is obvious to R' Perem: for distancing four amot, without feces equals with feces; only regarding as far as the eye can see he says one may distinguish that without feces a person stands opposite and prays. Further it appears to me per the above path, somewhat differently, in explanation of Magen Avraham's words in siman 83, note 5, who wrote Ravina is uncertain — perhaps it deals with an old one, etc. — for at first glance one should wonder: why indeed did they not answer Rav Yosef refers to the mishnah, and why did they need to answer in a new one; and also whence did the questioner challenge? And it appears to me that at first glance it is difficult otherwise on Magen Avraham's words: how is it possible to say R' Perem deals with an old bathroom without feces — for then it implies distancing four amot is not needed either. Granted for what he thought, that it deals when there is feces in it, Magen Avraham properly wrote he cannot challenge from the mishnah, for one could say R' Perem deals when he distanced four amot because the law is obvious in the mishnah that four amot from feces are required, and R' Perem as leniency comes to inform that even opposite one stands and prays and need not distance as far as the eye can see. Whereas if it deals without feces, this law was not yet mentioned in the mishnah, and from R' Perem's plain wording it implies even four amot are not needed — then although we could say Rav Yosef refers to the mishnah, nevertheless distancing four amot is in any case required, and it is difficult on R' Perem; and therefore it deals, etc.; and Magen Avraham's question returns regarding the Mordechai. And one must say the Mordechai, according to Magen Avraham's intent in his words, holds that what R' Perem said — a person stands opposite a bathroom, etc. — this is as explained that four amot distancing is required, for less than four amot is not called opposite but inside; and therefore what the Gemara answers — Ravina needs to ask to pray inside, opposite is permitted — means at a distance of four amot, for even in a new one four amot are required; only Ravina asks whether opposite is permitted or whether as far as the eye can see is required like other bathrooms without feces. And now one can say he cannot answer Rav Yosef refers to the mishnah; and regarding distancing four amot it was stated: if so, it is difficult — resolve Ravina's question, for from Rav Yosef it implies specifically in a bathroom without feces four amot are required, but at least there was feces in it before and they removed it; but a new one does not even need four amot, for otherwise Rav Yosef should have informed us more that even in a new one distancing is required; and the questioner thought Ravina's question regarding opposite is whether distancing is required, and he should have proven this from Rav Yosef. Rather necessarily Rav Yosef deals regarding as far as the eye can see, that it is required even though there is no feces, and through this a new one is permitted; but four amot perhaps require distancing — and if so it is difficult on R' Perem who said one need not distance as far as the eye can see; and they answered nevertheless they established Ravina's question inside specifically, but opposite is permitted — why did they need to establish R' Perem in a new one; let us say R' Perem deals with an old one and Rav Yosef refers to the mishnah; and nevertheless resolve Ravina regarding inside — this is so, for as I already wrote: what does the answer mean, Ravina needs to ask to pray inside — not literally inside itself, but also within four amot is called inside per the Mordechai's view; and opposite that is permitted is outside four amot. If so, from Rav Yosef it is proven that specifically in a bathroom without feces four amot are required, whereas a new one does not need four amot — and automatically Ravina's question is resolved that it is not reasonable to distinguish between inside itself and outside four amot; and the Gemara in conclusion properly distinguishes between inside and opposite: for an old one as far as the eye can see is required, and in a new one not. But per our way of learning, even in an old one one need not distance except four amot — if so, in a new one too if we say inside is forbidden, obviously four amot are required. Rather necessarily Rav Yosef deals regarding as far as the eye can see, and they had to establish R' Perem in a new one. Unlike Magen Avraham who properly says Ravina's question is not obvious stringently from R' Perem who said opposite — implying outside four amot, as far as the eye can see permitted, whereas inside forbidden — because he holds perhaps in a new one even inside is permitted, and R' Perem deals even with an old one, and Rav Yosef refers to the mishnah; for now that we stand that a new one is permitted inside, Rav Yosef can properly refer to the mishnah — and specifically in an old one without feces distancing is required, but a new one even inside is permitted; and this is Ravina's question: if a new one is forbidden, then R' Perem deals with a new one and only opposite is permitted but inside forbidden. Or perhaps R' Perem deals with an old one and Rav Yosef likewise, and specifically in an old one it is forbidden but a new one permitted; and then the Mordechai who rules the question leniently — accordingly R' Perem deals, and what he said to permit opposite is through distancing — or as Rav Yosef said.
