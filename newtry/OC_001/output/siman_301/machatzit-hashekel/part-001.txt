<b>Se'if katan 1: One should not run, etc.</b> 'Even on a weekday, etc.' Maharsha in Chiddushei Aggados, page 113, asks: If so, what difference is there between Shabbos and a weekday? He answers that on a weekday there is no prohibition, only harm to the eyes; Shabbos adds an actual prohibition. Another practical difference is a blind person, for whom running is nevertheless forbidden on Shabbos.

<b>(ס"ק ג') ומרוצתם כו'</b> דלא כב"ח שכ' ליישב ל' הטור שכ' וז"ל בחורים המתענגים כו'. וכן לראות כ"ד שמתענגים בו מותר לראותו וכ' הרב"י דתיבת מותר בתרא הוא מיותר והב"ח הוסיף דגם תיבת לראות הראשון מיותר ולכן פי' הב"ח דשני הדינים שכ' הטור חלוקים לענין ההיתר דהיתר הריצה אסור לכתחלה ר"ל דלכתחלה אנחנו א"ל שלא ירגיל א"ע להתענג בריצה אבל אם כבר הורגלו בתענוג הריצה אין אוסרי' לו בשבת כי המניעה מהריצה יבטלנו מעונג שבת. וטעם איסור לכתחלה כיון שדרך הבחורים בחול להגביל מקום ורצים ומי שמקדים לרוץ ולבא למקום הגבול יקח כך וכך מעות. וא"כ בשבת אם יראוהו רץ איכא מראית העין שרץ להרויח מעות כדרכם בחול. משא"כ לרוץ לראות דבר המתענג מותר אפי' לכתחלה דבזה ליכא מראית העין כי מי יתן לו ממון על ריצתו לראות דבר שהוא מתענג בו. ולכן חלקם הטור לשני הדינים כיון שחלוקים בדינם ומ"א חולק וס"ל דגם המתענג בריצה מותר לכתחלה ותיבת לראות ומותר המיותרים בטור יתיישב ע"פ מ"ש מ"א בס"ק ד':
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Se'if katan 3: And their running, etc.</b> This is unlike Bach's explanation of Tur. Tur writes: 'Young men who take pleasure, etc.; likewise, one may see anything whose sight gives him pleasure.' Beis Yosef says the latter word 'permitted' is superfluous. Bach adds that the first occurrence of 'to see' is also superfluous. He therefore explains that Tur's two cases differ in the scope of permission. Running for pleasure is not initially permitted: Initially we tell a person not to train himself to derive pleasure from running. If he has already become accustomed to it, however, we do not forbid him to run on Shabbos, because preventing him would detract from his Shabbos pleasure. The initial restriction arises because young men on weekdays designate a finish line and race, with the first to reach it receiving money. If he runs on Shabbos, onlookers may suspect that he is running for money as on a weekday. Running to see something pleasurable, by contrast, is initially permitted, because no one would pay him merely for running to see something he enjoys. Tur therefore separated the two laws because their rulings differ. Magen Avraham disagrees and holds that one who enjoys running may do so initially. Tur's redundant words 'to see' and 'permitted' are resolved by Magen Avraham's explanation in se'if katan 4.

<b>(ס"ק ד) וכן לראות פירוש אע"פ שאסור כו'</b> ובזה א"ש לשון הטור שחלק הדינים דכל אחד הוא ענין היתר אחר דברישא מתיר ריצה שמתענג בו ובסיפא מתיר ראיית דיוקנאות אם מתענג ובסיפא לא דיבר הטור כלל מהיתר ריצה לצורך הראיה והב"ח פי' דמותר לרוץ כו' ודלא כט"ז שאוסר דוקא אם הריצה גופה תענוג לו אבל כאן דאין הריצה תענוג כ"א היא לצורך הראיה שמתענג בה לא הותרה לו הריצה כיון דבשעת הריצה אינו מתענג ומ"מ אפי' לפי פירוש מ"א נראה דמודה לדינא לב"ח דהריצה לצורך הראיה אע"פ שאינו מתענג בריצה מותר וכדמוכח מס"ק ה':
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Se'if katan 4: Likewise, to see; meaning, although it is forbidden, etc.</b> Tur's wording is now sound: He divided the laws because each contains a different permission. First he permits running when the running itself is pleasurable; afterward he permits looking at images when their sight is pleasurable. In the latter clause Tur does not discuss permission to run in order to see them. Bach explains that one may run, etc. This differs from Taz, who permits only when the running itself is pleasurable. Here the running is merely instrumental to the pleasurable sight; during the running itself he has no pleasure, so Taz forbids it. Nevertheless, even according to Magen Avraham's interpretation, it appears that he agrees with Bach's practical ruling that one may run in order to see the sight despite deriving no pleasure from the running, as se'if katan 5 proves.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Se'if katan 5: It is permitted, etc.</b> This requires examination: Does he not derive pleasure from it? At first glance, what is the question? He does not enjoy himself while running, only afterward when exercise makes him perspire. Magen Avraham must therefore hold that permission does not require pleasure during the running itself. From this difficulty concerning the Tosefta, Taz proves that one must enjoy the running at the time. Magen Avraham answers that the case must be, etc., as stated in siman 328: Food eaten by healthy people may be eaten for medicinal purposes. Thus, if he walks in the ordinary manner, even to warm himself or for healing, such walking is common to everyone and resembles ordinary food, which may be eaten medicinally. Running, however, is not everyone's ordinary practice and is therefore forbidden for healing.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Se'if katan 7: To greet, etc.</b> This is derived from the Shunammite woman. Rosh Hashanah 16b states: Rabbi Yitzchak said that a person is obligated to greet his teacher on a festival, as the verse says, 'Why are you going to him today? It is neither the New Moon nor Shabbos.' Her husband said this when she went to Elisha. The Gemara infers that on the New Moon and Shabbos one should go. Magen Avraham asks: Rabbi Yitzchak opens with a festival but concludes with the New Moon and Shabbos; the verse itself mentions the New Moon and Shabbos. Moreover, how do we know there is an obligation rather than merely a mitzvah? Magen Avraham therefore explains that 'on the New Moon and Shabbos one should go' means there is a mitzvah. Rabbi Yitzchak then reasons that on a festival, which occurs only periodically, there is an obligation.

<b>(Seif katan 10) With a sword, even girded, etc.</b> So it also appears from the Gemara, page 63, that the Sages obligate one if he went out with one of these, for they hold that it is a burden and not an ornament; and Rabbi Eliezer exempts, for he holds that they are ornaments. The Gemara asks: What is Rabbi Eliezer’s reason? It answers that it is written, ‘Gird your sword upon your thigh, O mighty one, your glory and your splendor.’ Thus Scripture called it glory and splendor. There it is written ‘gird,’ which implies that the main glory and ornament is when it is girded on his loins. In such a case Rabbi Eliezer exempts, and even in such a case the Sages disagree and obligate.

<b>(ס"ק יב) מפני כו'</b> ת"ל דבשבת לאו זמן תפילין כו' דאי' בשבת דף ס"א ע"א אהא דתנן לא יצא בתפילין א"ר ספרא לא תימא אליביה דמ"ד שבת לאו זמן תפילין אלא אפי' למ"ד שבת זמן תפילין לא יצא דילמא אתי לאתויי ד' אמות בר"ה (והיינו ע"י שיצטרך להסירן כשיכנס לבית הכסא) ולכן הקשה התי"ט דלדידן דקי"ל שבת לאו ז"ת לא הוצרך לטעם מפני שצריך להסירן כו' ע"ז תי' מ"א ניהו דלגבי תפילין לא אצטריך להאי טעמא מ"מ כיון דהטעם אמת נקט להאי טעמא דנ"מ לקמיע שהוא מן המומחה דמותר לצאת בו דהוי תכשיט מ"מ אם כתובים בו שמות אסור לצאת בו כ"א במחופה עור מטעם מפני שצריך להסירן כו':
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Se'if katan 12: Because, etc.</b> One might derive it simply from the fact that Shabbos is not the time for tefillin. Shabbos 61a comments on the Mishnah, 'One may not go out wearing tefillin.' Rav Safra says: Do not think this follows only the view that Shabbos is not the time for tefillin; even according to the view that Shabbos is the time, one may not go out, lest he come to carry them four amos in the public domain when he must remove them to enter the bathroom. Tosafos Yom Tov therefore asks that, for us who rule Shabbos is not the time for tefillin, the reason that he must remove them was unnecessary. Magen Avraham answers that although it is unnecessary regarding tefillin, the reason is true and was stated for its consequence regarding an expert amulet. Such an amulet is an ornament and may ordinarily be worn outside, but if Divine Names are written in it, it may be worn only when covered with leather, because he may need to remove it before entering the bathroom.

According to what is written at the end of siman 308:4, there is no difficulty. Although we rule that Shabbos is not the time for tefillin, this follows Rabbi Akiva's exposition of 'It shall be a sign for you': Tefillin are a sign that we are Hashem's servants and bear His seal like a slave's fetter; Shabbos and Yom Tov are themselves a sign and need no additional sign. If one nevertheless wishes, he may put on tefillin on Shabbos. Thus, even according to our ruling, the reason that he must remove them is needed: Tefillin are not a burden because he may wear them. Rav Safra's wording then needs explanation. What difference is there between the views whether Shabbos is the time for tefillin, if even according to the latter rationale one still needs the concern that he may remove them? Eruvin 96a cites: 'You shall observe this statute at its appointed time, from days to days.' Rabbi Yose HaGelili expounds: days, not nights; 'from days,' not all days, excluding Shabbos and Yom Tov. Rabbi Akiva says this statute refers only to the Paschal offering. The Gemara later cites Rabbi Akiva: One might think a person may put on tefillin on Shabbos and Yom Tov; therefore 'It shall be a sign for you'; those who need a sign, excluding days that are themselves a sign. Tosafos writes that Rabbi Yose HaGelili relies upon the nearby exposition from 'a sign upon your hand.' Their intent is that 'from days' merely indicates some days without tefillin; why choose Shabbos and Yom Tov rather than other days? The verse about a sign identifies them. If so, why is the exclusion 'from days' needed, since Shabbos and Yom Tov are already excluded by 'It shall be a sign'? It must be as Magen Avraham writes: The verse about a sign teaches only that Shabbos and Yom Tov do not require tefillin, but one may wear them voluntarily; 'from days' teaches that it is forbidden. Rav Safra therefore says: Do not think the Mishnah follows only the view that Shabbos is not a tefillin time; meaning Rabbi Yose HaGelili, who prohibits wearing them. Even according to the view that Shabbos is a tefillin time and one is even obligated to wear them, one may not go out lest he carry them four amos. We rule like Rabbi Akiva, under whom one may voluntarily wear them on Shabbos and Yom Tov; Shulchan Aruch therefore needed the reason that he may have to remove them.

The Mishnah on page 62 says that she may not go out with a pierced needle or a sealed ring; if she does, she is liable. Ulla says, 'Their rules are reversed for a man.' The poskim dispute what Ulla meant. All agree he certainly referred to rings: For a man, an unsealed ring incurs liability and a sealed ring is exempt. Whether it is initially permitted is disputed. Rashi forbids it; Rabbeinu Tam and Rambam permit it initially. The concern that a wearer may remove an ornament to show it and carry it four amos applies to women, who are ostentatious; a man does not ordinarily show off his jewelry. They agree, however, that an item serving as jewelry for both men and women is forbidden to a man too, because the Sages made no distinction, as stated in se'if 9 and in the Yerushalmi cited below in se'if katan 27. These are the two views in Shulchan Aruch: The first is Rashi and Maggid Mishneh; the reversed view is Rif. Bach explains Maggid Mishneh's equality to mean that both a pierced and an unpierced needle are exempt for a man. This does not apply to a man, who is therefore always exempt. Magen Avraham responds that this is not compelled. Rashi and Maggid Mishneh may mean that men and women are equally exempt for an unpierced needle and equally liable for a pierced one.

It then gives two explanations: 'It was made for this and for that,' and 'The same applies to a man and a woman.' The first means that an object made both as an ornament and for practical use, such as a key, is forbidden to both men and women. The second means that an ornament made for both men and women is forbidden to a man as well: Since women are ostentatious and may remove it to show it, the Sages made no distinction and prohibited it for men too. Both interpretations are accepted, as stated in se'ifim 9 and 11. This implies that a purely male ornament having no practical use is permitted even in the hand. Magen Avraham therefore writes that 'in his hand' is not literal. Yet the Yerushalmi plainly proves that an ornament may be forbidden in the hand. If carrying an ornament in the hand were permitted, why would they rebuke him because he might remove it to display it? The ordinary cases of women's ornaments would not prove this, because those objects may be ornaments only when worn, whereas a cane or gold key is ornamental specifically when held. The Yerushalmi therefore establishes that even such an object is forbidden.

Accordingly, nowadays one may be lenient even with sounding bells, because their prohibition is only a safeguard lest one repair an instrument.

Tosafos on Shabbos 61b explains Rav Pappa's discussion. If a physician wrote three amulets for three people and each amulet cured three times, it is obvious that both the physician and the amulets are established as expert. Rav Pappa asks about three amulets written for one person, each curing him once. The individual amulets are not expert; is the physician expert, or should all three cures be attributed to that patient's favorable fortune in responding to written amulets? Rashi says that when one amulet cures the same person three times, the amulet is expert, because it is more reasonable to attribute the cures to a correctly prepared amulet than to the patient's fortune. When there are three different letters, however, the possibilities are either the physician or the patient. Rashba asks about the obvious case: How can the third amulet become expert? As soon as it cures the third patient once, the physician has already become expert, so its next two cures might be due to the physician rather than the amulet. Tosafos answers that the two statuses of expertise take effect together. This answer assumes that Rav Pappa's unresolved question is decided to mean that three letters curing one patient do not establish the physician, because the cures are attributed to the patient. Maharsha explains that Tosafos's answer works only under that assumption. If Rav Pappa's question were resolved in favor of the physician's expertise, the third amulet could never become expert, since the physician's status would precede it. Later authorities ask: We treat Rav Pappa's unresolved question stringently and do not deem the physician expert. In the obvious case, then, how can we call the third amulet expert, since perhaps the other side of Rav Pappa's question is correct and the physician became expert first? To treat the physician as expert would itself be a stringency here. The answer is that if the physician were certainly expert, Maharsha's point would be correct. Where the matter remains doubtful, however, the third amulet may still become expert. In the obvious case, at least one of them; the physician or the amulet; is certainly expert, and when the two possibilities are evenly balanced we treat both as expert. Rav Pappa's question differs because there it is possible that neither the physician nor any amulet is expert, for all the cures may depend upon the patient's fortune; we therefore rule stringently. Although Rashi indicates that a cure is generally more attributable to the amulet than to the physician, the physician has the countervailing advantage that his expertise may have begun earlier, together with the possibility that Rav Pappa's question should be resolved in his favor. The considerations balance, so both are deemed expert. Maharsha too would disagree only if Rav Pappa's question were certainly resolved in favor of the physician; while it remains doubtful, both may be expert.

<b>(ס"ק מט) בב' מלבושים כו'</b> עסי' כ"ג ר"ל דמבואר שם דדבר המחובר בבגד בטל לגבי בגד. ודע דמ"ש רמ"א בהגהה ומותר ללבוש שני כובעי' זע"ז כתב בד"מ וז"ל ובא"ז כתב דמותר לצאת בשני כובעים שכן דרכו בחול לשום כובע קטן עכ"ל נלע"ד דר"ל כובע על מיצא"ל או מה שקורין שלאפ"ל דגם זה קרא הא"ז כובע קטן ולכן התיר דכן דרכו בחול וכן כוונת רמ"א שמקורו מן א"ז הנ"ל אבל ב' כובעים ממש זע"ז ודאי אסור דאין דרך לילך כך בחול:
**** ENGLISH ****
Rama speaks of two hats. Darkei Moshe cites Or Zarua as permitting two because on weekdays people commonly put on a small cap. It appears that this means a cap worn beneath a mitzal or shlafel, which is called a small hat. Two ordinary hats worn one atop the other are forbidden, because that is not the weekday manner of dress.
