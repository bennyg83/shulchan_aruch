<b>ס"ג יכול לדלג ולקפוץ כו' ומוטב שידלג.</b> רש"י שם מותר אף לדלג. אסור לקפוץ. ש"ד לדלג. ונראה כוונתו דקשה בגמ' דתליא אם יכלו כו' דתלי ביכול ואין יכול ול"ק שאסור להניח מ' דבמקום שיכול אף לדלג כו' מותר ובמקום שאינו יכול אף אם אין מדלג אסור והיאך אפשר וצ"ל בקופץ שעוקר ב' רגליו כא' כמ"ש בירושלמי סוף י"ט והביאו הג"א שם וע' ר"מ בפי' פ"ח דאהלות מתני' ה' וסובר דקיפוץ קל מדילוג שהאיסור כאן שמפסק רגליו בהרחבה כעין פסיעה גסה שאמר שם אלא כו' מ' דברישא האיסור ג"כ בכה"ג וז"ש ש"ד לדלג דאף לדלג מותר וכן כאן ל' הטור ומוטב שידלג כו' ולא כ' אסור להקיף דודאי אם לא * לדלג מותר להקיף אבל אצל לעבור כ' ואסור לעבור דלעולם אסור לדבר הרשות כמ"ש בס"ד ע"ש:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Se'if 3: He may step over and jump, etc., and it is better that he step over.</b> Rashi there writes that it is even permitted to step over, but forbidden to jump; stepping over is permitted. It appears that his intent is as follows: the Gemara is difficult, for it makes the matter depend on whether they were able, etc., meaning on whether he can or cannot, and it does not simply say that it is forbidden to place forty se'ah there. For where he can, even stepping over is permitted, and where he cannot, even if he does not step over it is forbidden. How can this be? One must say that it refers to jumping, where he lifts both feet at once, as stated in the Yerushalmi at the end of Yom Tov, cited by Hagahos Ashri there; see also the Rambam's commentary to Ohalos, chapter 8, mishnah 5. He holds that jumping is lighter than stepping over, for the prohibition here is that he spreads his legs widely, like the large stride mentioned there. But the beginning also implies that the prohibition is in such a case, and this is why he writes that stepping over is permitted, meaning that even stepping over is permitted. So too here the Tur writes, 'and it is better that he step over,' etc., and did not write that it is forbidden to go around, for certainly if he cannot step over it is permitted to go around. But regarding crossing through, he wrote that it is forbidden to cross, for it is always forbidden for a mundane purpose, as written in se'if 4; see there.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Provided, etc., so that, etc.</b> The Rosh and the Ran there asked from the above, se'if 2, where it says that it is forbidden to cross, etc. They answered that since they permitted it only in the manner in which he is dressed, as it says below, 'provided that he not,' etc., the matter is recognizable and is not like ordinary carrying. The Ran brought proof from chapter 2 of Yom Tov, folio 18a: Rav Yosef said it is a decree lest he wring it out; they challenged him from the law of a niddah, etc., who immerses while wearing her garments. The answer is: there it is different, for since it was permitted to her only by means of wearing it, she remembers. It is clear from their words that the prohibition is not because of a burden, as Rashi explained, but because of recognizability. This is what is meant by 'provided,' etc. It does not mean changing from the usual manner of wearing them; on the contrary, he must go in the manner of wearing them. Rather, it is a change from the usual manner of crossing through water, for people are not accustomed to go through water in the manner of wearing them. Therefore, according to the Rosh, the reason that he should not take them out, etc., is because of the required change. For if the concern were burden, what does the answer 'since' accomplish, when there too it is needed in that manner? Rather, now that we say it is because of a change, unlike his ordinary crossing through water in garments, there, where the garments do not reach the water, as it says there that sometimes they are lifted, etc., it indicates that when this happens only occasionally, there is no change. The Tur learned from the Rosh's wording, 'since they permitted,' etc., that because of the water itself they did not permit it, unlike Rashi's explanation; otherwise he should have said, 'since it is forbidden except,' etc. The wording 'they permitted' implies that it is a special dispensation, as in Yom Tov there, 'since it was permitted to her,' etc., unlike the Beis Yosef in siman 613. They hold that it is not considered a burden, as will be explained in se'if 31; see there.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Because he needs.</b> This is the language of the Tur. It requires study, for we rule that Shabbos is not the time for tefillin, and the Rambam omitted this reason; he wrote only the latter clause, that one who goes out with tefillin is exempt, and some teach, etc. After analysis it appears that although there it says, 'Do not say this according to the one who says,' etc., and we rule that Shabbos is not the time for tefillin, that statement there is according to Rabbi Yose HaGelili in Menachos 36b, who derives it from 'as a sign.' But we rule like Rabbi Akiva, who derives it from 'and you shall guard,' as stated there; he holds that it is a halachah but we do not instruct so, and there is no prohibition against putting them on on Shabbos and Yom Tov, as Tosafos write in Niddah 51b, s.v. The Rambam, who did not write this, follows his own view that night is not the time for tefillin, for he wrote that one transgresses a negative commandment. The Tur follows his own view that it is the time for tefillin, as written in simanim 29 and 30. And that which is said in Menachos there, 'doubtful darkness,' etc., 'but certainly,' etc., applies even according to Rabbi Akiva, as stated there, namely when one puts them on for the sake of the mitzvah of tefillin, as the Magen Avraham writes in siman 29; see Tosafos there.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>If there are, etc.; and they are, etc.</b> This follows Rava there, who holds that a person does trouble himself, for he understands that 'and now' is only a rejection. Therefore, 'tied' is not because of the trouble involved, for if so he would not have said 'and now,' etc.; rather, the concern of trouble is sufficiently addressed by the straps alone, and Rava too agrees to this. What he says 'and now' does not mean that it proves against Rava, but that there is no proof for Rava, since in any case we require them to be tied. But Rava himself does not hold so. The practical difference is that if they have no straps at all, one need not even wait until nightfall, unlike where they are not tied; therefore the Tur wrote both. I will explain the view of the Rif and the Rosh, and the Rambam in chapter 2 of Hilchos Tzitzis, halachah 7, will be resolved. There the Rambam ruled that one who finds techeles in the marketplace, even if the threads are spun and cut, it is invalid. The Raavad objected that this contradicts the conclusion of Eruvin 97a, 'and now,' etc., where all agree that a person does not trouble himself, etc.; if so the Gemara's law is the reverse, that they are valid. The sages of Lunel asked the Rambam this question, and he answered in his responsum: certainly the truth is as you say, and I erred, etc.; and they corrected the text; see there. But the truth shows its way: his original words are clear and valid, and he did not err at all. In his responsum the Rambam overlooked the Rif's method in chapter 10 of Eruvin there, on the mishnah of one who finds tefillin. The Rif wrote: why not with new ones? Perhaps they are amulets, etc.; but with old ones, since they are tied, a person does not trouble himself, etc. And the father of Rav Shmuel bar Yitzchak taught: what are new ones, etc.? Those with straps and tied are old, etc.; those without straps and not tied are new. So too wrote the Rosh there. At first glance these two reasons contradict one another, for the reason of amulets is because a person does trouble himself, while the statement of the father of Rav Shmuel bar Yitzchak necessarily holds that a person does not trouble himself, as stated in the Gemara, 'and now,' etc. Rather, their method in this sugya is as follows: the mishnah is necessarily not like Rabbi Meir, who said both new and old, etc.; nor is it like Rabbi Yehudah, for it is not reasonable to establish the mishnah like Rabbi Yehudah and not according to halachah, since Rabbi Yehudah follows his own view that a bow is a full knot, as Abaye said; Abaye's answer is more reasonable than Rav Chisda's answer there, as Tosafos objected there, that it is not reasonable to establish it like Rabbi Yehudah but not for his reason. Rather, the mishnah is not like Rabbi Meir or Rabbi Yehudah, as above. The mishnah holds that a person does trouble himself, because of the concern of an amulet, and it also accepts what the father of Rav Shmuel bar Yitzchak taught, 'what are new,' etc., but not for his reason of tying; rather, because of trouble. Anything not tied gives us reason to suspect that perhaps it is an amulet, for a person does trouble himself, etc. A compelling proof that the mishnah holds that a person does trouble himself is that otherwise, how could it teach that with new ones he is exempt? They are tefillin. If it held that a person does not trouble himself, etc., it should have said: but with new ones, or if he found them, etc., he waits until nightfall, etc. Rather, the mishnah necessarily holds that a person does trouble himself. That which Rava said, 'whether a person troubles himself or does not trouble himself is a dispute of Tannaim,' is according to the baraisa of Ahavah the son of Rabbi Zeira and Rabbi Elazar, who established it as a Tannaic dispute of Rabbi Meir and Rabbi Yehudah. The conclusion that everyone holds, etc., is also according to that baraisa, and Rabbi Elazar established them according to everyone, namely Rabbi Meir and Rabbi Yehudah, who both hold that a person does not trouble himself. But the mishnah necessarily holds that a person does trouble himself. According to this, the words of the Rif and Rosh are clear: they brought the reason of amulet regarding the mishnah, and also 'what are,' etc., to teach what counts as new. The Rambam's method is exactly like the Rif, as is his usual way, and the Rosh also follows their method, as above. It follows that according to the Rif, the Rosh, and the Rambam, even if they have straps we suspect they are an amulet until they are tied, as above. The practical difference is for danger from bandits, whether one may carry them less than four amos at a time or guard them. According to the Raavad, even without straps we do not suspect this, as above. The Baal HaMaor also objected to the Rif as above; see Milchamos Hashem, who reconciled the Rif in another way.
