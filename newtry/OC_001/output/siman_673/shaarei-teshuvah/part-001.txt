<b>Valid.</b> See Baer Heitev, who wrote in the name of Shaar Ephraim, and copied this from Leket HaKemach, who wrote so. With pardon, what he wrote in his name, that it is permitted, is actually the opposite, for in truth he concludes there that it is forbidden to kindle with it for the Chanukah lamp, which is forbidden to use by its light. Although here there is only benefit of the mitzvah, and mitzvos were not given for benefit, nevertheless it is forbidden to kindle with it. Even though we do not rule like Razah, who holds that rabbinic mitzvos were given for benefit, nevertheless one may say that since a Chanukah lamp requires a measure, meaning that one must put enough oil in the lamp to burn for the required measure, and since meat and milk is forbidden for benefit, its measure is considered crushed. Therefore it is also forbidden to kindle with orlah oil. Do not say that this applies specifically to orlah, which is among the items that must be burned, whereas with meat and milk, which is among the items that must be buried, we do not say its measure is crushed. Nor should one say here that all items that must be buried may not be burned because there the reason is the ashes, whereas with kindling a Chanukah lamp there are no ashes except the ash of the wick, and the wick was not forbidden for benefit. Nevertheless, one must forbid it, for even if the reason of ashes does not apply here, the rule that all items that must be buried may not be burned is not nullified. It is proven in several places that because a reason is absent, the law is not nullified, as written in Rivash, siman 34. One may also say that when buried items are burned, the rule of crushed measure also applies to them; see there. See Eliyah Zuta, who wondered about this, because in any case it is forbidden because of cooking meat and milk, for in siman 318 we hold that there is cooking after cooking; see there. In Responsa Shevus Yaakov, part 1, siman 38, he was asked about this by one scholar, who answered Eliyah Zuta's words that here, when the head of the wick burns, it burns and consumes the prohibition, and this is not called cooking of meat and milk, meaning cooking through which it becomes edible, as stated in Chullin 28. Shevus Yaakov answered that before the prohibition is consumed, it is fit to eat because of the heating, for even heating in a secondary vessel is called cooking regarding meat and milk. These words are astonishing in my humble opinion; see there further, where he wrote that Chullin 24 implies this to be forbidden by Torah law. Greater than this we find regarding inserting a spoon, etc. See Yam Shel Shlomo, chapter Gid HaNasheh, siman 45, and Pri Megadim in the introduction to meat and milk, that hot with hot without liquid is not a Torah prohibition and is permitted for benefit; see there. This is not the place for it. Furthermore, even if there is room to question and say that it is not complete cooking, nevertheless Eliyah Zuta properly wondered that they did not raise this concern at all. Until here. See Pri Megadim, Yoreh Deah, siman 87, and here in this siman, who noted regarding the words of Shaar Ephraim that this is frying, and Maharam Schiff wrote in his novellae to Chullin that frying is rabbinic. So too in Soles LaMinchah, rule 85, he wrote that butter mixed with milk may be used for lighting, because frying is not a Torah prohibition; see there and in the introduction. Nor should one say that what was absorbed in the pot became forbidden for benefit and then was emitted back into the butter and prohibited it, for this is not so. Absorbed meat and milk does not prohibit the butter for benefit, since in lighting one does not benefit from this absorption, as we say regarding a pile of wheat, etc.; see Pri Megadim's introduction to meat and milk there. See Orach Chayim, laws of Pesach, regarding wheat found in a chicken; in Magen Avraham I wrote at length about this. What Baer Heitev wrote in the name of Shevus Yaakov briefly, see there, where it is explained that even if one's regular practice is always to kindle with olive oil, and he came home and did not find olive oil, and began preparing himself with wax candles, he should finish the mitzvah with wax candles, since he has already begun with them. Because of a preferred mitzvah, one should not disgrace the first mitzvah, as implied in Yoma 7, regarding one who was standing and offering the Omer meal-offering, etc. Similarly, Beis Yaakov, siman 26, wrote regarding chalitzah when the older brother came after they had already begun the order of chalitzah with the younger one; see there. In Chacham Tzvi he rejected his proof, because here it is not called beginning the mitzvah; he merely made a preparation and designation by affixing the candles to the wall. He gave further reasons, etc. Therefore it appears that he must withdraw from the wax candles, even if he already affixed them in their place, and kindle with olive oil. He brought proof from Yoma, chapter Hotzi'u Lo, where Rav Pappa said the first is sent, etc., and wrote at great length. He wrote that it is not comparable to Menachos 89, since he began with the applications he finishes, because there, since he began the actual mitzvah, he should not reject this one for another, but to fulfill the mitzvah itself in the preferred manner, etc. He explains Maharil as holding that one does not fulfill the Chanukah lamp with wax. Even where the practice follows Rema, the reason is that olive oil is not so available for them; but when it is available, one should no longer be in doubt, etc. There is no distinction whether until now he was accustomed to olive oil or not. What Shevus Yaakov wrote regarding beautification of a mitzvah up to one-third is only where he has not yet begun the mitzvah, but this is not correct. If so, one who has an esrog in his hand and they bring him another, even for free, that is a thousand times more beautiful than the first, would be forbidden to set aside the first, for in chapter Hatadir we say that when its time arrives it is like slaughtered, and so too when the eastern sky lights on the first day, etc. Furthermore, if he already took an esrog that is not beautiful in his hand to bless, and before he blessed they brought him a free gift of one more beautiful than any other, should he bless on the first one that is not beautiful? Let such a thing be forgotten. The matter is clear, both with esrog and with Chanukah and with all mitzvos in the world: one blesses on the beautiful and superior one even if it came last. In Shevus Yaakov, part 2, siman 35, he became somewhat an advocate for his words and wrote that this preparation is certainly a significant beginning, as we say regarding a haircut, from when he places the cloth, etc. Nevertheless, it is clear there that he agrees that one who wants to perform the mitzvah in the preferred manner has permission to do so. He wrote that his proofs in part 1 were only to say that one need not disgrace the first mitzvah, etc.; see there. Baer Heitev brought this briefly. I saw fit to copy the roots of the matter, its reasons and explanations. It seems that if he already began the blessing, even though the olive oil with the wick is already prepared before him and he need not work on it, nevertheless since he began blessing over this, he finishes. There is also doubt whether he needs to bless anew, as explained in siman 206; see there. The same applies regarding an esrog in such a case. It also seems that with an esrog on the first day, where the one that is not beautiful is his, and he took it in hand to bless, and meanwhile his friend comes and wants to give him a more beautiful one as a gift on condition that it be returned, one can properly rely on Shevus Yaakov. Although a gift on condition to return is effective, nevertheless it is better that he fulfill the mitzvah with what is truly his. It seems that Chacham Tzvi too agrees to this. What Baer Heitev wrote in the name of Responsa Shaar Ephraim, see Birkei Yosef, who questions this and wrote that since the concern that they will say he kindled it for his own need does not apply, only that they will say he is not among the mehadrin, etc., one should not decree. Also, since each person placed his lamps in a designated place, and since the recognition is for the members of the household and they certainly know, so it seems to me to be lenient. See siman 675 in Beis Yosef in the name of Mahari Abuhav. Until here. See Eliyah Zuta, that nevertheless it is proper to kindle one night with wax and one night with oil; see there. What he wrote in the name of Shevus Yaakov, in a place where olive oil is expensive, etc., seems from this that he disagrees with what he wrote earlier in the name of Shaar Ephraim, that one should not kindle partly from olive oil and partly from other oils. In Responsa Shevus Yaakov there, his words are garbled, and this is how one should read them: for this reason, one who is held among non-Jews and will not be able to kindle lamps should kindle one each night. Meaning, even if he has oil for eight days, he should not kindle on the second day like the mehadrin, and likewise on the other nights; rather, he should kindle one each night. What Shaar Ephraim wrote in siman 39, that one should not compare this to a partition of five, etc., nevertheless, since here the main mitzvah is only one lamp and the matter is not so recognizable at the time of kindling, etc.; see there. This is how one should read it.

<b>Coins.</b> See Baer Heitev. See Pri Chadash in the name of Maharikash, that walking by the light of the Chanukah lamp in order to see that one does not stumble is permitted, for one is not obligated to close his eyes, and this is not called use. See above, siman 263, Magen Avraham, paragraphs 13 and 14; see there.

<b>Holiness.</b> See Baer Heitev and what Turei Zahav wrote on this. See Responsa Shevus Yaakov, part 1, siman 39, and there in siman 40 the responsum of the great Rabbi David Oppenheim, of blessed memory, and what he wrote in Kuntres Moed Deyad printed in the sefer Beis Yehudah; see there. See also Responsa Shemesh Tzedakah, siman 30, two responsa on the matter, and there the laws of doubtful blessings are explained. See Birkei Yosef in the name of Mahari Molcho, that if one needs to examine a law about a doubt that arose to him concerning the lamps that he kindled, and he has no other lamp, it is permitted to examine that law by the light of the Chanukah lamp, for many great authorities permitted mitzvah use. Even according to the one who forbids, nevertheless for its own need one may permit it, similar to sleeping in the synagogue on the night of Yom Kippur for the need of the synagogue, and likewise regarding the red heifer, anything for its own need is not labor; see there. His proof can be rejected. But since it is temporary use, it seems obvious that it is permitted.
