<b>Section 1.</b> The oils and wicks are valid for the Chanukah lamp, etc. Note: we read in the Talmud: Rav Huna said, all oils, etc., may not be used for Chanukah, whether on a weekday or on Shabbos. He holds that if it went out he is required to tend to it, and it is permitted to use its light. Tosafos asked: from where do we know that he holds it is permitted to use its light? Perhaps the reason is that if it went out he is required to tend to it, and on Shabbos he cannot kindle it. They answered that if so, he should have said plainly, they may not be used for Chanukah. Since he said whether on Shabbos, it proves that on Shabbos there is another reason to forbid it, because perhaps he will tilt it, etc. At first glance this is not understood, for in the end, why does he need to teach this? Since because of if it went out he is required to tend to it alone it is also forbidden on Shabbos, why did he need to mention whether on Shabbos? To say that he teaches this so that we will know that it is permitted to use its light is difficult. Besides being forced, for he should have stated explicitly that it is permitted to use its light, there is a stronger difficulty: Tosafos themselves wrote that Rav said one may kindle whether on a weekday or on Shabbos, and did not say plainly, because if he had spoken plainly I would have thought only on a weekday and not on Shabbos, for the initial assumption would have been that no amora would hold that it is forbidden to use its light. If external reasoning says it is permitted to use it, to the point that Rav needed to teach us that it is forbidden, then the one who says it is permitted to use it would not need to teach this, since reasoning already says so, for no person would say that it is forbidden to use its light. Rather, the intent seems as follows. Since he said plainly, they may not be used for Chanukah, this proves that even mehadrin min hamehadrin may not use them, even for the additional lamps. At first glance this is difficult. Since we hold in several places that where after the fact it is valid without this, ab initio we are not concerned lest one forget, then granted, with one lamp, since he holds that if it went out he is required to tend to it, then even after the fact he has not fulfilled the Chanukah lamp, and therefore one must be concerned lest he forget to kindle it afterward. But regarding the mehadrin lamp, since after the fact even if he forgets there is no concern, it should be permitted to kindle the additional lamps of the mehadrin with these oils. Why did he say plainly that they may not be used for Chanukah? Perforce, he holds that it is permitted to use its light. If so, on Shabbos it is automatically forbidden to kindle even the additional lamps, because since it is permitted to use its light, perhaps he will tilt it. Therefore even on a weekday it is forbidden to kindle with these oils even for the additional lamps, because if it were permitted to kindle the additional lamps with them, people would say that the reason one may not kindle with them on Shabbos is also only because if it went out he must tend to it. They would then mistakenly kindle the additional lamps with these oils on Shabbos just as on a weekday, while in truth there is a concern that he may tilt. Therefore even on a weekday one may not kindle with them at all, even for the additional lamps, as a decree because of Shabbos. This proves that he holds it is permitted to use its light, for without this, even on Shabbos it would be permitted to use these oils for the additional lamps, and he would not have said plainly that they may not be used for Chanukah. Thus it proves that he holds it is permitted to use its light, and the question of Tosafos is resolved; examine it. <b>There in the same section in the gloss.</b> And the practice in these countries is to kindle with wax candles, because their light is clear like oil. Note: regarding the law of one who was affixing wax Chanukah candles to the wall and then olive oil was brought to him, what should he do? The great authorities of the world disagreed. The view of Shevus Yaakov is that he should finish with the wax candles, while the view of Chacham Tzvi in responsum 44 is the opposite. Since this is Torah and I need to learn, I too, the poor one, will answer according to my share in this matter. First, it seems possible to bring proof from the sugya in chapter 2 of Shabbos, from the words of Tosafos on the question whether kindling performs the mitzvah or placement performs the mitzvah. Tosafos wrote there that the practical difference is if a deaf-mute, mentally incompetent person, or minor kindled it. Now it is not difficult that they should have said the practical difference is if it went out between kindling and placement: if kindling performs the mitzvah, he already performed the mitzvah, and we hold that if it went out he is not required to tend to it; but if placement performs the mitzvah, he has not yet performed the mitzvah and must go back and kindle. This can be answered by saying that they wanted to state a practical difference according to everyone, even according to the one who says that if it went out he is required to tend to it. One may also say that before placement, according to all, if it went out he is required to tend to it, even if kindling performs the mitzvah, for at any rate we require a recognizable sign that it is for Chanukah, and we hold that one who was holding a Chanukah lamp and standing has done nothing, as explicit in the Talmud, because one who sees says that he kindled it for his own need. If so, as long as he has not placed it, according to all he is required to tend to it. But this is difficult: they should have said that the practical difference is if he was kindling wax candles, and after the kindling olive oil became available. If kindling performs the mitzvah, then he already kindled and it is not relevant to kindle a second time. But if placement performs the mitzvah, he has not yet performed the mitzvah, and he is obligated to withdraw his hand and kindle olive oil. Since they did not write this, it proves like Shevus Yaakov. Therefore, even if placement performs the mitzvah, since he has at least begun their performance, he is no longer obligated to withdraw from them, and therefore they could not state this practical difference; examine it. Now let us turn to the words of Chacham Tzvi. Although he rejected the proofs of Shevus Yaakov well, nevertheless there is no proof in the opposite direction. What he brought as proof from what Rabbi Nachman said, I agree where there are leftovers for eating, proves that where there are no leftovers for eating one does not say to bring another, is no proof. But Shevus Yaakov speaks of a case where delay is not needed. Then one may say that he must withdraw his hand in order to perform the preferred mitzvah. The proof he brought from the end of chapter Hotzi'u Lo, from the fact that the mitzvah is with the first, he himself already saw that this proof can be rejected, for one may say that there the first was already fit. What he brought as proof from what is brought there, that if the second is more choice than it he should bring it, and from what is written in Shevuos 11, if he found another nicer than it, and from untying from garment to garment, etc., all of this seems to be rejectable with one rejection. Certainly where the beautification is recognizable in the object itself, he is obligated to beautify and pursue beautification, even if he already began dealing with another, for what does the body of the mitzvah lose by that? Any time the beautification is recognizable in the body of the mitzvah, this is honor for the mitzvah, and this we learn from This is my God and I will beautify Him. In that case he is certainly obligated to do so even if he already began with another. But where the beautification is not in the body of the mitzvah, only from the perspective of the owners, upon whom it is incumbent to do a mitzvah in the preferred manner, such as in our case, where from the viewer's perspective there is no difference between olive oil and a wax candle, and the advantage of olive oil is only because the miracle occurred with oil and because it is similar to the Menorah, then the preferred mitzvah is not because of beautification in the body of the mitzvah but because of the performer. This is not learned at all from This is my God and I will beautify Him. In such a case one may well say that once he already began with another item, he is not obligated to withdraw and go back to do it another way, even if it would be a mitzvah in the preferred manner. This is correct in my humble opinion, for Chacham Tzvi compared separate matters. Therefore as law it would appear like Shevus Yaakov. But there is one point to raise from Yevamos 39, where it says: come and hear, if he made the matter depend on a minor until he grows up, we do not listen to him. Let us say that since now they are dealing with the adult, they should not stop from him and go after others. This proves that because of beautification of the mitzvah one stops from one matter to another. One cannot say that there it speaks where they had not begun dealing with him at all, for still, what is the question? Let us say that the Mishnah speaks where they already began dealing with yibbum or chalitzah with this one who is here, and afterward he made it depend on the adult or minor, and therefore we do not listen to him. However, one may say that there it proves perforce that it speaks where they had not begun dealing with him at all; for if they had begun, why mention specifically that the adult is overseas or the minor is still truly a minor? Even if he were nearby and fit for yibbum and chalitzah, they would not stop from the one with whom they began in order to perform the preferred mitzvah. But if you say that he does not even want chalitzah, etc. This implies that even according to the initial assumption, before it knew that the mitzvah rests upon him, nevertheless since all of them wanted chalitzah, he comes first. Perforce, Rashi holds that because of beautification of the mitzvah too, we go from the matter being dealt with to the matter not being dealt with, to perform the preferred mitzvah. But Tosafos there wrote that even if they did not want yibbum but only chalitzah, it could have asked. This implies that they hold like Shevus Yaakov. Did the questioner not know what is explicit in the verse and Mishnah, that the mitzvah of yibbum is with the oldest, and that this is beautification of the mitzvah? Even so, it proves that because of beautification of the mitzvah one does not stop from what is before us to return to what is not before us. The Talmud answers that the mitzvah is with the oldest, meaning that yibbum is different, because the verse states, and the firstborn whom she bears. Therefore whenever the oldest is possible and before us, it is as though the mitzvah did not take effect at all on the younger brother, and this is not merely beautification of the mitzvah but considered the body of the mitzvah. But generally, where one fulfills with another item and the second is only beautification of the mitzvah, one should not stop from what he began to go back after another item. There too the beautification is not recognizable in the body of the mitzvah, exactly like the Chanukah lamp and olive oil. Therefore Tosafos follow their own view, and for this reason in Shabbos too they did not write this practical difference. Examine it. <b>There, section 2.</b> Kindling performs the mitzvah; therefore, if it went out before its time passed, he is not required to tend to it, etc. Note: the language is astonishing, as Turei Zahav wondered, and we already wrote several ways in our novellae. Now it seems as follows, based on what Beis Yosef wrote to resolve why in siman 672 they wrote that leftover oil in the Chanukah lamp may be benefited from, while in siman 677 he ruled the opposite. Beis Yosef answers that if it remains from the measure needed for lighting, it is forbidden to use, but if more than the required measure remains, one may benefit from it. At first glance it seems possible to add flavor to this matter: since we hold that nothing whose mitzvah has been performed remains subject to me'ilah, and for this reason the ash of all burned items is permitted, here too, if it burned for the required measure and more remains, its mitzvah has been performed, and therefore it is permitted for benefit. But if the required measure remains, then its mitzvah has not been performed, and therefore it is forbidden for benefit. However, the fact that Beis Yosef did not write this reason appears to be because we hold that kindling performs the mitzvah, and if it went out he is not required to tend to it. If so, immediately upon kindling the mitzvah was performed, and even if the required measure remains, its mitzvah has been performed. He therefore had to give another reason for this. See what I wrote about this regarding a Torah scroll that wore out and was erased, whether one may write mundane text on it, in a responsum to the holy community of Botishan. In any event, according to this one may say as follows. Generally, we find many times that they forbade keeping prohibited items lest one come to a mishap, as in the first chapter of Pesachim regarding making it into perfume; see there. If so, here too it would have been proper to say that if it went out within the time, he is required to relight it immediately, since it is forbidden for benefit and there is concern that perhaps one will come to a mishap with it. But one may say that in a rabbinic matter they were lenient. Yet we find several matters where they strengthened their words like Torah law. This is when there is one rabbinic level; but with two rabbinic levels, they certainly did not strengthen it like Torah law. According to this, one may say that there are two meanings of if it went out, he is required to tend to it. The one the Talmud debates is from the law of the Chanukah lamp itself: whether he is obligated to relight it as a Chanukah lamp. A practical difference would be whether he must bless again. If it were from the law of the Chanukah lamp, he would have to bless, as with tefillin when the knot snapped and the like. So too it appears from Rashba, brought by Beis Yosef in this siman, who wrote that if it went out unintentionally, he need not bless, for we hold that if it went out he is not required to tend to it. This implies that if he were required to tend to it, he would have to bless. About this the Talmud debates, and this law does not depend on whether kindling performs the mitzvah or placement performs the mitzvah; therefore it does not resolve from this. We hold that if it went out he is not required to tend to it, and he is not obligated to relight it because of the law of the Chanukah lamp. Nevertheless, for another reason it would have been proper to require him to tend to it, since at any rate it is forbidden for benefit and requires burning, and there is concern for a mishap lest he forget and benefit from it. But for this reason he would not need to bless, since it is only because of concern for a mishap, similar to what we find in siman 434, that on a search after Pesach one does not bless, since it is only because of concern for a mishap; see there. However, this would be if we held that placement performs the mitzvah. If so, its mitzvah has not yet been performed, since it did not burn the required measure. If this were Torah law, it would be forbidden for benefit by Torah law; automatically, even with the rabbinic Chanukah lamp, they would have strengthened it like Torah law to relight it immediately, so that one not come to a mishap. But since kindling performs the mitzvah, immediately when he kindled it, its mitzvah was performed. If so, even by Torah law it would be permitted for benefit. Thus it is not comparable to Torah law, and wherever the equivalent does not exist in Torah law, we do not say that they instituted it like Torah law, as stated in Gittin 64. Therefore, although the Rabbis were stringent and forbade the oil that remains from the required measure, nevertheless since it is two rabbinic levels, we should not be stringent to be concerned for a mishap, because the body of the prohibition is rabbinic and also its mitzvah has been performed. Therefore if it went out he is not required to tend to it even because of concern for a mishap, and it is permitted to keep it for the other days. This is well understood; examine it. Now, the explanation of the distinction is as follows: if kindling performs the mitzvah, it is considered that its mitzvah has been performed; but if placement performs the mitzvah, then even though we hold that if it went out he is not required to tend to it, it is not considered that its mitzvah has been performed. If kindling performs the mitzvah, in truth he kindles only at the beginning, not every moment. Perforce, the enactment is based on the rule that anything fit for mixing is not invalidated by lack of mixing. Since at the beginning he performed the main mitzvah, we say afterward regarding the rest that anything fit for mixing is not invalidated by lack of mixing. If so, at the beginning of the lighting his entire mitzvah was performed, since it was fit for mixing. But according to the one who says placement performs the mitzvah, one may say that the mitzvah is the whole time of its placement, for it remains placed constantly, and he performed all of its placement when he placed the oil. If so, one may say that the mitzvah is the entire time of its placement. This is unlike kindling, where he did not kindle all the oil, only a little, and perforce the rest works through the rule of fit for mixing. But with placement, he placed the vessel, and the mitzvah is throughout its placement. The rule that if it went out he is not required to tend to it is only because the Merciful One exempts an unavoidable circumstance and they did not obligate him to trouble himself and rekindle. In this case, although the Merciful One exempts an unavoidable circumstance, at any rate there is no fulfillment of a mitzvah and its mitzvah has not been performed; therefore it is forbidden for benefit by law. This is clear and correct, and this must be said to understand the Mechaber's intent, since he made them depend on one another; examine carefully. There is another way to resolve this. One may say that the reason whether if it went out he is required to tend to it depends on whether kindling performs the mitzvah or placement performs the mitzvah. For according to the one who says kindling performs the mitzvah, it is difficult: he only kindles for a moment, and the rest burns on its own. Perforce, the intent is as I wrote in the first chapter of Bava Kama regarding one's fire being because of his arrows, that what burns at the beginning is considered as though he kindled all of it immediately. According to this, the later measure of how much it should burn is not the main mitzvah, but an appendage of the mitzvah. Regarding an appendage of the mitzvah, we say that anything fit for mixing is not invalidated by lack of mixing. But according to the one who says placement performs the mitzvah, the main mitzvah is that it rests there in its place. If so, the whole time of its placement is the main mitzvah, and with the main mitzvah we do not say that anything fit for mixing is not invalidated by lack of mixing, as Rashbam wrote in Bava Basra 81b; see there. Therefore, if it went out, he is required to tend to it. But at first glance it is difficult: even though anything fit for mixing is not invalidated by lack of mixing, that is only after the fact, as written in Menachos that if he did not mix it, it is valid. But ab initio there is a mitzvah to mix. If so, here too, ab initio why should he not be required to relight it? Perforce, the reason is not because of anything fit for mixing, but because this was the enactment from the beginning. If so, there is no distinction between whether kindling performs the mitzvah or placement performs the mitzvah. However, one may say that since by Torah law, regarding a meal-offering, even if he did not mix it, it is valid, then if by Torah law it is valid after the fact, it is proper to say regarding the Chanukah lamp, which is rabbinic, that it is permitted ab initio. Thus one can properly say that it depends on anything fit for mixing. But this depends on the dispute in Gittin 65: Rav Chisda holds that anything the Rabbis instituted they instituted like Torah law, while another opinion holds that we say they instituted like Torah law only in a matter that has an equivalent in Torah law, but in something that has no equivalent in Torah law, they did not institute it like Torah law. Now, since we hold that a minor, even if he can distinguish a pebble and throw it, etc., cannot acquire on behalf of others, perforce we hold like Rav Chisda, according to the Gemara's answer that partnerships in alleyways are rabbinic. If so, in something that has no equivalent in Torah law, they did not institute it like Torah law. Therefore it is well understood. The Talmud's question whether kindling performs the mitzvah or placement performs the mitzvah follows Rav Chisda, that in every matter they instituted it like Torah law. If so, one cannot say that the reason if it went out he is not required to tend to it is because anything fit for mixing is not invalidated by lack of mixing, for ab initio he would still be obligated to kindle, as with a meal-offering. Perforce, it does not depend on anything fit for mixing, but rather this was the enactment of the Sages from the beginning. Therefore the Talmud does not resolve from this. But we hold that there is a distinction between whether something has an equivalent in Torah law or not. If so, Chanukah, which has no equivalent in Torah law, was not instituted like Torah law. Therefore one may say that this is the reason that if it went out he is not required to tend to it: because anything fit for mixing is not invalidated by lack of mixing, and since by Torah law it is valid after the fact, it is permitted rabbinically ab initio. Thus this properly depends on whether kindling performs the mitzvah, and it proves that kindling performs the mitzvah. This seems to me in my humble opinion, with the help of Heaven, and it is well understood; examine it.
