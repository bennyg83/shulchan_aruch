<b>And one must, etc., and it does not suffice, etc.</b> This is a dispute among the rishonim. The Rashbam wrote that just as Rabbi Eliezer permits through redemption in Avodah Zarah, as stated there at the end of chapter 3, 49b, the same applies to all prohibitions in the Torah. Tosafot cite this in Yevamot 81b, s.v. all of them, etc., and in Zevachim 72b, s.v. all of them, etc. So too the Mordechai wrote at the end of chapter Kol HaTzelamim, that chametz on Pesach, which is forbidden for benefit, may be redeemed to cast the value away, but not to permit eating; and so wrote the Hagahot Ashri there, s.v. and they did not permit, etc., and chametz. This is also the opinion of the Ramban in Milchamot, chapter 2 of Pesachim. The Razah wrote there that some books have the reading there, 27a, and so Rabbi Eliezer would forbid in all prohibitions in the Torah, and this refers to redemption; on this the Rashbam relied in writing that the same applies to all prohibitions. But the Razah disagreed with him there and in Avodah Zarah there, and so did Tosafot in Yevamot there and Zevachim there, the Rosh in Avodah Zarah there, the Rash at the end of Orlah, and the Ran in Avodah Zarah there and Pesachim there, that this applies specifically to idolatry, whose value is increased. Therefore they said in Pesachim 27a regarding those bowls, etc., that they are forbidden, implying that they are entirely forbidden. See the Ran there. Tosafot, the Rosh there, and the Rash there further wrote another answer, that this is specifically bread, where the substance of the prohibition is not present, etc. The Ramban proved that the same applies to other prohibitions, for Rabbi Eliezer with respect to redemption is more lenient than sale, since there he derives no benefit at all from the prohibition, and by sale it is certainly the same with other prohibitions, as stated in the Yerushalmi cited by Tosafot in Yevamot there and Zevachim there in brief, and by the Rash there, that only where there is concern lest he sell it to a Jew is it forbidden, but with things not bought from a non-Jew it is permitted; the same applies to redemption. But the Razah and the Ran in Avodah Zarah there wrote that Rabbi Eliezer has no connection to Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel, for each is separate; this is also the opinion of Tosafot there and there and the other poskim. See what I wrote in Yoreh Deah, siman 142. In Darkei Moshe there are confused matters here, for he wrote that the Ran’s opinion is to permit, and he further wrote that the Mordechai in chapter 2 of Pesachim forbids; this is not so, for the Mordechai wrote there to forbid sale, and the Razah and the other poskim already wrote that this is not related to the other matter. However, according to what is written, that they permitted redemption only barrel for barrel, as with the sale of yayin nesech, and since the Mordechai forbids for this reason, the same would be true that he forbids redemption. But the Ramban and Ran there wrote that this is specifically by yayin nesech, whereas by other prohibitions, even Torah prohibitions, it is comparable to ordinary wine of non-Jews. See what I wrote in siman 467, seif 10. What is written here, and one must, etc., and it does not suffice, etc., means that even according to those who permit, that is only with respect to benefit, but eating is forbidden, as the Razah and the Rosh wrote in his name, and so wrote the Mordechai, Hagahot Ashri, and Hagahot Maimoniyot. Even according to the words of Rabbeinu Yitzchak cited by the Rosh there, who permits even eating, that is specifically by bread, as above, and as stated there, that it was forbidden only because of benefit, for the prohibition of eating did not forbid it, from the case of an oven heated with cumin, etc. So too Rashi wrote in Avodah Zarah 49b, and it should be permitted to you for benefit, etc. This is what he says: and one must burn, etc., and it does not suffice, etc., meaning to exclude those who permit, who hold that one need not burn all of it, but may give it to a non-Jew and the like.

<b>Seif 9: Dry with dry, etc.; and some say.</b> Avodah Zarah 74a: “What does this tanna count, etc.?” and “This tanna counts two, etc.” The phrase “these are” comes to exclude other matters, as Rashi explains there. The Rosh likewise writes there, section 1, except that he writes that one need not remove one portion, as stated in Orlah, chapter 2: orlah and kilayim are nullified in two hundred, and one need not remove a portion. Removal was required only for terumah because of the theft of the kohanic tribe. See Yoreh Deah, siman 109. He writes that the mixture is permitted even for eating. Tosafot, however, explain there that “to exclude” means only that those matters were not listed, but they nevertheless prohibit in any amount, just as Bava Kamma 5a says “to exclude an informer and one who renders an offering piggul,” even though one is liable for them. A proof is that the Gemara says it comes to exclude an item counted individually, rather than items forbidden in benefit. Its meaning is to exclude neveilah, as explained there, and in Chullin 100a it states that neveilah is not nullified because it is an item counted individually. The Yerushalmi in Avodah Zarah likewise states: Why was neveilah not taught together with them? Rabbi Yose bar Rabbi Bun answered that the Mishnah teaches only an item forbidden in benefit, whereas neveilah is permitted in benefit. They challenged this from chametz on Pesach. The answer is that chametz carries kares, whereas those items do not carry kares. The Raavad adduces proof from Temurah 28a, which asks: Why, regarding mixtures of consecrated items, does the Mishnah teach prohibitions of eating and prohibitions of benefit together, meaning the Mishnah in Temurah there, while regarding mixtures of ordinary prohibited foods it separately teaches prohibitions of eating and prohibitions of benefit, meaning the aforementioned Mishnah in Avodah Zarah, which lists only prohibitions of benefit? The Gemara answers that because it wished to explain the prohibition of wine used for idolatrous libation, it listed all prohibitions of benefit resembling it, etc. This passage is missing from our text of the Gemara but appears in Rashi, although he explains it as referring to chapter 2 of Avodah Zarah. We see that when the Mishnah lists prohibitions of benefit, it includes prohibitions of eating as well, except that it did not list them explicitly, as above. Even among prohibitions of benefit, it lists only those specifically resembling wine used for idolatrous libation. See Ran and Rosh there. The Rif writes that this general rule is not accepted, because the Gemara there says it comes to exclude, etc., whereas we rule that chametz prohibits in any amount. The Ran writes that the statement there follows the opinion that, like Rabbi Yehudah, chametz after Pesach remains forbidden according to the Torah. According to our ruling, however, that follows Rabbi Shimon, it is an item that will become permitted, which is not nullified even in one thousand, as Rambam writes. See Yoreh Deah 102:4. Terumas HaDeshen, responsum 107, discusses this at length and rules like the first view; see also his responsum 262.
