**** ENGLISH ****
[1] <b>And nowadays they did not follow this practice, etc.</b> The reason for reciting Yotzer Or would be according to the opinion that an individual may not recite this Kedushah alone, as the Levush writes. His words are astonishing, for according to our ruling in siman 59 that an individual may recite Kedushah, how are we to explain the explicit Mishnah in the third chapter of Megillah, which teaches that one leads the responsive recitation of Shema, and which the commentators explain as referring to Yotzer Or? It therefore appears to me that the primary explanation is that of the Beit Yosef. Even according to the opinion that an individual may recite it, since the shaliach tzibbur says Barechu they must in any event recite some blessing. Otherwise they would appear, Heaven forbid, as though they deny the supreme God, Who tells them to bless Him, and they do not wish to do so. They therefore recite the first blessing of Krias Shema. The reason we do not recite the first blessing nowadays has already been explained by Tosafos Yom Tov in Megillah: the custom is to lead the responsive recitation before the shaliach tzibbur begins the repetition aloud, so that the shaliach tzibbur immediately begins the blessings of the Amidah, and there is therefore no appearance of denial. On this basis, Derech Chochmah writes that in communities where the responsive recitation of Shema is conducted after the prayer, they must ensure that at least one person is able immediately to recite the blessings of Yotzer. Otherwise it would appear as though they deny. It seems, however, that this is not limited to those blessings. The same applies if someone can recite the Amidah, the blessing of Ahavah Rabbah, or a similar blessing. This may be why I have not seen people careful to investigate the matter, since presumably there is someone able to recite some blessing. In truth, however, the matter requires study, for sometimes no one is able to recite any blessing, yet they still conduct the responsive recitation. It also appears that according to this reasoning the blessing must be recited aloud so that everyone hears it. Otherwise, those remaining would appear as though they do not wish to bless. In my humble opinion, the correct explanation accords with our practice, namely that we do not accept the Beit Yosef's concern that they appear, Heaven forbid, as deniers when they do not recite Yotzer. By answering, 'Blessed is Hashem, Who is blessed,' they demonstrate that they wish to bless Him. Nachalas Tzvi likewise writes this in the name of Darkei Moshe and brings proof from the blessing over the Torah, where the congregation answers only, 'Blessed is Hashem, Who is blessed.' The Derishah agrees, though he challenges the proof of Darkei Moshe, since the main praise is that the one called to the Torah then recites 'Who has chosen us' and 'Who has given us,' blessings enacted in the presence of ten, and one who hears is considered as though he answered. In my humble opinion, the Rama can be defended as holding that only Barechu must be recited aloud so that the congregation may answer, but not 'Who has chosen us,' as will be explained in siman 139. I am therefore astonished at Tosafos Yom Tov there, who writes that 'Blessed is Hashem, Who is blessed' is merely a response to the mention of Hashem's name, as in the third chapter of Yoma, where the people answered after the Kohen Gadol mentioned the Name, 'Blessed is the name of His glorious kingdom forever and ever.' According to him, the shaliach tzibbur's instruction that they bless has still not been fulfilled. He overlooked the words of Darkei Moshe. Furthermore, in my humble opinion there is no proof from the Kohen Gadol, for that response itself may also constitute a blessing. Alternatively, when the Kohen Gadol pronounced the Explicit Name as written, a response was required, unlike after the shaliach tzibbur, where no such response is required and the response itself is called a blessing. It further appears that here, because they answer and mention the Name in 'Blessed is Hashem, Who is blessed,' it certainly also constitutes a blessing. By contrast, in the response after the Kohen Gadol they do not mention the Name. Proof may be brought from what the Beit Yosef writes at the end of siman 200 regarding zimun: if three interrupt for seven, they need not interrupt beyond the blessing of zimun, because the Name is mentioned and this is properly considered a blessing. See there carefully. Nevertheless, the Mishnah cited above still requires explanation. Why, in the time of the Gemara, did they recite the blessing of Yotzer Or? I saw that the Kesef Mishneh explains, following Rabbeinu Yonah, that although the shaliach tzibbur discharges one who is not proficient, they cannot discharge their obligation in Krias Shema except by listening and answering amen. Therefore they conduct the responsive recitation of Shema to discharge the listeners through their answer of amen only in the presence of ten, for otherwise their answering amen has no effect. It follows that the purpose was to discharge one who was not proficient. Nowadays, when we are all proficient in the blessings and conduct the responsive recitation only in order to answer Kaddish and Barechu, there is no need to recite the first blessing. One should not object that they should then also have recited the other blessings to discharge them, for perhaps Rabbeinu Yonah holds that one may not answer amen after 'Who chooses His people' or after the blessing of redemption because it would be an interruption, and the responsive recitation would therefore not help for those blessings. This is the language of the Rama in Darkei Moshe. I find this difficult, since the Rama himself rules in siman 59 and siman 66 that one answers amen after 'Who chooses His people' and after Ga'al Yisrael. Moreover, we rule below in siman 215 and in many places that answering amen is not indispensable, for anyone who listens discharges his obligation. We must say that Krias Shema is different. See above, siman 62, seif 3, though this requires study. Furthermore, would they entirely refrain from discharging them merely because of the interruption? It would surely be better for them to interrupt in order to fulfill the obligation of the blessing. Magen Avraham writes in siman 74 that if they conduct the responsive recitation after the final Kaddish, they should recite several verses before doing so, because Kaddish is never recited except after verses or after the Amidah.

[ב] <b>משום דליכא קדיש וכו'.</b> ובתשובת מנחם עזריה שם ראיתי טעם אחר דאיתא באגדה כי במה נחשב הוא בכל בוקר האדם אשר נשמה באפו לא מתיישבא גביה עד דעני ברכו, ובערבית דליכא האי טעמא לא הנהיגו. והא דנוהגין לפרוס כשסיימו הציבור תפילתן, כתב הב"ח טעם לפי שכל אותן שלא שמעו קדיש וברכו מתקבצין אל הפתח ואסור לעבור נגד המתפללין לכן ממתינין עד שיסיימו תפילתן:
**** ENGLISH ****
[2] <b>Because there is no Kaddish, etc.</b> In the responsa of Menachem Azariah I saw another reason. An Aggadah states that every morning the person 'who is esteemed as nothing, whose breath is in his nostrils' does not regain composure until he answers Barechu. At the evening service, where this reason does not apply, the practice was not instituted. Regarding the custom to conduct the responsive recitation after the congregation has finished praying, the Bach explains that all those who did not hear Kaddish and Barechu gather at the entrance. Since it is forbidden to pass in front of those still praying, they wait until the congregation has finished.
