<b>Magen Avraham, seif katan 2.</b> This follows the other explanation, etc. Meaning, according to one explanation the Tur means the place where the bread baked well; according to the other, the place that bakes first. According to the reading “where cooking precedes,” it likewise means the place that cooks first, and this is identical with the place that bakes first. They dispute which side this is, some saying it is the upper side, etc. Magen Avraham then cites Rashi, because the bottom edges of the bread cook before its center. Magen Avraham writes, “With our bread he should cut above.” It appears to me that he does not mean that one should cut only above. Rather, because Rashi's statement to cut from the lower edge implies that this alone suffices, Magen Avraham says that with our bread one should also cut above, thereby satisfying the earlier opinions. Perhaps a word is missing, and the text should read: “With our bread he should also cut above.”

<b>שם </b>דבורא לשעבר משמע ולא דמברכינין בורא ולא אמרינן שברא דהא ודאי לשון עבר משום דיש לנקוט לשון בינוני דבאמת ז"א דודאי גם בורא לשון עבר והא דלא אמרינן שברא שהוא לשון עבר לכ"ע משום דנקטינן בורא שהוא לישנא דקרא כמ"ש התוס' שם ולעולם צ"ל בברכות לשון עבר. ואח"כ כתב המג"א דבחידושי הרשב"א כתב בשם הירוש' דאף ב"ש ס"ל לומר שברא לא פליגי אלא באש (ומ"ש לפנינו אלא כמ"ש ט"ס הוא וצ"ל באש) שאין מתחדש בכל שנה אבל בדבר המתחדש אף ב"ש מודה דמברך בורא שהוא לשון בינוני ומ"מ תלמודא דידן עיקר ולקמן סימן ר"ד סקי"ד הביא דברי חכמת מנוח ורמז לכאן ע"ש:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>There.</b> The statement that borei implies the past does not mean that we recite borei rather than shebara because the latter is certainly past tense while one must use the participle. That is incorrect, for borei too can certainly refer to the past. We do not say shebara, although all agree it is past tense, because we adopt borei, the Scriptural expression, as Tosafos writes there. Blessings should always refer to the past. Magen Avraham then writes that Rashba's novellae cite the Yerushalmi that even Beis Shammai agrees one says shebara; they dispute only regarding fire. The printed words “except as written” are a scribal error and should read “regarding fire,” which is not renewed every year. Regarding something renewed, even Beis Shammai agrees one blesses borei, a participial form. Nevertheless, our Talmud is primary. Below, siman 204, seif katan 14, Magen Avraham cites Chochmas Manoach and refers back here. See there.

<b>Magen Avraham, seif katan 18.</b> “But drinking is permitted, and the proof is from Rivkah,” etc. This requires study. It would have been preferable to bring proof from Hashem's command in Parashas Chukas, “Give drink to the congregation and their animals.” Ohr HaChaim there addresses this and answers that in danger a person precedes; likewise in Rivkah's case, where there is danger or distress, a person precedes. See Nedarim 80b regarding the dispute of the Sages and Rabbi Yehudah over the verses “for their cattle” and “for all their animals,” and Beis Shmuel, Even HaEzer, siman 80, together with what I wrote there and in my responsa, Even HaEzer. In my humble opinion, the reason one may not taste before feeding his animal is that a person should not presume himself righteous. At times he lacks merit and eats only through the merit of the animals, as stated in the Midrash concerning Alexander, which cites “Man and animal You save, Hashem.” Accordingly, the Gemara at the beginning of Keitzad Mevarchin explains “You shall gather your grain” as referring to those who do not fulfill Hashem's will. The verse can mean: “I shall give grass in your field for your animal,” and in that merit, “you shall eat and be satisfied.” Since he eats through the animal's merit, it is fitting to feed it first, and this is derived from that very verse. This applies specifically to ordinary eating through natural means. Where food or drink comes miraculously, there is no reason that the miracle should occur in the animals' merit. Binyan Ariel writes similarly regarding “Shall we and our cattle die there?” The miracle is certainly for the person's sake and the animal is secondary, so the person properly precedes. Therefore, when water emerged from the rock, where there was no basis to attribute the miracle to the animals' merit, Hashem commanded Moshe, “Give drink to the congregation and their animals.” The wording “bring forth for them” means for their sake and in their merit, so they precede. According to this, one might prove the opposite for ordinary circumstances: the animal should also precede in drinking. A practical difference would arise if one interrupted between washing and HaMotzi by saying to give the animal drink. Perhaps only feeding the animal must precede one's own eating, while watering the animal need not precede eating, as implied by “I shall give grass in your field for your animal,” where the insistence upon precedence concerns eating. If one nevertheless holds that watering must also precede drinking, then one who intended to drink and interrupted by instructing that the animal be given drink need not repeat the blessing. The universal custom, however, is not to be meticulous regarding drinking, even among those careful regarding eating. Based on what I wrote, the reason is that food production requires rain to saturate the earth and produce its yield, and the Midrash states that rain falls only in the animals' merit. Drinking water, however, is plentiful upon the earth and does not depend upon their merit. The requirement to give precedence therefore does not apply to watering, and one who interrupted must repeat the blessing. I later found that Eliyah Rabbah and Peri Megadim cite Rashi on Chukas: from here we learn that the Holy One, blessed is He, has compassion upon their property. According to what I wrote, there is no need to derive precedence from there, since the miracle certainly occurred in the people's merit. I have abbreviated. Most people are nevertheless not careful about this even regarding feeding. It appears that this is because they generally have servants or hired workers entrusted with the animals and birds, who are instructed to provide their food early each morning, and there is a presumption that they perform their task. It requires study whether one who always relies upon his servants, but then interrupts by saying “Give food to the ox,” has made an interruption, since for him the statement appears unnecessary. One who has no servant must personally be careful, since Chazal said that a person is forbidden to eat before feeding his animal. See Sefer Chareidim for a remarkable account from the holy Arizal on this subject.

<b>Magen Avraham, seif katan 19.</b> “Wine during the meal” means a case where not everyone presently wishes to drink, for otherwise it would be wine after the meal, since everyone would turn their attention to drinking. It must therefore refer to a case where everyone still wishes to eat, but one person now wishes to drink. We say that the others should not intend to fulfill their obligation through his blessing, because they would then have to answer amen while their throats are occupied with eating. Magen Avraham therefore asks that since they do not intend to drink immediately, they should in any event not fulfill their obligation through his blessing, because they would interrupt between the blessing and drinking. He answers that although they do not wish to drink now in the ordinary manner, they will each immediately taste a small amount from the cup over which he blessed, one after another.

<b>Seif katan 15.</b> “Nevertheless, one should not permit,” etc. Bach writes that it is initially forbidden because they might become impure first, and whether it is permitted is a dispute, as Taz ultimately cites in his name.
