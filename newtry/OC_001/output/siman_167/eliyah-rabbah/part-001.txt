<b>[Levush] that this is a manner of honor, etc.</b> For this reason I heard that one should not break bread from a loaf part of which was burned until that part is unfit to eat; if there is no other whole loaf, it is better to cut away the burned part, since it is not honorable. However, in the responsa Shaar Ephraim I found that as long as the burned part has not been cut away, it is called a whole loaf for lechem mishneh. If challah was removed from a whole loaf, its law is like a sliced loaf. I elaborated to prove this in a responsum at the end of my Eliyahu Zuta. The Magen Avraham wrote that with our bread one cuts above. It seems that this refers to where dirt is stuck below, and so it appears from the Bach. It also seems that this speaks of a slice, for with a whole loaf one should cut around it, though this can also be done with a slice. The precise way of cutting and marking before the blessing on Shabbos and Yom Tov is discussed by the Maharshal, Bach, Toras Chaim, and Maharil. The Maharil would prepare the cut so that the remainder was still recognizable at the time of the blessing. Regarding the size of the HaMotzi piece, there are views in the Yerushalmi whether it is a kezayis or less than a kezayis. In practice, whether for oneself or for others, one should give a kezayis, thereby satisfying all opinions. The Maharil's custom of eating half a kezayis has support from Sefer Yereim that less than half a kezayis is nothing. At a large meal, the piece distributed should come from the slice separated for HaMotzi. The Magen Avraham wrote that even with a slice one should not cut completely before the blessing, so that it appear larger. The Darkei Moshe wrote that one should break opposite the split, for that is where it began to bake. The Shiyarei Knesses HaGedolah wrote amounts of pieces corresponding to the Torah readers: on Shabbos seven, on Yom Tov five, on Rosh Chodesh and Chol HaMoed four, and on a weekday and at Shabbos Minchah three.

<b>[Levush] for the verse is written, etc.</b> Rabbeinu David Avudraham also answered that there it is impossible to correct matters another way, and that joining a letter after a tzerei or segol is not like joining a letter after a kamatz, which opens the pronunciation more. The laws of Maharann state that one who leaves space between the words bread and from the earth is assured that he will not be harmed at that meal. Avudraham explains that HaMotzi bread means food, as in the phrase making bread, since bread itself does not come from the earth, but grain from which bread is made. Similarly with the manna, it says that Hashem would rain down bread, although actual bread did not fall. Toldos Yitzchak explains that we bless HaMotzi bread because in the future Hashem will bring forth actual bread, as at the end of Kesubos, that Eretz Yisrael will produce ready cakes. This also explains why we say bread from the earth and not from the ground, since earth alludes to Eretz Yisrael. For this reason the Bach wrote that we say HaMotzi, which can also imply the future, as an allusion to the future. This is the meaning of the statement that from a person's blessings it is recognizable whether he is a Torah scholar. Avudraham also explains that the wording follows the verse, and Kol Bo writes that it is considered as if He brought forth bread, since the wheat kernel contains no true waste, for even the bran is fit with it, as the poor eat it.

[ה] <b>שתי ידיו וכו'.</b> ואם יש לו בית יד צריך להסירו. כתב של"ה ויכוין בברכת השולחן יותר משאר ברכות כי המאכל והמשתה מביא האדם לידי גסות הטבע וגובה לב, ולכן צריך להתבונן כדי שימשוך לבו תאות השם יתברך ולא לצד תאות מאכל ומשתה. כתב מהרי"ל הלכות שבת לעולם כשהזכיר השם בברכת המוציא הגביה פת בשתי ידיו למעלה עד שיסיים הזכרת השם והורידו כבראשונה בחול הרים הפת ובשבת השתים:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>His two hands, etc.</b> If he has gloves he must remove them. The Shelah wrote that one should have more intent in the table blessing than in other blessings, because food and drink bring a person to coarseness of nature and haughtiness of heart; therefore he must contemplate so that his heart is drawn toward desire for Hashem, blessed is He, and not toward the desire for food and drink. Maharil wrote in the laws of Shabbos that whenever he mentioned the Divine Name in the HaMotzi blessing, he raised the bread with both hands until he finished mentioning the Name, and then lowered it as before; on a weekday he raised the bread, and on Shabbos the two loaves.

<b>[Levush] and it is possible, etc.</b> The phrase dry bread implies that only where he intends to eat dry, unclean bread does it require salt, but in the first case, clean bread such as ours, there is no mitzvah at all. From the Rama it implies that the mitzvah applies to all cases, and so I found explicitly in Mateh Moshe. In my humble opinion, all can be resolved: certainly even one who is satiated may still be able to eat, but food is repulsive to him because he is already full. The Levush understood Kol Bo's phrase cannot eat as meaning that he is completely full and unable to eat at all, and on this the Beit Yosef disagrees. But the Beit Yosef agrees that we require satiation, even if he still can eat somewhat; if he is not satiated, then even if he does not intend to eat more, he must bless and eat. This is why the Levush wrote when he wishes to eat. The Bach also understood the Beit Yosef this way. I am also puzzled by the Beit Yosef's statement that this is possible according to the Raavad, for in siman 172 he cites the Raavad as speaking of one who intends to eat more. It is also difficult to identify the authorities who disagree. The Rashba's novellae imply that even if one cannot eat, he blesses. Moreover, Kol Bo and Baal Halachos Gedolos rule like the earlier authorities. Therefore I say that even if he does not intend to eat, he must go back, bless, and eat; however, if he is so satiated that food is repulsive to him, it appears that he should not return and bless, for the intent of Baal Halachos Gedolos and Kol Bo can be explained as referring to one unable to eat because food is repulsive to him.

<b>[Levush] one fulfills through the other's blessings, etc.</b> Olas Tamid explains that even if the one blessing did not intend to discharge the others, they nevertheless fulfill their obligation, and he says this is proven from seif 13. I found similarly in the Prishah, who wrote that according to the Rokeach, if they did not establish themselves they fulfill with intent; then where they did establish themselves, they fulfill even without intent, following the view in the laws of Rosh Hashanah, siman 589, that intent to discharge and fulfill is not required. This resolves the Taz's question why seif 13 needed to mention if the one blessing intended.
