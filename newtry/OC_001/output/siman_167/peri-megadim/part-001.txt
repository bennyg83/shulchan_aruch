<b>Already</b>, see Taz regarding his statement that with blessings over benefit each person must bless, yet one may still fulfill his obligation through another's blessing. His statement that one must answer amen is qualified by siman 213, seif 2: after the fact, one fulfills even without answering amen. Regarding what he cites in the name of Bach, Bach's intent is inferred from Rashi on Berachos 40a, who explains, “the one who breaks the bread, before he broke it,” rather than speaking generally. Even among participants, if one says to the person near him, “Take and bless,” it is not an interruption. This shows that Rashi refers specifically to the one breaking the bread. Once he has broken and tasted, another participant's speech, even about something unrelated to the meal, is not an interruption. As for the comparison to putting on tefillin, one may distinguish between an obligatory mitzvah, which rests personally upon him and therefore constitutes an interruption, and optional eating with a blessing of praise and thanksgiving, where one person praises the Master of all on behalf of everyone, since a person's agent is like himself. Once the one breaking bread has eaten, the others' speech is no longer an interruption. Regarding eating matzah on the first night of Pesach, however, if the leader tasted and the others interrupted, they must repeat the blessing, because it resembles a blessing over mitzvos. Taz proposes a safeguard: if the participants interrupted after hearing HaMotzi, the one breaking bread should intend to discharge only those who will not interrupt between hearing and eating. Such a person is discharged in every event, and no blessing is in vain even according to the Rokeach, because in that case one who interrupted was never included in the shared intent. See Eliyah Rabbah, seif katan 12: according to Magen Avraham, if either the one breaking bread or one participant tasted, all are discharged. Levush holds that initially speech is forbidden but after the fact they are discharged. Rama in the gloss also agrees that initially one should not speak even after the one breaking bread has eaten.

See Beit Yosef and Perishah regarding the a fortiori inference from Berachos 35a and 21a, and Magen Avraham, seif katan 7. According to that reading, when Berachos 21a lists Bircas HaMazon and Krias Shema as Torah obligations and prayer as Rabbinic, HaMotzi is not expressly classified. Its Torah status would derive only by an a fortiori inference, which the Gemara calls “from the words of the Sages.” One might argue that Chazal have authority to uproot a Torah law by requiring passive nonperformance, but not through an active violation, as explained in Yevamos 90b. With Bircas HaMazon they could maintain their enactment in a case of seminal impurity through passive nonperformance. With HaMotzi, however, if such a person may not even bless mentally but nevertheless eats, would the Torah obligation be actively uprooted because of Ezra's enactment? This may be compared to tzitzis on Shabbos in siman 13, where one may wear a four-cornered garment in public. Wearing is not itself forbidden, for the Torah did not say, “Do not wear without tzitzis,” but rather commanded, “You shall make tzitzis.” The positive command is therefore suspended passively for human dignity. The same applies here. The Torah did not say, “Do not eat without a blessing,” but imposed a positive obligation to bless first. One who eats without HaMotzi neglects a positive command, but the eating itself is not forbidden. It is enough that HaMotzi should be like Bircas HaMazon: just as the eating does not become prohibited because one failed afterward to recite Bircas HaMazon, so too before eating. Furthermore, as I wrote in several places, there are three reasons why punishment is not imposed through an a fortiori inference: this is the received rule given to Moshe; one may distinguish degrees of severity and not infer from them; and perhaps the very severity means there is no atonement through lashes, or perhaps further analysis will uncover a refutation, so we prohibit but do not punish from doubt. Accordingly, even if this is a true a fortiori inference, one still may not repeat the blessing from doubt, lest some refutation exist and he violate “You shall not take Hashem's Name in vain,” according to the view that this prohibition is Torah-level. See simanim 215 and 67. The Mechaber did not conclude, as the Tur did, that HaMotzi is not Torah-level. It is therefore possible that he holds it is Torah-level through the a fortiori inference, yet one still cannot repeat it for the reason stated. This also explains why he should eat a kezayis, as Magen Avraham, seif katan 7, writes. Taz therefore concludes that HaMotzi is only Rabbinic; see Magen Avraham there. Levush writes a refutation to the a fortiori inference. See Rashi, Chullin 7b, s.v. batza, which implies that HaMotzi concerns a kezayis, and the remarkable conduct of Rabbi Pinchas ben Yair, who was careful not to eat a kezayis from others. See Maharsha in Chiddushei Aggados and understand.
