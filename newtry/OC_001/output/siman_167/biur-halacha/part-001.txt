<b>And he should not speak</b> until he has swallowed at least a small amount, as I wrote in Mishnah Berurah. One must be very careful about this, for Magen Avraham and Eliyah Rabbah hold that chewing is not yet considered eating; if he speaks while chewing, he has interrupted between the blessing and the eating. Eliyah Rabbah cites Bircas Avraham as agreeing with Magen Avraham. Many later authorities, however, write that although one must certainly be careful initially, if he spoke while the food was still being chewed in his mouth he need not repeat the blessing, because chewing is itself the beginning of eating. [Gloss: Magen Avraham's proof is from siman 210, where one who chews and spits out does not require a blessing. In truth, according to the later authorities there, that ruling is because his intent was merely to taste. It therefore provides no proof for our case, where his chewing was intended for eating. Bigdei Yesha, Magen Giborim, and Rabbi Yaakov Emden in his siddur all reject Magen Avraham's ruling on this basis. It also appears to me that although Eliyah Rabbah here states Magen Avraham's view without qualification, in siman 210, seif katan 3, where he cites Olas Tamid, it is explicit that chewing food with the intent to eat, rather than merely to taste, is considered eating. Nevertheless, Magen Avraham's view might be explained according to what he initially wrote in siman 210 following the Rokeach, that even when one's intent is to eat, it is still called tasting. Although that case concerns actual eating, Magen Avraham may hold that regarding chewing alone one should certainly follow that view and regard it as mere tasting. The later authorities cited above did not accept this for the law after the fact. In my humble opinion, this remains very difficult. According to Rambam, who understands the sugya concerning blessings as referring to swallowing, it is possible that without swallowing, mere chewing is still called tasting even when intended for eating. Practically, it appears that one should follow Chayei Adam's ruling. It is also possible that even Magen Avraham agrees that if he swallowed the flavor extracted in his mouth by chewing, he need not repeat the blessing, because the blessing thereby took effect.] Chayei Adam likewise agrees with this practical ruling, though his words imply that this applies specifically where he first swallowed at least the flavor extracted in his mouth through chewing. <b>Nevertheless, initially he should not interrupt at all</b>, even to say, “Give food to the animal.” This is implied by Kol Bo, and Shiyurei Berachah cites Sefer HaOrah to this effect; it is likewise evident from Behag and Sefer HaEshkol. Magen Avraham cites Responsa Maharam in siman 271 that eating before one's animal is a Torah prohibition, which implies that he regards the exposition as a full derashah and not merely an asmachta. According to that view, even after reciting HaMotzi one would plainly have to interrupt. The authorities cited above, however, evidently do not hold this way. Know further that Rama's unqualified wording implies that even where the bread is not clean, although initially one should certainly bring salt before reciting the blessing, if he already recited HaMotzi he may not interrupt before swallowing some of the piece of bread. Peri Megadim writes that Rama refers only to clean bread, but this does not appear correct. I later found, with Hashem's help, that Behag and Sefer HaEshkol explicitly support what we have written.

<b>But afterward, etc.</b> See Mishnah Berurah, where I cited the later authorities who agree with Beit Yosef. In truth, Rama's source is the Rokeach and Or Zarua. One who studies the beginning of Or Zarua's discussion will see that it does not address Rama's law of listeners who interrupted before eating. Rather, Or Zarua debates whether those discharged by another's blessing are specifically obligated to taste from the bread of the one who broke it, or whether each may eat from the loaf before him, since the leader discharged them only from the blessing and did not obligate them to eat from his loaf. Although someone must certainly taste from the loaf over which HaMotzi was recited, for otherwise the leader's blessing would be void and consequently the listeners would not be discharged, the obligation does not rest upon every listener. It is enough if the one who broke the bread, or even one other participant, tastes. The others may then eat from their own loaves. His proof is from Eruvin, where the Gemara says to give it to a child to taste, showing that not every listener must taste from the cup and that even if the one who blessed does not taste, the tasting of one listener suffices. This is the substance of Or Zarua's words, and the other Rishonim in this sugya also appear to debate this point. Nevertheless, each listener is forbidden to interrupt before eating from his own loaf, and if he does so, he loses the blessing. The Rokeach cited by Beit Yosef can also be read this way, with a minor textual correction: instead of “they spoke,” it should read “he spoke.” The meaning is that after the one who blessed ate from his loaf, even if he subsequently spoke, everyone was discharged by his blessing and may then eat from their own loaves. If, however, he spoke before eating, he lost his blessing, it became a blessing in vain, and the others likewise may not rely upon it, as explained below in siman 213, seif 2. This is unlike Peri Megadim, who wished to say that in such a case all agree that the listeners are discharged. The same interpretation may be given to Recanati, siman 74, cited by Eliyah Rabbah. Accordingly, he too may agree with Beit Yosef. It follows that neither the Rokeach nor Or Zarua provides a source for Rama's ruling, and the law therefore remains as Beit Yosef and the later authorities ruled.

<b>In place of the blessing HaMotzi, etc.</b> If one recited Borei Minei Mezonos, many later authorities hold that he fulfilled his obligation after the fact. Eliyah Rabbah, citing the Derishah in siman 168, writes that Borei Minei Mezonos is a general blessing like Shehakol and covers every food, since all foods provide nourishment, except water and salt. <b> בלא הסיבה</b> -  עיין מגן אברהם שהעתיק בשם ש"ג דאם מקצתן מסובין אחד מברך לכולם אף שאינן מסובין ועיין בפמ"ג שכתב דלדידן דישיבה הוי כהסיבה ה"ה בישבו מקצתן מהני ולענ"ד צע"ג על עיקר דברי המ"א בשם הש"ג שהרי מבואר לקמן בסימן קצ"ג ס"ב דאף השלישי צריך לקבוע אותם בהסיבה עכ"פ בגמר אכילה ולדידן בישיבה וכמבואר בהדיא בדברי הרא"ש בפ' ג' שאכלו דממנו נובע זה הדין וידוע שיטת הרא"ש וש"פ וכן סתם השו"ע דמתניתין מיירי בין בברכת המוציא ובין בבהמ"ז וחד דינא הוא לתרווייהו וצע"ג:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Without reclining</b>. Magen Avraham cites Shiltei Giborim that if some reclined, one person recites the blessing for all, even for those who did not recline. Peri Megadim writes that according to our practice, where sitting is equivalent to reclining, the same applies if only some sat. In my humble opinion, the principal statement of Magen Avraham in the name of Shiltei Giborim is very difficult. Below, siman 193, seif 2, it is explicit that even the third person must join their fixed meal through reclining at least at the end of the meal, and for us through sitting. This is explicit in the Rosh in the chapter Sheloshah SheAchlu, which is the source of this law. It is well known that the Rosh and other poskim, and the unqualified ruling of the Shulchan Aruch, understand the Mishnah as referring both to HaMotzi and to Bircas HaMazon, with the same law applying to both. This therefore requires considerable study.

<b> אלא אם כן ישבו בשלחן אחד וכו'</b> -  עיין במגן אברהם סקכ"ו שהסביר טעם הדבר דבבית דאיכא שלחן ואין כולם אוכלים על שלחן אחד מוכח דאין דעתם לקבוע יחד ובטל מה שקבעו מקום מתחלה והגר"א בביאורו כתב דאגב שיטפיה מקודם העתיק המחבר בשלחן אחד וכו' ובאמת לא בעינן כלל שלחן אחד או מפה אחת היכא שקבעו מתחלה לאכול יחד או בעה"ב עם בני ביתו אלא כל שיושבין ואוכלין ביחד דהיינו שאינם מפוזרים אחד הנה ואחד הנה מצטרפים וכמו בסי"ב ודלא כמ"א:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Unless they sat at one table, etc.</b> See Magen Avraham, seif katan 26, who explains that when they are in a house containing a table but do not all eat at the same table, it is evident that they do not intend to establish a joint meal, and their original designation of a common place is nullified. The Gra writes in his commentary that the Mechaber copied “at one table” inadvertently from the preceding discussion. In truth, one table or one tablecloth is not required where they initially agreed to eat together, or where the householder eats with his household. Whenever they sit and eat together, meaning that they are not scattered, one here and another there, they combine, as in seif 12, contrary to Magen Avraham.

<b> אם כוון המברך להוציאם וכו'</b> -  הקשה הט"ז דמאי אשמעינן דוקא הכא הא אפילו בדקבעו נמי צריך המברך להוציא והשומעים לצאת ועיין בא"ר שתירץ דאפשר דבקבעו אף שצריכין כונה לצאת מ"מ א"צ לכוין להדיא להוציאם דמסתמא כונתו כך כיון שקבעו דומיא דש"ץ התוקע בשופר דלקמן סי' תקפ"ט וכזה תירצו ג"כ שארי אחרונים:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>If the one reciting the blessing intended to discharge them, etc.</b> Taz asks what novelty is taught specifically here, since even when they established a fixed meal together, the one reciting the blessing must intend to discharge them and the listeners must intend to fulfill their obligation. See Eliyah Rabbah, who answers that perhaps when they established a fixed meal together, although the listeners still require intent to fulfill their obligation, the one reciting the blessing need not explicitly intend to discharge them, because it is presumed that this is his intent once they have established themselves together. This is analogous to the chazzan who blows the shofar, as discussed below in siman 589. Other later authorities likewise give this answer.
