<b>דכיון דמינח נייחי. </b> הטור כ' זה בשם הר"א דאע"ג דגבי לתיתה אינם אסורים אא"כ נתבקעו שאני התם דעסיק ביה הלכך אינם באים לידי חימוץ עד שיתבקעו אבל הכא כיון דנייחו אתי לידי חימוץ והר"ן בשם הרא"ה כת' דכאן דא"צ נתבקעו דמיירי בחטים שהם בקיעים בתולדתן ודוק' גבי שעורים בעינן ביקוע והרא"ם חילק בין דליפ' מועטת למרוב' כמבואר בב"י וכיון שזכר כאן דעת הר"ר אפרים משמע דקי"ל אין חילוק בין חטים לשעורים ולא בין דליפ' מועטת למרוב':
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Since they are resting.</b> The Tur wrote this in the name of R' Ephraim: even though regarding soaking they are forbidden only if they cracked open, that case is different, for he is occupied with it, therefore they do not come to chametzing until they crack open; but here, since they rest, they come to chametzing. And the Ran in the name of the Ra'ah wrote that here we do not need that they cracked open, for it deals with wheat that are cracked open by nature; and specifically regarding barley do we require cracking open. And the Ra'em distinguished between a little dripping and much, as explained in Beis Yosef; and since he mentioned here the view of R' Ephraim, it implies that we hold there is no distinction between wheat and barley, nor between little dripping and much.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>By force of a double doubt.</b> The Rama's view here requires further study, for these words the Agur wrote, and Beis Yosef brought them regarding wheat on which water passed through the roof: that we rely on the permission that Ravyah wrote, and the long Mordechai brought in the name of Ravyah: to eat some of them before Pesach or to sell to a non-Jew little by little, and the rest should be permitted by force of a double doubt: perhaps water did not come down on them, and even if it did, perhaps they did not crack open (meaning did not become chametz); but the lower wheat near the attic floor almost certainly had water come down up to a finger's thickness or half a finger, and those one should cast off, and the rest is permitted to keep, but not to eat from them on Pesach; for even though regarding "bal yeraeh" the doubts are nullified in their majority, regarding eating, which forbids in any amount, we are not lenient (end quote). What he wrote "to eat some of them," etc., appears to have a scribal error in his words; for this permission to eat some of them my master and father-in-law of blessed memory brought in the name of Ravyah in the long Mordechai, that it is permitted even on Pesach so that we can say it is a double doubt: perhaps they already ate from the forbidden ones, and even if they did not eat, one can say they will now eat from the permitted; and like the case of a ring of idolatry that mixed and one of them fell into the Great Sea, that all were permitted for benefit together, for certainly there is permitted [among them]; and even if they did not eat from them he can take from them and cast into the sea, or eat from them before Pesach, and the others become permitted; and there is no "nullifying prohibition l'chatchila" here since it is before Pesach, and you have no matzah that does not have more than this prohibition two by two; and in a double doubt we follow leniency even regarding a Torah prohibition (end of his words). And in the Agur we mentioned he mixed two grounds of permission, namely eating some of them (which helps even if there is certain chametz among them) and he mentioned a double doubt because perhaps it did not become chametz; and it appears to me there is some omission in his words. <b>And in any case,</b> since the main permission here is by force of a double doubt, why is eating on Pesach forbidden? For even regarding a Torah prohibition it is permitted with a double doubt, all the more so regarding a rabbinic any-amount. And further, the Rama himself, who wrote here that a double doubt does not help for eating, wrote in siman 453 se'if 3 that if there is among them grain that sprouted, if there is sixty in what did not sprout it is permitted if they grind them, that is even to eat on Pesach; so we do not say "returns and revives"; and why did he rule here to forbid on the ground of "returns and revives"? One must therefore say that here it deals with a case where there is not sixty against the prohibition, and the main novelty he teaches is that it is not permitted even with a double doubt: the first difficulty remains: why in truth does a double doubt not help here? And further, Ravyah indeed permitted because of a double doubt even for eating, as written in his name; and also in the following se'if this is evident; and this requires further study. And for practical law it implies even according to all that the prohibition to eat is because there is no loss in keeping them; implying that if there is loss, for example he has no other, it is permitted even for eating.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>There are those who are stringent.</b> This is the Mordechai, that we are concerned lest flour or fine flour was mixed in. And Beis Yosef wrote that there is no prohibition in this, for it is fruit juice and does not make chametz except with fine flour; and it is possible we are concerned that it is full chametz since they first soak the wheat. <b>And</b> the Rosh wrote in a responsum, klal 24, that he did not see a custom of prohibition regarding honey, for mixing flour is uncommon and also it was nullified before Pesach; and if we come to be concerned because of mixtures we should forbid it all year, for some say they put nevelah meat into it and it turns into honey and is permitted, for we follow the present state (end quote). And nevertheless the Rama concludes that one may not eat it on Pesach except what they bring from the hive, even though in the Mordechai he forbids this too because of confusion with other honey; and that is an excessive stringency. <b>And I saw</b> in many places that they eat it, and also in the community of Krakow there is no custom to forbid, unlike the Rama's words here who wrote not to eat honey except that which they bring from the hive from which they make a drink, etc.: meaning even to eat it plain is permitted, for they make a drink from it l'chatchila; but one should not say that specifically the drink made before Pesach is permitted, for he did not mention "before Pesach"; and further, he began with eating, implying that this is permitted even for eating, only that he mentioned the drink as proof that they practice permission. <b>And based on this</b> it appears to me there is full permission to cook on chol ha-mo'ed a drink called mead from honey that is recognizable as coming from the hive to people's eyes, whatever is fit for drinking on Yom Tov; even though my master and father-in-law of blessed memory wrote to forbid making the drink on the festival because of concern for mixtures, and wrote that so they practiced: nevertheless it appears to us that one who is lenient for the honor of Yom Tov in these lands where we have no other drink on Pesach has not lost; and no custom of prohibition applies here, for each prepares for himself before Pesach; but if one comes to cook on the festival and has no drink other than that, one should not protest him, and the praise of Yom Tov's honor is in his hand, as appears to me. And based on the Rosh's responsum that we mentioned in the name of Rabbeinu Yonah, it appears to teach merit regarding what is written in Shulchan Aruch Yoreh De'ah at the end of siman 114, that the Rashba was careful about saffron because they mix dry meat into it; and according to Rabbeinu Yonah's words there is permission, since the saffron dissolves in the food and the prohibition is nullified, and it is exactly like honey here all year.

<b>The chicken is forbidden.</b> For perhaps the wheat kernel was in the water before the boiling and became chametz and absorbed from the water; so the Tur wrote in the name of the Ramban. And it is difficult: why do we not say "here it was found, here it was," and after the chicken was scalded it came there? And it appears that he derives prohibition from tumah, as we say in chapter "BaKol Me'arvin" (Eruvin folio 35): He touched one at night and does not know whether [the person was] alive or dead, and the next day he found him dead: the Sages declare tamei, for all tumahs are [judged] as at the time of their finding; and for this reason it is written in the Rokeach: a wheat kernel found in baked matzah, and it is unknown whether it became chametz while still dough or never became chametz: that matzah alone is forbidden, but other matzos we maintain in their presumptive status and are permitted; therefore here too we say the water retrospectively is as at the time of their finding now; and one should not say to maintain the chicken too in its presumptive status, for since the water was thus, it certainly makes chametz, and like the chicken that was certainly there its presumptive status was weakened; which is not so regarding the other matzos above, for even though we say retrospectively it is as now, that is regarding that matzah, but this is not compelling for the other matzos, as appears to me. And one should not say it is a double doubt: perhaps the wheat kernel was digested in the chicken, as the Rama wrote afterward: one can say that here it deals with a whole wheat kernel; and also one should not say "perhaps it did not become chametz," since one who is expert can distinguish: this is not called a doubt, as in Yoreh De'ah siman 84.

<b>By force of a double doubt.</b> My master and father-in-law of blessed memory raised a difficulty that this is not a double doubt, for the mind of fools is not knowledge, and it is possible that one who is expert knows to assess that it was not digested. And I do not know the difficulty, for this matter cannot be known: it is like bones in a full stomach; and all such cases are certainly a doubt, as written in Yoreh De'ah siman 81. And so explicitly in Levush siman 92, and this is his language: regarding chametz and yayin nesech that are forbidden in any amount, they do not become nevelah-in-essence; and if it fell again into another pot it is nullified in the majority if unrecognized; and if recognized one discards it and the rest is permitted (end quote). And there we wrote that this "if unrecognized it is nullified" is not precise, and they said this only when recognized: one discards it, and its release does not forbid in any amount since it itself was forbidden in any amount; and if so this case here is certainly very difficult. And it appears to me to resolve properly: here with the spoon we are concerned lest the spoon touched the wheat kernel itself in the pot and was forbidden from it, not from the cooked dish in the pot. And we find such in Yoreh De'ah siman 95 regarding meat bowls rinsed in a dairy cauldron: those who forbid there do so because the bowls touch the pan and receive taste from it, and we do not follow the water in the pan; so too here the spoon receives from the wheat kernel itself; and one should not say regarding this "here it was found, here it was," for the way of the spoon is to touch everything in the pot when stirring, and it was forbidden by taste imparted, not by any amount, for there needed to be sixty in what was absorbed in the spoon against the wheat kernel; and it is possible there is not so much absorption, even though we assess the whole spoon everywhere: that is for stringency, to assess against it because we do not know how much comes out of it, but for leniency no, for perhaps there is not so much absorption that there would be sixty in it against the wheat kernel; therefore it properly forbids. (But if one inserted) another spoon into that pot that was forbidden because the first spoon was inserted into it, that spoon is not forbidden so much as to forbid another pot into which they insert it, since it is forbidden only in any amount because of the cooked dish and will not forbid others in any amount; and likewise if they removed the wheat kernel from the pot it fell into and someone came and inserted a spoon into it, it does not afterward forbid by insertion into another pot in any amount, unless there is sixty against the spoon, and it is permitted, as appears to me clearly; and it is a peg that will not move, with Heaven's help.
