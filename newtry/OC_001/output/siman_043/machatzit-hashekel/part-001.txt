<b>Se'if katan 1: 'With tefillin, etc.; the same applies upon his arm, etc.'</b> This means that it is likewise forbidden when the hand tefillin rests upon the arm in its proper place. This is so although the Rosh, Hilchot Tefillin, siman 12, cites R' Yehuda of Orleans as uncertain regarding the hand tefillin: perhaps the rule applies specifically to the head tefillin, which bears the letter shin, a law given to Moshe at Sinai. <b>(ס"ק ד) חולצן כו'</b> מיירי בקבוע ר"ל כיון דרישא מיירי בין בעראי ובין בקבוע שפיר י"ל דמ"ש אח"כ חולצן קאי אקבוע בשלמא אי ברישא לא מיירי כלל בקבוע אז ל"ל דאח"כ חולצן קאי אקבוע דהא לא מיירי כלל בקבוע. וכ"מ מדקתני חולצן דאי קאי אב"ה עראי א"כ ממ"נ אם הם בראשו הא רשאי להשתין בהם אע"כ מיירי כשהן בידו א"כ לא שייך חולצן אלא יסירם דהא כבר חלץ אותם והם בידו אלא דקאי אקבוע ומיירי כשהן בראשו וא"ש מ"ש חולצן:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Se'if katan 4: 'He removes them, etc.; this discusses a permanent privy.'</b> Since the first clause discusses both a temporary and a permanent privy, it is reasonable to say that the subsequent words 'he removes them' refer to the permanent one. Had the first clause not discussed a permanent privy at all, there would be no basis for saying that the later words refer to one. This is also implied by the formulation 'he removes them.' If it referred to a temporary privy, then either way: if the tefillin are upon his head, he may urinate while wearing them. It must therefore be discussing tefillin already in his hand, in which case 'he removes them' is inapplicable; it should say 'he sets them aside,' since he has already removed them and holds them in his hand. Rather, it refers to a permanent privy and discusses tefillin upon his head, so the wording 'he removes them' is appropriate.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Se'if katan 6: 'Without a pit, etc.'</b> As stated in siman 83. This means when they are constructed as explained there: the opening is four cubits from the pit and the structure slopes, so that the excrement rolls and immediately falls far away, and the urine does likewise. This requires study, because Rashi comments upon the preceding Gemara. Berachos 23a states: 'Our Rabbis taught: One who enters a privy removes his tefillin four cubits away and enters. R' Acha bar Huna said in Rav Sheshet's name: They taught this only of a permanent privy; at a temporary privy he removes them and immediately relieves himself. They asked: May a person enter a permanent privy wearing tefillin to urinate? Ravina permitted it; Rav Ada bar Ahavah prohibited it. They asked Rava, and he answered: It is forbidden, for we are concerned that he may defecate while wearing them'; another version says, 'lest he pass gas while wearing them.' Rif, Rambam, and Rosh omit R' Acha bar Huna's statement in Rav Sheshet's name distinguishing a permanent from a temporary privy. This implies that they make no such distinction. Beit Yosef writes that their omission requires explanation; see there. We must say that this statement was absent from their text. The Rosh writes that Rav Hai Gaon was troubled: why ask whether one may enter a permanent privy to urinate? The baraita already states that even entering it without urinating is prohibited and the tefillin must be removed four cubits away. Rav Hai therefore reads the question as, 'May a person enter a discreet privy?' meaning one destined to become permanent but presently temporary. The Rosh writes that this emendation is unnecessary; the reading 'May he enter a permanent privy?' is correct and presents no difficulty. The baraita's requirement to remove the tefillin four cubits away is not caused by entering; it discusses one who intends to defecate. The Rosh concludes in Hilchot Tefillin: 'For this reason the baraita does not specify a permanent privy, because even if it is not permanent, once he enters to defecate he must remove them four cubits away.' This wording also implies that his text lacked R' Acha bar Huna's statement. According to the Rosh's reasoning, there is no basis to distinguish between a privy in an open field and one constructed over a pit. Since he intends to defecate and the prohibition is caused by defecation, what difference does the place make? As the Rosh himself writes, since defecation requires removal four cubits away everywhere, the same applies to urinating while wearing them, because he may defecate. This applies in a place normally used for defecation (a permanent privy) even if constructed over a pit. Rashi, who distinguishes a privy in the field from one over a pit, follows Rav Hai and holds that the prohibition is caused by entry. His comment does not address the question about one who intends to urinate, where the prohibition arises lest he defecate; that prohibition applies even to a privy over a pit. Rather, Rashi comments upon the baraita, 'One who enters a privy removes them,' which discusses entering without intending either to defecate or urinate. Even so, because it is a permanent privy he must remove them four cubits away. Here Rashi properly distinguishes: one may enter a privy constructed over a pit if he does not intend to defecate or urinate, because entering it involves no degradation, as no excrement or urine is present there. If he enters to urinate (the Talmud's question, whose conclusion prohibits it lest he defecate) Rashi concedes that there is no distinction. We rule like the Rosh, however, that the prohibition is not mere entry but applies when one wishes to defecate or urinate. There is therefore no distinction between a field privy and one over a pit. Magen Avraham's words are now understood. Chiddushei HaRashba likewise has our reading: 'R' Acha, etc.: They taught this only of a permanent privy; at a temporary privy he removes them and immediately relieves himself.' Rashba objects that although it is temporary, once he defecates he ought to move four cubits away under the rule, 'Your camp shall be holy'; all the more so when he intends to defecate. This last objection is Magen Avraham's, not Rashba's.

<b>ולפי מ"ש כו'</b> אבל כשהוא בחפירה חולץ ומשתין לאלתר ואצ"ל ד"א אע"ג דתחלה כתב דאפי' כשרוצה להשתין בב"ה קבוע צ"ל ד"א כיון דא"ל שמא יפנה בהן מ"מ אף דמסתבר ליה למ"א כן מ"מ כתב מ"א דהרב ב"י לא סבירא ליה כן ואין בזה מקום לתפוס על הרב"י די"ל דס"ל ניהו דאם נפנה צריך הרחקה ארבע אמות אפי' עשוי בחפירה מ"מ אי משתין דאין צריך חליצה כ"א משום גזירה שמא יפנה וכיון שהוא בחפירה לא החמירו כ"ה להצריכו הרחקה ארבע אמות אע"ג דאין להרב"י ראיה לזה מדברי רש"י מ"מ י"ל כן מסברא אבל לא ידעתי לעפ"ד לחלק בזה בין ע"פ השדה לחפירה:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>'According to what was written, etc.'</b> When it is constructed over a pit, however, he removes them and immediately urinates; he need not move four cubits away. Although Magen Avraham first writes that even one who wishes to urinate in a permanent privy must move four cubits away, because we tell him that he may defecate while wearing them, Magen Avraham nevertheless writes that Beit Yosef does not hold this. There is no basis for criticizing Beit Yosef. He may hold that although actual defecation requires a distance of four cubits even over a pit, urination requires removing the tefillin only by decree lest one defecate; when the privy is over a pit they were not so stringent as to require four cubits. Beit Yosef has no proof for this in Rashi's words, but it can be maintained logically. In my humble opinion, however, I do not understand how to distinguish here between a field privy and one over a pit.

<b>(ס"ק ט) וי"א אפי' כו'</b> משמע דלסברת הרא"ש כו' ודאי מלשון הש"ע אין משמעות די"ל דלדעת הי"א אפי' בלא צרכיו צריך להרחיק ארבע אמות אבל לדעה קמייתא שהוא דעת הרא"ש א"צ להרחיק ד"א אלא בעושה צרכיו אבל הכניסה י"ל אפי' לדעה א' אסור אפי' אינו עושה צרכיו אלא כוונתו דמדברי הרא"ש גופי' משמע כן וכבר הבאתי דבריו בס"ק י' ואעפ"כ אשנה להעתיק דברי הרא"ש להקל וז"ל אבעיא להו מהו שיכנס אדם לב"ה קבוע וישתין מי חיישינן שמא יפנה כו'. ואע"ג דתניא לעיל הנכנס לב"ה חולץ תפליו ברחוק ד"א ונכנס לאו משום הכניסה צריך לחלוץ אלא משום שרוצ' לעשות צרכיו ומתוך קושיא זו היה גורס ר"ה גאון מאי שיכנס אדם לב"ה צנוע כו' ולאו קושיא היא כדפרישית עכ"ל והן השני דעות שהובאו פה בש"ע וא"כ לכאור' לדעת הרא"ש ע"כ צ"ל דאם אינו רוצ' לפנות מותר לכנוס דאי ס"ד דהכניסה אסורה ואין החילוק בין רוצ' לפנות או לא אלא אי צריך להרחיק ד"א א"כ אין תירוץ הרא"ש מספיק מ"ש לאו משום הכניסה כו' דניהו דבאין דעתו לפנות א"צ להרחיק ד"א מ"מ הכניס' אסורה ומאי קמבע"ל אי שרי לכנוס לב"ה קבוע להשתין ת"ל אפי' אינו רוצה להשתין הכניסה לבדה ג"כ אסורה אלא ע"כ צ"ל דלהרא"ש כשאינו רוצ' לעשות צרכיו מותר לכנוס:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Se'if katan 9: 'And some say even, etc.; this implies that according to the Rosh, etc.'</b> The Shulchan Aruch's wording itself proves nothing. One can say that according to the 'some say,' even when he does not intend to relieve himself he must move four cubits away; according to the first view, which is the Rosh's, four cubits are required only when he relieves himself. Yet mere entry might be forbidden even according to the first view when he does not relieve himself. Rather, Magen Avraham means that this follows from the Rosh's own words. I cited them in se'if katan 10, but will repeat them for clarity: 'They asked: May a person enter a permanent privy and urinate? Are we concerned that he may defecate, etc.? Although it was taught above that one who enters a privy removes his tefillin four cubits away and enters, he need not remove them because of entry but because he intends to relieve himself. Rav Hai Gaon changed the reading to “May a person enter a discreet privy?” because of this difficulty, but it is no difficulty, as I explained.' These are the two views recorded here in the Shulchan Aruch. According to the Rosh, it apparently follows necessarily that one who does not intend to relieve himself may enter. If entry itself were forbidden and the only distinction concerned whether he must move four cubits away, the Rosh's answer would be insufficient. Granted, without intent to defecate he need not move four cubits away, but entry would remain forbidden. Why, then, would the Gemara ask whether one may enter a permanent privy to urinate? Even without intending to urinate, entry alone would be forbidden. We must therefore say that according to the Rosh, one who does not intend to relieve himself may enter.

<b>'And a new privy, etc.' In siman 83, seif 2: 'Know, etc.'</b> According to Rashi, the reason is entry, as stated in seif 1 and as Magen Avraham writes in se'if katan 6, based upon Rashi's distinction between a privy constructed over a pit and one that is not. Rashi would therefore face the aforementioned objection of the Rosh and Rav Hai: why does the Gemara ask whether one may enter a permanent privy to urinate? Entry itself should make it forbidden. Rashi apparently had our reading, 'May one enter a permanent privy to urinate?' rather than Rav Hai's reading, 'a discreet privy.' We must therefore say that Rashi holds urination is different because it is a human necessity. This proves Magen Avraham's reasoning. Rashi holds that although mere entry normally requires a distance of four cubits, urination, because it is a human necessity, would permit entry were it not for the decree lest one defecate. The Rosh disagrees. He holds that if entry itself required four cubits, even the human necessity of urination would not permit entry, since entry would be so severe as to require four cubits. He therefore had to explain that the four-cubit distance is not caused by entry but by the intent to defecate. Once entry itself does not require four cubits but is merely prohibited, logic permits distinguishing urination as a human necessity: entry would be permitted were it not for the decree lest he defecate. When the Rosh answers, 'He need not remove them because of entry, etc.,' he means the removal mentioned in the cited baraita (removal four cubits away) is not caused by entry but by his intent to defecate. At first glance, the main answer is still absent from the Rosh's words. Granted that entry does not require four cubits, entry itself remains forbidden. Why, then, does the Gemara ask whether one may enter a permanent privy to urinate? We must answer that urination is a human necessity, yet that essential answer is absent. One can explain the Rosh's intent differently. Since the baraita's four-cubit requirement is not caused by entry but by intent to defecate, the baraita does not establish whether one who has no intent to defecate must remove the tefillin; perhaps even entry is permitted. The Gemara's question about entering to urinate therefore poses no difficulty. We independently infer that entry is forbidden even without intent to defecate because it is no better than entering a bathhouse, which is forbidden even without intent to bathe. We consequently distinguish urination as a human necessity. The Rosh did not mention the bathhouse objection, so he had no need to resolve it; perhaps the distinction based upon human necessity was obvious to him. Had entry required a four-cubit distance, its severity would have prevented this reasoning from permitting entry even for a human necessity; the Rosh therefore needed his stated answer. The Rosh came only to resolve Rav Hai's objection from the baraita requiring four cubits, and his answer sufficiently resolves that objection.

<b>'But in a bathhouse it is forbidden.'</b> This means when he enters to bathe, for bathing is also included among human necessities: it is one of the five afflictions of Yom Kippur, and Nedarim 81a indicates that according to one opinion even laundering is called a human necessity; all the more so washing the body. See there. The Tur therefore did not cite Rav Hai's view, because it is obvious that a privy is no better than a bathhouse. Although Rav Hai appears to require four cubits even when the person does not intend to defecate, making the privy more stringent than a bathhouse, and the Tur should seemingly still have cited his view, one can answer as follows. Rav Hai does not explicitly say that one who has no intent to defecate must move four cubits away. He may also hold that the baraita requires four cubits because the person intends to defecate, but maintain that even without such intent, entry itself is at least forbidden although four cubits are unnecessary. He therefore needed to emend the text to 'a discreet privy.' Although it is very strained to interpret Rav Hai this way, the opposite is not explicit. The Rosh, by contrast, explicitly shows that one who has no intent to defecate need not move four cubits away and has the same rule as one entering a bathhouse. The Tur therefore omitted Rav Hai's view. See Magen Avraham, se'if katan 14, for another answer concerning the bathhouse.
