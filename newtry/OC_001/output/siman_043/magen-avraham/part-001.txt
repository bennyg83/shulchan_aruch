**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Without a pit.</b> A privy in a pit is considered closed, as in siman 83:4; Beis Yosef writes likewise in Rashi's name. At first glance this implies that one may urinate there while wearing head-tefillin, since it refers back to the preceding law. This requires examination. Rashi comments on the Gemara's earlier ruling that one removes them four amos away: this applies only without a pit; with a pit it resembles a temporary privy, where the Gemara says one removes them and immediately relieves himself. Rashi holds the removal is required because of entering the privy, so where there is a pit entry is permitted; even Krias Shema may be recited within it. But Rosh and Beis Yosef hold that removal is not because of entry, but because he intends to relieve himself; even at a temporary privy, one who intends to defecate must remove them four amos away. Chiddushei Rashba asks how one may remove them and immediately defecate at a temporary privy, when 'your camp shall be holy' is required. Certainly the same applies when he intends to defecate at a privy in a pit, and one also may not urinate there wearing head-tefillin, since we still decree lest he defecate. According to my explanation above; that 'rather, he removes them four amos away' concerns urinating at a permanent privy; the matter is resolved: it applies specifically without a pit, but with a pit he removes them and urinates immediately without distancing four amos. Beis Yosef, however, evidently erred here and thought Rashi explained this concerning the Gemara's inquiry; see there.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And some say even, etc.</b> Rosh's view appears to permit entering a privy wearing tefillin when he does not need to relieve himself. This is difficult: is it better than a bathhouse, where one removes them as in siman 45:2? Indeed, a privy is more severe, for Krias Shema is permitted in a new bathhouse but forbidden in a new privy. I understood this question from the gloss of the author of Shach, who noted, 'See below, siman 45:2.' It therefore appears that everyone agrees one may not enter a privy wearing tefillin when not urinating. The inquiry concerned only entry to urinate, since this is a vital human need; the conclusion forbids it because we fear he may defecate while wearing them. Tur and Shulchan Aruch in se'if 1 say, 'It is forbidden to enter, etc., to urinate' as the greater novelty: even urination is forbidden, certainly entry without need. Proof is that he must remove them four amos away, and according to Rashi this is because of entry, as in se'if 1. Entering a bathhouse wearing tefillin is forbidden because when bathing one removes his clothes regardless. Tur did not cite Rav Hai because the matter is obvious. This is also implied by se'ifim 7 and 8.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>A strap.</b> A strap also possesses sanctity, since the Divine Name is formed in it. Beis Yosef writes that concerning entry to a permanent privy, Rabbeinu did not say to give them to another person; apparently this is because it is a mitzvah to guard them oneself. But se'if 1 concerns urinating while standing at a permanent privy. He cannot bring them in his hand because he must wipe droplets, nor on his head because we fear he may defecate; he must therefore give them to another person. Here he relieves himself seated and may hold them in his hand rather than give them away, as explained above. At a temporary privy he may urinate while wearing them on his head. Responsa Rema MiPano 59 permits entering filthy alleyways wearing tefillin even when an openly filthy place is present; it is good to cover them with one's hat, and one may instead remove and later replace them. Berachos 24 supports this: Rabbi Abbahu walked while reciting Krias Shema and fell silent upon reaching filthy alleyways. Surely he did not recite Krias Shema without tefillin; had he removed them the Gemara should have said so. Evidently, one may enter such alleyways wearing them. Nevertheless, one might say that a person who knows he will pass through filthy alleyways should put them on at the synagogue, not at home. Shabbos 127 relates an even stronger case: Rabbi Yehoshua refused to enter a gentile's house wearing tefillin, saying that sacred words should not enter a place of impurity. Olas Tamid writes that he should put them on at home and cover them in filthy alleyways.
