[2] <b>And if not, etc.</b> See siman 141, seif 3, where this will be explained. Olas Tamid wrote: “I do not know why the Shulchan Aruch omitted the statement in the chapter HaRoeh that three things shorten a person’s life, etc., including one who is given a Sefer Torah to read and does not read.” I saw that Sheyarei Knesses HaGedolah similarly wondered why Rambam and Tur omitted it. It appears to me that the primary resolution is that this applied only in the time of the Gemara, when the person called up himself read, and not the chazzan. This also resolves the question of Magen Avraham above, siman 53, seif katan 22.

Sheyarei Knesses HaGedolah writes that Responsa Rama, siman 41, rules that if he comes to a place where he is unknown and they ask him how he should be called, he should be called by his paternal grandfather’s name. **** ENGLISH ****
[4] <b>By the name of his maternal grandfather, etc.</b> Taz disagrees, because perhaps he will later divorce his wife and write that name in the get, thereby invalidating it. Therefore, he should be called “son of Avraham,” which certainly does not invalidate it, since everyone is a child of Avraham. This is no difficulty, for the rabbi must investigate the father’s name, and the investigation will reveal the truth. Proof is that the son of one who converted to idolatry is called by the paternal grandfather’s name. However, it appears to me that in that case Rama holds like Beis Shmuel in Even HaEzer, siman 129, seif katan 19, in the name of Maharshal, that a get is valid with the paternal grandfather’s name because of the concern of agunah. Even so, this is difficult, since by strict law it is invalid, and Rosh also invalidates it, as does Rama there in his responsum at the end of the matter. Moreover, “son of Avraham” is invalid in a get, as Beis Shmuel writes there in seif 20, unless he wrote “Avinu.”

[ז] <b>יהפוך פניו וכו'.</b> והב"ח מסיק דנכון והגון שלא יהפוך פניו כלל ויהא פתוח לגמרי דלא חיישינן שיאמרו ברכות כתובה בתורה וכך היה נוהג והביאו אחרונים וכן מצאתי במטה משה סימן רכ"ז, ושיירי כנסת הגדולה כתב אני נוהג שלא להסתכל בכתב אלא להסתכל חוץ. כתב פענח רזא ויתן על פניו מסוה מכאן כשבאין מן התיבה ומקריאת התורה שמכסין את הפנים. כתב של"ה דף רנ"ד שמעתי ממהרש"ל דאותן העולים לספר תורה ומשתחוים בברכת התורה ילמדו אותם שלא ישתחוה, אך היה מנהג וכשיעלה לספר תורה וראה הפרשה שיקרא השתחוה ולא משום שהשתחוה של ברכה רק לכבוד הספר תורה השתחוה ואחר כך אמר הברכות, עד כאן. ובלחם חמודות דף מ"ג מצאתי זה לשונו, אני אומר שטועים כל השוחים בברכת התורה תחילה וסוף עד כאן לשונו, ועיין לעיל סימן נ"א. אבל לקמן סוף סימן ק"נ מבואר כלבוש דמשתחוה, וכן ראיתי ברוקח סוף סימן שי"ט כתב אומרים יהללו כשמחזירין הספר תורה למקומה לפי שבברכת התורה משתחוים לכבוד התורה, אומרים יהללו להודיעך שלא בשביל אלהות שבתורה הוא משתחוה ששכינתו שורה עליהו ולא בשביל שגם הוא אלוה שהרי נשגב שמו לבדו עד כאן לשונו, והביאו מטה משה סימן רנ"ו באריכות, וסיים מתוך דבריו נשמע שמנהג קדמונים הוא לכרוע בברכת התורה, ולא כאותם שאומרים שאינו מנהג, עד כאן. ומשמע דבברכה עצמו השתחוה, כי דחוק לפרש דמיירי קודם התחלת ברכת התורה, ומכל מקום נראה שלא יכרע נמי סוף הברכה, דאז נראה כאילו עושה משום ברכה כדלעיל סימן קי"ג. וכתב בתשובת פני משה ברכה אחרונה לכולי עלמא דרבנן, ראשונה לרמב"ן ורשב"א וספר החינוך דאורייתא ולרי"ף ורמב"ם ורא"ש דרבנן:
**** ENGLISH ****
[7] <b>He should turn his face, etc.</b> Bach concludes that it is proper and fitting not to turn his face at all, and that the Sefer Torah should remain fully open, because we are not concerned that people will say the blessings are written in the Torah. This was his practice, and the later authorities cite him. I found the same in Mateh Moshe, siman 227. Sheyarei Knesses HaGedolah writes: “My practice is not to look at the written text, but to look elsewhere.” Paneach Raza writes on the verse, “He placed a veil upon his face,” that this teaches that when one comes from the bimah and from Torah reading, one covers his face. Shelah, page 254, writes: “I heard from Maharshal that those who are called to the Torah and bow during Bircas HaTorah should be instructed not to bow. Yet there was a custom that when one ascended to the Sefer Torah and saw the passage he would read, he bowed, not because he bowed during the blessing, but in honor of the Sefer Torah, and afterward recited the blessings.” In Lechem Chamudos, page 43, I found: “I say that all who bow at the beginning and end of Bircas HaTorah are mistaken.” See above, siman 51. Yet below, at the end of siman 150, the Levush rules that one bows. I likewise saw in Rokeach, end of siman 319, that Yehallu is recited when returning the Sefer Torah to its place because during Bircas HaTorah one bows in honor of the Torah. Yehallu is then recited to inform you that he did not bow because of divinity in the Torah, but because the Shechinah rests upon it, not because the Torah itself is a deity, for “His name alone is exalted.” Mateh Moshe, siman 256, cites this at length and concludes that from his words it is evident that the ancient custom was to bow during Bircas HaTorah, unlike those who say it is not a custom. This implies that the bow occurred during the blessing itself, for it is difficult to explain that he refers to before beginning the blessing. Nevertheless, it appears that he should not bow at the end of the blessing, because then it appears that he bows on account of the blessing, as explained above in siman 113. Responsa Penei Moshe writes that according to all opinions the concluding blessing is D’Rabbanan. The opening blessing is D’Oraisa according to Ramban, Rashba, and Sefer HaChinuch, while according to Rif, Rambam, and Rosh it is D’Rabbanan.

[9] <b>They should not answer with him, etc.</b> This is somewhat difficult, for above, siman 124, seif 5, it appears that they do answer. Perhaps one may distinguish that there the congregation answers, whereas here only the chazzan answers. According to this, Olas Tamid’s inference from Rama’s words, “so that they should hear,” etc., namely that even if ten heard, he must repeat the blessing for the sake of the others, is difficult, for they could answer when they hear those who heard responding. It is strained to distinguish between ten and a congregation. I later saw that Magen Avraham in siman 57 writes that if the congregation hears others answer “Baruch,” etc., they answer amen after them, as explained in siman 139, seif 6. He did not notice all of this. In my humble opinion, if one hears the congregation, he too answers “Baruch,” etc. I also find it difficult why Levush omitted the Shulchan Aruch’s ruling that some say if he recited the blessing silently, he must repeat it aloud. It appears that Levush holds that since Shulchan Aruch writes in seif 8 regarding one who arrived late, etc., that he repeats because of the honor of the Torah, it must be that fundamentally he need not repeat it aloud and that this is not indispensable after the fact. This is also implied by Levush, and Beit Yosef similarly infers in the name of Mahari Abuhav from the Tur. In truth, I am astonished by them, for the Tur mentions only that Bar’chu should be said aloud so the congregation can answer “Baruch Hashem,” etc. It is possible that the Tur agrees that this is indispensable. Seif 8, however, discusses the blessing Asher Bachar Banu after he already recited it, and therefore the reason of honoring the Torah is necessary. This is also implied by Abudraham. Rama’s gloss, “so that the people should hear Baruch,” etc., likewise implies that only Bar’chu must be repeated aloud, but not Asher Bachar Banu. I likewise showed grounds for this above in siman 49. This also raises a difficulty against Bach and Olas Tamid, seif katan 16. Mahari Abuhav writes that he found in the name of Rabbeinu Yonah that one must repeat the blessings aloud. It appears to me that this citation is imprecise, for I examined Kol Bo, page 10, which writes these very words in the name of Rabbeinu Yonah, but says only that they must be recited aloud. Mateh Moshe and Shelah cite it likewise. The reasons they give may also be understood as applying only to Bar’chu. I am also astonished that Mahari Abuhav concludes, “Therefore it is good to say it aloud,” which implies that all the reasons apply only initially, but after the fact it is not indispensable and he need not repeat the blessing. This is likewise implied in Kol Bo there and somewhat at the beginning of the next siman. Perhaps for this reason Levush omitted it. This requires study. In any event, initially one must be careful to recite all the blessings aloud. See Sefer Chassidim, siman 254. They move the Sefer Torah when saying, “Who gave us His Torah,” and likewise when saying, “the Torah of truth.” Mateh Moshe.

[י] <b>צריך לחזור וכו'.</b> זה לשון מגן אברהם כתב הלבוש דאם לא רצו להמתין לו עד שיקרא פסוק אחד לא יעלה כלל ויקרא אחר במקומו עד כאן לשונו. וזה דבר שאין לו מציאות דבקל יכול לומר פסוק יברכך דרך הילוכו עד כאן לשונו. ותמיהני דלא נזכר מזה בלבוש ואדרבה משמע דאינו מעכב, ונראה שטעות סופר נפל בדבריו וצריך לומר הב"ח במקום הלבוש כי כתב הב"ח וזה לשונו, וכן שלא יעלה כלל אלא יקראו אחר במקומו אם לא שממתינין לו בכדי שיאמר ברכת כהנים או יעלה ומברך, עד כאן. ולפי זה לא מקשה מידי דהב"ח מצריך לומר כל ברכת כהנים ולא פסוק יברכך לחוד וכמו שכתבתי לעיל סימן מ"ז ס"ק י"ב:
**** ENGLISH ****
[10] <b>He must repeat, etc.</b> Magen Avraham writes: “Levush wrote that if they were unwilling to wait for him until he read one verse, he should not go up at all, and another should be called in his place.” He then comments that this is a case that cannot occur, since he can easily recite the verse Yevarechecha while walking. I am astonished, for this is not mentioned in Levush, and on the contrary, Levush implies that it is not indispensable. It appears that a scribal error entered his words and that “Bach” should be read instead of “Levush,” for Bach writes: “He should not go up at all, and another should be called in his place, unless they wait for him long enough to recite Bircas Kohanim, or he goes up and recites the blessing.” According to this, there is no difficulty, because Bach requires him to recite the entire Bircas Kohanim, not merely the verse Yevarechecha, as I wrote above in siman 47, seif katan 12.
