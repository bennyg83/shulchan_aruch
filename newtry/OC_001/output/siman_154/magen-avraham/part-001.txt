**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Which are not sacred.</b> For they are neither for beauty nor for clothing, but only as a sign so that they not err. There. Nowadays the practice is to hang them on the Torah scroll for beauty. Cloths spread over the Torah scroll may be hung on the wall of the synagogue, and silver utensils placed on the Torah scroll may be placed on the pillars in the synagogue. Binyamin Ze'ev, siman 174. The Bach wrote in siman 153 that the Maharshal wrote: it appears that it is not called lowering from sanctity if other books are placed in the ark alongside the Torah scroll, for its sanctity still remains. Know this, for we always place invalid sheets in the ark, and the same applies to Chumashim and other books. However, it requires study where they removed the Torah scroll from it, whether one may place other books in it, for since there is no Torah scroll there, perhaps there is a lowering from sanctity temporarily, or perhaps not. This is his wording. To me it appears obvious that it is forbidden even temporarily, and even placing them next to a Torah scroll does not appear easy to permit, for who says to us that this is the practice of the pious. This is his wording. In Sefer Chasidim, siman 834, it says that it is forbidden to place invalid sheets inside it. Perhaps it is permitted because the heart of beis din stipulates. In an ark built like a room, it is obvious that it is permitted, as written at the end of seif 1. In Megillah we say that it is permitted to place the ark in the window to seal it so that impurity not enter the synagogue, even though this is mundane use. One must say that since he does not use it for anything, what difference is there whether it lies here or here? Tosafot in Bava Batra 20 wrote that since he does not nullify it to be there forever, it is permitted. In the Taz in Yoreh Deah it is written that it is forbidden to take a book and place it under another book to raise it so that it be convenient to see and examine it, unless the book is already lying there; then it is permitted to place the second on top of it. This is his wording. From what I wrote above it implies that it is permitted, and certainly when he does so to study from it, as written in siman 315, seif 7. In Sefer Chasidim, siman 898, one should not score lines on booklets upon a book, because booklets have no sanctity until something is written in them. This implies that once something is written in them, one may place them upon it. Afterward he wrote that if the sun is shining and he cannot study, it is permitted to place one book to serve as shade, but not for a need unrelated to study, for it is forbidden to benefit from it; see there. One should not place booklets in a book, nor write his accounts in a book, nor test the quill in it. This is his wording. We say in the second chapter of Shabbos: women die for the sin of calling the Aron Kodesh 'arna,' for one must say Aron Kodesh. However, many times in the Gemara and poskim the term 'aron' appears. One must say that when engaged in study it is different, for everything is a sacred matter. Rashi wrote: 'arna' is what they called their chests, but 'aron' is a name designated also for the Aron Kodesh. I am astonished at the great men of the generation who do not protest against those who make covers for sacred books from pages of sacred writings when binding them. Certainly this is a complete prohibition, for they lower them from their sanctity, and it is proper to put them in genizah, so that they not lie in disgrace.

<b>A synagogue candle.</b> This is the language of the Beit Yosef: Rabbeinu Yerucham, netiv 2, wrote that a synagogue candle may be used for reading by its light, whether Talmud or any of one's needs, for his intent is for illumination, to increase the light of the synagogue; the more he reads, the greater the mitzvah, and he receives reward. So wrote Rav Natronai in a responsum. Although the Kol Bo wrote that the custom was not to light from a synagogue candle, since they were set aside for their mitzvah, and the early authorities practiced a great prohibition, and even to write or study they did not light from them, the world practiced according to Rav Natronai. Nimukei Yosef wrote in the laws of tzitzis that it is forbidden to light from them while they are burning for their mitzvah for a weekday need, but a Shabbos candle or Chanukah candle for Torah study, for a woman in childbirth, or for a sick person is permitted. However, they did not have the practice to be careful about this. This is the wording of the Beit Yosef. He understood that according to Rabbeinu Yerucham it is permitted to read by it even a weekday matter, and all the more so to light from it a weekday candle; according to the Kol Bo, even for a mitzvah one may not light from it; and according to Nimukei Yosef, if it is for a mitzvah it is permitted, but for a weekday matter it is forbidden. It requires study how it entered the mind of the Gaon to explain the words of Rabbeinu Yerucham that one may read weekday material, for he wrote, 'the more he reads, the greater the mitzvah, and he receives reward.' What mitzvah and reward is there in a weekday matter? Furthermore, he did not copy the words of Rabbeinu Yerucham as written. This is his language: oil that they vowed to light in the synagogue may be used for reading by its light, for the only reason they forbade reading by a Shabbos candle was not because of sanctity but because perhaps he will tilt it. Therefore on a weekday it is permitted to read by its light, whether Talmud or any of his needs. And even though a Chanukah candle has sanctity, and we conclude that one should not treat mitzvos disgracefully, there the mitzvah is to light a Chanukah candle; here he intends illumination, to increase the light of the synagogue, and the more he reads, the greater the mitzvah, and he receives reward, etc. This is his wording. It is thus clear that what he wrote, that it is permitted to read, etc., all of his needs, refers to a Shabbos candle, meaning that he may read by it on a weekday for all his needs if some of it remained, as the Beit Yosef wrote in siman 677 in the name of Tosafot and the Ran. But by a synagogue candle it is forbidden to read a weekday matter by it, as with all uses of a synagogue; only to study by it is permitted. We do not say that he did not donate with this in mind, for the more he reads, the greater the mitzvah. If so, according to everyone it is forbidden to light a weekday candle from it, as written in seif 14. Thus what is written here, that it is permitted to read by its light, means specifically a matter of study, as is also implied by the fact that in seif 14 he forbids even lighting from it. However, this is specifically where one vowed a candle to the synagogue, for that is what he discusses. But the candles that the treasurers make, where the practice is that on Motzaei Shabbos each person takes a candle home and uses it for weekday use, the heart of beis din stipulates regarding them. But those that stand on the menorah, it is forbidden to read weekday matters by them.

<b>Only what he gave.</b> Presumably they stipulated only regarding a portion that was defined at that time, and not to make a different appraisal. Even if there were room to be uncertain about their language, what they intended, I would likewise judge this law in that way, for in any event the Torah scroll remains with those who remain; they merely need to return the money. There is doubt how much they must return, and they can say: the Torah scroll is certainly ours; and if the issue is money, bring proof and take it, as we say regarding orphans. This is the wording of Teshuvos Maimoniyos, Sefer Mishpatim, siman 18. According to the latter reason, even if the books decreased in value, he need give him only according to the lower price. Levush. See Choshen Mishpat, siman 115, seif 5, and siman 176, seif 40. It appears to me that if the condition was that if he wishes, he will give them its value and take the Torah scroll, then even if he afterward agreed to give them the Torah scroll, nevertheless they are not called the ones in possession, since he has the ability to remove them, as written there in Choshen Mishpat, siman 115; see there. If one found an item in the courtyard of a synagogue, he acquired it, and we do not say that the courtyard of a synagogue acquires for sacred property, because a courtyard acquires by the power of a hand, and sacred property has no hand. Agudah at the beginning of Me'ilah; see there, and see Shekalim, chapter 6. Mabit, part 2, siman 171, wrote: townspeople who had one synagogue and, because of some circumstance, needed to divide themselves into two, the law is that all sacred utensils and Torah scrolls are divided in the following way: if there are known objects that a particular person dedicated, he or his heirs may take them to the synagogue in which he is located; and what is not known is divided according to the number of people from age thirteen and upward, for all have a right in them.
