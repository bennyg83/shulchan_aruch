<b> השוכרים בית וכו'</b> -  עיין במ"ב טעמו של דבר וזה כתבתי לפי מה דאסברה לנו הלבוש עי"ש אמנם מעולת תמיד מוכח דאין דין זה אלא בכגון ההיא דר"י בן חביב שלא היה שם רשות להם בתוגרמה להזמין מקום קבוע לתפלה מפני המלכות והיו מטמינים את עצמם בתחתיים להתפלל ובכה"ג בודאי אינו חשוב אלא עראי כי היום או מחר יתודע ויתבטל באמצע הזמן משא"כ בשוכרים בגלוי כדרך כל העולם שעל אותו הזמן כמכורה היא להם בודאי יש בה קדושה כיון שקבעו אותו מקום לתפלה בכל משך הזמן ולא באקראי בלבד ואע"פ שלאחר כלות הזמן אפשר שלא ירצה בעה"ב להשכיר מ"מ אין זה מקרי אקראי בעלמא כיון שבכל אותו הזמן אין להם קביעות אחרת כ"א שם וכעין זה משמע ג"כ מהכנה"ג הובא בשערי תשובה שכתב דאם הוא נעשה ברשות המלך [והיינו שלא בהצנע וכנ"ל] יש בה קדושה. ולענ"ד שכן נכון לדינא מאחר שגם בגוונא דמהר"י בן חביב כתב מהרי"ט ח"ב סימן ד' שלא ברירא ליה דבר זה והוא חוכך בזה להחמיר כל משך הזמן של השכירות [והביאו בכנה"ג] וא"כ עכ"פ בשוכר בגלוי כדרך כל הארץ בודאי אין להקל בזה:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Those who rent a house, etc.</b> See the Mishnah Berurah for the reason, which I wrote according to the Levush’s explanation. Olas Tamid, however, indicates that this ruling applies only to a case like that of Mahari ben Chaviv, where under the Turkish government they had no permission to designate a permanent place for prayer and therefore hid in basements to pray. Such a place is certainly only temporary, since today or tomorrow it may be discovered and the arrangement cancelled before the rental period ends. This differs from people who rent openly in the ordinary manner. For the rental period the premises are effectively sold to them, and they certainly acquire sanctity, since the place is fixed for prayer throughout that entire period and not used merely occasionally. Although the owner may refuse to renew the lease afterward, this is not merely casual use, because during the rental period they have no other fixed place. Knesses HaGedolah, cited in Shaarei Teshuvah, likewise implies that if it is established with the king’s permission, meaning openly and not secretly, it has sanctity. In my opinion this is the correct practical ruling. Even in Mahari ben Chaviv’s case, Maharit, part 2, siman 4, writes that the matter is not clear to him and he inclines to rule stringently throughout the rental period. Therefore, where a place is rented openly in the ordinary manner, one certainly should not be lenient.

הגר"א בס"ט משמע ג"כ דאינו יכול להוריד תשמישי קדושה מקדושתן בשום גווני ועיין בפמ"ג בקנ"ג במשב"ז אות ט"ו שגם הוא נשאר בדין זה בצ"ע:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Articles serving sacred objects, etc.</b> Regarding whether the seven representatives of the city can remove the sanctity from a mantle or similar article, as they can with a synagogue under siman 153, seif 7, Magen Avraham writes there, seif katan 14, that no device can remove the sanctity of articles serving sacred objects. Eliyah Rabbah rules likewise. We wrote in Shaar HaTziyun there that this is evident from Ramban, Rashba, Ritva, and Ran at the beginning of the chapter Bnei HaIr. They struggled even to explain why the seven representatives can remove a synagogue’s sanctity. Ramban and Rashba explain that a synagogue’s sanctity is no more severe than that of other mitzvah articles, which may be discarded after they cease serving a mitzvah. That reasoning does not apply to articles serving sacred objects. Peri Megadim, however, notes in siman 153, Eshel Avraham, letter 14, that Rambam, chapter 10 of Hilchos Sefer Torah, implies that the city representatives can remove even the sanctity of articles serving sacred objects, at least when they intend to use the proceeds for a higher sanctity. The Tur, Yoreh Deah 282, adds that with the seven representatives acting in the presence of the townspeople they may remove the object’s sanctity even to drink beer with the proceeds, exactly as with a synagogue, where the buyer may use the building for mundane purposes and the proceeds may also be spent on beer. Beit Yosef writes there that Rambam agrees when the representatives act in the presence of the townspeople and merely abbreviated his wording. Shulchan Aruch, Yoreh Deah, rules without qualification accordingly. The later authorities ask how this view answers Ramban’s objection. See Responsa Chasam Sofer, siman 38, who struggles with this. His further objection that Rambam contradicts himself by stating at the beginning of halachah 4 that articles serving sacred objects require burial, without distinguishing a sale, is not difficult, because Rambam’s words at the end of that halachah apply to everything stated earlier in the halachah. In my opinion, Rambam and Tur’s reasoning is straightforward. They hold that the authority of the city representatives regarding a synagogue rests on the principle that anyone who dedicates property does so subject to their authority. It is therefore comparable to a conditional dedication, which is effective. This resembles the Yerushalmi in the chapter Bnei HaIr, cited by the Rishonim, where Rabbi Yirmiyah saw people placing a tool in the ark and Rabbi Ami answered that they had presumably stipulated this from the outset. Under this reasoning there is no distinction between mitzvah articles such as a synagogue and articles serving sacred objects; the representatives’ authority applies to all. Meiri in Megillah likewise indicates that this is the basis of their authority. He writes that if the seven representatives sell a synagogue in the townspeople’s presence, it loses its sanctity even if they sell it in order to lower the proceeds to a lesser sanctity or spend them on mundane matters, and the building itself becomes mundane in the buyer’s hands for every use, because it was dedicated subject to that understanding and to their authority. Even according to this approach, however, the Tur’s juxtaposition of his father the Rosh’s ruling that an individual’s authority over his own property equals that of the city representatives is difficult. The Tur clearly implies that an individual may likewise sell articles serving sacred objects that he dedicated and remove their sanctity. One could say that when he dedicated them he did so subject to his own intent and discretion, no less than if he had made their status dependent on another individual’s decision, as Mordechai, siman 824, cites from Raavyah. Still, it is reasonable that his retained authority permits him to sell them and use the proceeds for mundane purposes, but not to remove the sanctity from the object itself whenever he wishes. Unless he stipulated explicitly, he presumably dedicated the object permanently. Practically, although Rambam, Tur, and Shulchan Aruch are lenient, and Raavyah cited by Mordechai, siman 826, also implies that a town’s sale can remove even the sanctity of articles serving sacred objects, it is difficult to be lenient against Ramban, Rashba, Ritva, and Ran. The Gra’s wording in seif 9 likewise implies that such articles cannot be lowered from their sanctity under any circumstances. See also Peri Megadim, siman 153, Mishbetzos Zahav, letter 15, who leaves the matter requiring study.

<b> ודוקא הדבר שמניחין וכו'</b> -  עיין במ"ב מה שכתבנו בשם הפמ"ג ומביאור הגר"א משמע דכל שהוא נוגע בקדושה עצמה אע"פ שהוא רק לשמירה לבד מ"מ מיקרי תשמישי קדושה [ומה דפסק הרמ"א לענין ארון הבנוי בחומה דמשום שהוא לשמירה לא מקרי תשמישי קדושה צ"ל לדבריו משום דאינו מצוי שיונח ס"ת בתוכו בלא מטפחת ואין הארון נוגע בקדושה עצמה] ועיין מה שכתבנו לקמיה:

<b>Specifically the item placed upon it, etc.</b> See the Mishnah Berurah, where we cited Peri Megadim. According to this, Rama’s ruling that an ark built into a wall is not considered such an article because it is made for protection must mean that a Sefer Torah is ordinarily not placed in it without a mantle, so the ark does not touch the sacred object itself. <b> אבל ארון הבנוי בחומה הנעשה לשמירה וכו'</b> -  הנה דין זה נובע מדברי האור זרוע והובא במרדכי ובהג"א ועי"ש באור זרוע שמפרש הסוגיא מ"ד הני לאו לכבוד עבידן לנטורי בעלמא עבידן קמ"ל דר"ל דלכבוד עבידן ומזה הוציא דכל היכי דלשמירה עבידן לא הוי תשמישי קדושה ועי"ש באור זרוע דכתב בעצמו דנוכל לפרש הסוגיא בהיפך דהיינו דס"ד כיון דלנטורי עבידן כאנדרונא דמיא [וזו היא גירסת הרי"ף והרא"ש] קמ"ל דלא דמי לאנדרונא ולפ"ז אפילו אם הוא עשוי לשמירה יש עליו שם תשמישי קדושה מ"מ הוא מצדד לבסוף שיותר טוב שנפרש כפירוש הראשון אמנם מצאתי במאירי וכן ברבינו ירוחם שמוכח מהם שסוברים כפירוש השני דאפילו כשנעשה לשמירה הוא בכלל תשמישי קדושה ולפ"ז אפשר דלדידהו אפילו כשבנוי בחומה אותו המקום הוא בכלל תשמיש קדושה ואולי דכיון שהוא רק זוית בעלמא חקוק בתוך חומת הכותל נתבטל אותו המקום לגבי שאר הכתלים והוי כאנדרונא בעלמא ואין על זה המקום כ"א קדושת בית הכנסת וצ"ע:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>But an ark built into a wall and made for protection, etc.</b> This ruling derives from Or Zarua and is cited by Mordechai and Hagahos Ashri. Or Zarua explains the Gemara’s phrase, “One might have thought that these were made merely for protection and not for honor; therefore it teaches us otherwise,” to mean that they were in fact made for honor. He derives that an object made for protection is not considered an article serving sacred objects. Or Zarua himself notes that the passage could be interpreted oppositely: one might have thought that because they were made for protection they resemble an ordinary storage room, and the Gemara teaches that they do not. Under that reading, even an article made for protection has the status of an article serving sacred objects. He nevertheless concludes that the first interpretation is preferable. I found, however, that Meiri and Rabbeinu Yerucham clearly follow the second interpretation and regard even something made for protection as an article serving sacred objects. According to them, perhaps even a recess built into a wall has that status. Alternatively, because it is merely a corner hollowed into the wall, it becomes subordinate to the wall itself and resembles an ordinary storage recess, having only the sanctity of the synagogue. This requires study.

<b> וכן נוהגין עכשיו משום לב ב"ד מתנה וכו'</b> -  לכאורה לפי מה שכתבנו במ"ב סקל"ה בשם תה"ד דבס"ת של יחיד שיש לו בביתו אין שייך לב ב"ד מתנה א"כ הכא כיון שבביתו נתקדש מכבר המטפחת לס"ת זו קודם שהקדיש הס"ת לביהכ"נ מה שייך לב ב"ד ע"ז ואולי דנאמר דמיירי בשהיה דעתו מתחלה בשעה שקנה אותה להקדישה לבסוף לבהכ"נ וממילא לא יחדה לזו דוקא אבל א"כ היה לו לרמ"א לפרש זה ומדסתם משמע דבכל גווני שרי וצ"ע:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And this is now the practice because the beis din is presumed to have stipulated, etc.</b> At first glance, according to what we wrote in the Mishnah Berurah, seif katan 35, in the name of Terumas HaDeshen, that the presumed stipulation of beis din does not apply to an individual’s Sefer Torah kept in his house, here too the mantle had already become sanctified for this particular Sefer Torah in his house before he donated the Sefer Torah to the synagogue. How, then, can a presumed stipulation of beis din apply? Perhaps the case is one in which, when he purchased the mantle, he already intended eventually to donate the Sefer Torah to the synagogue, so he never designated the mantle exclusively for that one Sefer Torah. If so, however, Rama should have stated this. His unqualified language implies that it is permitted in every case. This requires study.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Wax candles, etc.</b> See the Mishnah Berurah regarding converting an idolatrous house into a synagogue. Magen Avraham, citing Re’em, is lenient, while Eliyah Rabbah holds that Re’em never intended to permit making it a permanent synagogue. Know that this entire discussion applies only when the non-Jews did not place their idols there. In such a case Magen Avraham inclines to distinguish it from wax candles, which remain repulsive for a mitzvah even after nullification because they had acquired the status of idolatrous ornamentation. The house never acquired the status of idolatrous ornamentation or appurtenance, because no idol stood there; they merely bowed and prayed there to their idols. Magen Avraham also joins the consideration that a house is attached to the ground, but that is not his principal reason, since legally it is treated like detached property, and even an attached object used for worship is prohibited for a mitzvah, as the Gemara proves. If idols were placed there, however, the house acquired the status of an appurtenance of idolatry and was prohibited even for mundane benefit, as Rama rules in Yoreh Deah 145:3. After the idols are removed and the house becomes permitted for ordinary benefit through nullification, it nevertheless remains prohibited for a mitzvah, just as idolatrous ornamentation remains prohibited after nullification. It appears that Magen Avraham alluded to this by referring to siman 145, seif 3. One cannot object from Yoreh Deah 143:2, which permits initially constructing a palace or courtyard containing a dome upon which an idol will be placed. There the idol is not in the house itself but upon the dome, so the house merely serves the dome and is only an appurtenance of an appurtenance. A house specifically designated for placing idols within it and worshipping them is a direct appurtenance of idolatry, as Rama explicitly states in siman 145:3, and it is certainly prohibited for a mitzvah even after nullification. I saw one great authority incline to permit because their practice is to place the idol in a box or similar enclosure, making the house only an appurtenance of an appurtenance. His words are unconvincing. It is well known that many idols stand exposed on the floor or walls. Moreover, the core argument is unsound, because the house was nevertheless designated for idolatry. See Dagul Merevavah in the name of Tosafos, and Ritva there, who writes similarly. Know further that if their house of prayer was merely rented to them, there may be room for leniency even in this case, since they lacked the power to prohibit the house permanently. See Peri Megadim and Ein Yitzchak, Orach Chayim, siman 10.
