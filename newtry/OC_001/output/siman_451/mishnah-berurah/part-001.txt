(3) <b>That were used, etc.</b> New utensils purchased in the marketplace, even earthenware utensils, need not be suspected of prior use, because new utensils are not ordinarily used before sale. Metal utensils require only immersion. If, however, they are purchased from a non-Jew in his home and one does not recognize them as certainly new, metal utensils must, because of the doubt, undergo hagalah or libun according to their manner of use, in addition to immersion. Earthenware utensils must be broken because of the doubt.

(5) <b>Even those in which, etc.</b> Meaning, even though they have absorbed a great quantity of chametz, and if one cooks in them after Pesach the food will certainly not contain sixty times the absorbed chametz, it is nevertheless permitted. We rule that chametz after Pesach is permitted according to the Torah and is forbidden only because the Sages penalized one who retained it. They imposed the penalty only upon actual chametz, not upon flavor absorbed in a utensil. One may therefore cook in these utensils after Pesach even initially.

<b>And if he filled it with coals, etc.</b> The reason is that an ordinary oven tile is not normally prone to crack, and therefore there is no concern that he will spare it and fail to heat it properly. Bricks fired in a kiln have the status of earthenware vessels. If a hot chametz substance was poured onto them, they can be kashered only through proper libun, as will be explained in siman 461. If food was baked or roasted upon them, their use was through fire and they require libun, as stated above.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Knives.</b> Both large and small knives, even if used with hot chametz throughout the year, require only hagalah for Pesach. Although Shulchan Aruch rules in Yoreh Deah 121 that knives that absorbed other prohibitions require libun before being used with hot food, here hagalah alone is permitted initially. This is because some authorities hold that even utensils that absorbed chametz directly through fire require only hagalah, and several authorities maintain that knives are not considered utensils whose primary use is through fire. Even if a knife was once used over fire, such as to hold a piece of pastry over the flame, its usual use is not through fire, and we follow its majority use, as stated below in seif 6. Nevertheless, one who is able should ideally purchase new knives for Pesach. The later authorities further write that although knives that absorbed other prohibitions initially require libun, after the fact, if one performed hagalah and used them with hot food, the food is not forbidden. Knives used for meat may be converted to dairy use through hagalah, but the common custom is to prohibit this. Chasam Sofer, responsum 110, writes that when one performs hagalah for Pesach, he permits changing a knife from meat to dairy use or the reverse, since the kashering was not undertaken for that purpose.

(כב) כל חלודה - דכתיב בפרשת הגעלת כלי מדין אך את הזהב וגו' כלומר רק את הזהב דהיינו כשאין שם חלודה רק זהב בלבד מועלת הגעלה להפליט הבלוע אבל כשיש שם חלודה יש לחוש שמא יש שם משהו מן החמץ והחלודה מכסהו ולזה לא מהני הגעלה כי אין הגעלה למה שהוא בעין. וכ"ז בחלודה שיש בזה קצת ממשות (שקורין ראס"ט) שכשגוררין אותה משם יש שם כמה עפרורין שאז יש לחוש שמא יש תחתיה משהו ממשות האיסור בעין והחלודה מכסהו אבל אם אין בחלודה ממשות כלל רק מראה בלבד כמו שנמצא לפעמים שיש שחרות או אדמימות בצד הפנימי של הכלי שאנו קורין (פלעקי"ן) אין קפידא. וכן הדין באותן כתמים הנעשים בכלי בדיל (שקורין ערד פלעקי"ן) שאין מעכבין את ההגעלה כיון שאין בהם ממשות רק מראה בלבד [אחרונים]:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>All rust.</b> The passage concerning kashering the vessels of Midian states, “Only the gold,” meaning that hagalah works only when there is no rust and only the gold itself remains. Where rust is present, there is concern that a trace of chametz remains beneath it, and hagalah does not remove an actual substance. This applies only to rust with tangible substance, called rust, which produces particles when scraped and may conceal actual prohibited matter beneath it. If the rust is merely discoloration, such as black or red stains on the inside of a utensil, called spots, it does not matter. Likewise, the stains that form on tin vessels, called earth spots, do not impede hagalah because they have no substance and are merely discoloration.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>They require libun.</b> A brief introduction is necessary for several places. The Gemara states that only prohibited matter absorbed into a utensil through fire requires libun. Permitted matter absorbed through fire that later becomes prohibited, such as sacrificial meat roasted on a spit and later becoming nosar, requires only hagalah. The authorities disagree concerning chametz absorbed into a utensil. Some consider this permitted absorption, since when the chametz pastry was absorbed during the year it was permitted, and therefore hagalah suffices. According to them, every utensil used through fire needs only hagalah for Pesach. Others maintain that nosar is different, because at the time of absorption it was not yet nosar at all. Chametz, however, was already chametz when absorbed and remains chametz now; only its prohibition has now taken effect. It is therefore considered prohibited absorption and requires libun. Most authorities agree with this view, and the Mechaber rules accordingly. Nevertheless, one should not automatically apply this ruling elsewhere, because when additional grounds for leniency exist, the view that chametz is considered permitted absorption may be combined with them.
