<b>And some explain, etc.</b> In Tosafos in Perek HaGozel Kamma, cited by the Beis Yosef, it says as follows: Rabbeinu Yitzchak found in a responsum of the Geonim that the upper leaf at the top of the lulav, with no leaf above it, is like two leaves joined together and is called the tiyomes, etc. According to them, we would not find a valid lulav, for with difficulty one finds even one in five hundred that has such a tiyomes. One can say that even according to them, it is invalid only because it was originally like this and then split, changing from its formation. In the Rosh in this chapter it is written here that if the tiyomes was not joined from the beginning, it is valid. Rabbeinu Yitzchak further said that some explain that all lulav leaves are doubled, each one folded into two, and at the head of the lulav, at the end of the spine, two leaves emerge, each folded into two like the other lulav leaves. Those two leaves emerging from the head of the spine are called the tiyomes. Regarding them the question is: what is the law if the tiyomes split, meaning that they split from each other, which means that a little of the spine split? However, most lulavim are not found in this manner. Nevertheless, one can say that the question was asked in case one is found with such a tiyomes and it split. These are the words of Tosafos. The Beis Yosef wrote about this that it appears to speak of lulavim that end in the middle with two leaves, each leaf folded like the other leaves of the lulav, and they are called leaves. According to what Rabbeinu Yitzchak found in the responsum of the Geonim, these two leaves must be literally joined without any separation, as if they were one body, and this is not found in his land. But among us it is often found in a lulav that, on its front side, these two leaves are joined, and the whole head of the lulav appears like one piece of wood without any separation. According to the last explanation written by Rabbeinu Yitzchak, those two leaves need not be joined to each other as one body, but they must be adjacent to each other according to their normal arrangement, so that they appear like two but with no division between them at all. If one leaf split from its fellow, meaning through a slight split in the spine, it is invalid, and even a small portion invalidates, etc. When Tosafos wrote that most lulavim are not found in this manner, that means that many lulavim do not end in the middle with two leaves; nevertheless, although most lulavim are not found this way, the question is relevant where such ones are found. These are the Beis Yosef's words. This implies that he explains the view of the Geonim and Rabbeinu Yitzchak's 'some explain' as one matter, that the spine ends in two leaves, and that the main distinction is that according to the Geonim they must all be joined, while according to the 'some explain' they need be joined only at the spine. This does not appear correct to me at all. If so, why did Tosafos first ask that such joining is not found even in five hundred, implying that they themselves are found, and afterward ask about the 'some explain' that such a lulav is not found at all, even without the joining? Further, why did they need to lengthen the explanation of the 'some explain' regarding the meaning of tiyomes, since that was already explained above? They should merely have said that some explain that the invalidation depends on the spine splitting, not on the joining of the leaves. Their language in the 'some explain' implies that it is another explanation. It appears plain to me that there are two ways to understand the word tiyoma. One is because of their joining and their constant existence together, meaning that the two upper leaves do not have one source; rather, one source is higher than the other, but those two adjacent leaves at the top of the lulav are always stuck together. If that joining separates, the tiyomes has split and is invalid. On this they asked that such joining is not found even in five hundred. The second way to explain tiyomes is because they are born from one place, like twin brothers, who after coming from one source separate. So too, two leaves emerge from one place where the spine ends, and after their emergence from the source we do not care about their being stuck or joined. The invalidation therefore depends on the place of their union in the spine splitting. On this they asked that this is not found at all, that they emerge from one place, and answered that the question was asked: if such a tiyomes is found, what is its law if it split? Above, in their first answer, they changed their wording and wrote, 'even according to them, there is no invalidation, etc.,' because there the main novelty is in their joining, and the question is whether that joining separated. This is clear in their words. I wondered at the Beis Yosef, who departed from this simple path to explain it another way. Tosafos and the Rosh further wrote after the explanation of the Geonim: Baal Halachos Gedolos explains well that 'the tiyoma split' means that back, where they are stuck to each other, the part that matches them and makes them one; what is the law if it split, etc. The Rosh explained the words of Baal Halachos Gedolos like the Rif, that the question is about each leaf that is folded into two, if it separated, namely the splitting of the joined part that connects the fold of each leaf; and this question is about the majority of every leaf. This is the language of the Shulchan Aruch here, which is the language of the Rambam. According to this, there is no greater invalidation in the middle leaf than in the other leaves. The 'some explain' of the Rema is Rashi's view in one version, brought in Terumas HaDeshen, siman 96, in the name of Semak, that if the middle leaf, which is normally folded like the other leaves, split by itself, it is invalid, namely if it split down below the leaves. The Or Zarua wrote about this: I rely on this for practical halachah. I also saw one of the great authorities validate lulavim that were split in the middle leaf at their tops by the width of a finger or more. The Ran brought this explanation of the middle leaf and wrote about it that some are even stringent to say that whenever the matched leaf split, even a minority, it is invalid, and when we permit a split that is not like a hemnek, that is where it split across its width. This is not the plain implication, but I saw fit to be concerned and stringent. Afterward he brought the explanation of the Rif and Rambam cited above, which is entirely lenient regarding the middle leaf. About this the Rema wrote that initially, for the choicest mitzvah, etc. We still must explain the minority that the Ran mentioned according to those who are stringent. It cannot mean any tiny amount at all, for if so, even a fingernail's nick would invalidate, and that is certainly not so; if it were, it should have been counted among the three nicks in the first chapter of Chullin 17, where Rav Chisda also counts nicks of a knife. Since this does not invalidate, it appears that even a measure less than a tefach gives no reason to be stringent. There it says: what is the measure of nicks of the altar? Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai says a tefach; Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov says an olive-volume. They distinguish there between plaster and stone. If so, there is certainly no reason to say here that less than a tefach or an olive-volume invalidates, especially since most poskim hold that the middle leaf has no extra stringency in this matter except together with most of the leaves, meaning the majority of each and every leaf. Why should we be overly stringent about the middle leaf according to this view? So it appears to me. See nearby in the sixth gloss.

<b>Only when it crumbles with the fingernail.</b> He wrote this in order to reconcile the custom. But it has already been explained that most poskim did not agree to this, for if so it would never be invalid. Therefore one who wants to satisfy all the poskim should be careful, when possible, not to take a lulav whose green color has already entirely disappeared.

<b>But if no other is available, etc.</b> In the Beis Yosef he wrote in the name of the Mordechai, in the name of Re'em, that the invalidation taught regarding a cut-off top means only initially, as we find elsewhere that the Mishnah teaches 'invalid' and it means initially; if no other is available, one may recite a berachah over it. The Beis Yosef wrote about this: I do not know what forced Re'em to explain this way, and this is not implied by the words of all the poskim. These are his words. I wondered about this, for the Rambam validates a dry lulav in pressing circumstances, with proof from Rabbi Yehudah's statement: there was an incident with the people of Karkom who would bequeath their lulavim to their descendants. They answered him: pressing circumstances are no proof. The Maggid Mishneh wrote: I saw it written that the Geonim relied on this to say that with a dry lulav, a cut-off top, and all invalid items, one fulfills the obligation in pressing circumstances even on the first day. These are his words. Although the Ra'avad disagrees with allowing dry ones in pressing circumstances and says that they bequeathed them only as a remembrance, not to recite a berachah over them, the Maggid Mishneh wrote there that the Ramban rejected this and holds like the Rambam, and this is reasonable. Thus the Rambam, Ramban, and Maggid Mishneh in the name of the Geonim wrote to permit in pressing circumstances. It is only that the Tur holds, according to his understanding of the Rambam, that pressing circumstances help only with dry, not with other invalidations, as he wrote at the end of siman 649, and he wrote there: this does not appear to me, and this is the view of the Rosh. How then did the Beis Yosef write that it is against all the poskim? Therefore the Rema ruled well here. See siman 646 regarding the words of the Mordechai that we mentioned about this, and the end of siman 649. In the Minhagim it is written: if its top was cut off, it is valid, and some say it is invalid; if no other is available, one recites a berachah over it. These words cannot be upheld as written, for the explicit Mishnah says that a cut-off top is invalid. And if he is speaking of pressing circumstances, why does he then conclude by deciding what to do when no other is available? The one who invalidates invalidates even in pressing circumstances. It appears to me that there is a scribal error there, and it should read: if its top was cut off, it is invalid, and some say that if no other is available one recites a berachah over it. This is the dispute of the Rambam and Ra'avad that we mentioned. Regarding the invalidation of a cut-off top, the Ran wrote on the Mishnah that 'its top' means any amount. So too what the Gemara says, that cut, split, and bent are invalid, refers to this middle leaf, because they made the top of the lulav like the nose of an esrog, where even the smallest amount invalidates. These are his words. It appears to me that he concluded this comparison to an esrog only regarding cut off, for regarding split we say that as long as it did not split like a hemnek it is valid, even if you explain, as the forced explanations do, that this refers to across its width. In any event, it is certainly not invalid in every way that an esrog is invalid. Rather, the Ran said this only to teach that this also refers to the middle leaf, but not to compare it to an esrog even in this respect. Even if you say that the Ran holds regarding split as well that any amount invalidates, as the Beis Yosef understood from him at the end of this siman regarding bent, it will be difficult, for regarding bent we certainly do not rule like him to forbid any bend, but like the Rosh, who disagrees and forbids only when it is bent in its body, not at its top, as the Shulchan Aruch wrote at the end of this siman. There is no choicest-mitzvah reason there to invalidate any bend at its top; on the contrary, it is more valid. Why then should a split of any amount invalidate? They were all stated together. Rather, certainly what I wrote is primary, and this appears to me clear.
