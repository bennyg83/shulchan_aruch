<b>ולא</b> עט״ז המחבר נקט לשון הר״מ ז״ל בפ״ח הג״ד בסוכה ולולב ועלי החריות הוא מברייתא ל״ב א׳ חרות פסול וכפירש״י ז״ל ענף הדקל עליו נושרים ושדרו מתקשה ונעשה עץ והב״י פי׳ דאין נושרים רק העלים במקום חיבורן בשדרה נתקשו וא״א לאגדן ולכופתן לשדרה יע״ש. והנה המ״מ שם מפרש להר״מ ז״ל כל שעליו תלוין למטה כעלי החריות פסול וזהו פי׳ הברייתא חרות פסול והיינו אע״פ שלא נתקשה ויכול לכפותן מ״מ הואיל והן תלוין למטה ואין עולין מאליהן כלל עם השדרה לת״ק ג״כ פסול הא אם עולין קצת מאליהן עם השדרה אע״פ שבראשן תלויות וכפופים קצת למטה כשר ולכתחלה מצוה לאגדן בזה וכמ״ש באות ב׳ אי״ה. ומ״ש הט״ז נעקר״ו כתבתי בזה פי׳ הב״י ולקמן אבאר עוד:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And not.</b> See Turei Zahav. The Mechaber used the Rambam's language in chapter 8, halachah 4, of Hilchos Sukkah and Lulav. The term 'leaves of charayos' comes from the baraisa on 32a: a charus is invalid. According to Rashi, this means a palm branch whose leaves fall away and whose spine hardens and becomes wood. The Beis Yosef explained that the leaves do not actually fall; rather, the leaves at their points of attachment to the spine harden and cannot be bound and tied to the spine; see there. The Maggid Mishneh there explains the Rambam that whenever its leaves hang downward like the leaves of charayos, it is invalid. This is the explanation of the baraisa, 'charus is invalid,' meaning that although it has not hardened and one can force the leaves upward, nevertheless, since they hang downward and do not rise at all on their own with the spine, it is invalid even according to the first tanna. But if they rise somewhat on their own with the spine, although their tips hang and bend somewhat downward, it is valid; initially it is a mitzvah to bind them in that case, as I wrote in letter 2, with Hashem's help. What the Turei Zahav wrote about 'were uprooted,' I explained according to the Beis Yosef, and below I will explain further.

<b>דהיינו</b> עט״ז המ״מ שם פ״ח מהל׳ לולב ה״ג. ובאות א׳ רציתי לפרש ויש חילוק בין אם ראשי העלין כפופים למטה או לאו. ועתה ראיתי הפי׳ הנכון הוא כך דחריות עלים תלוין למטה ובמקום חיבוריהן בשדרה נתקשו וא״א לכפותן וכאלו נשרו ונדלדלו וכמ״ש הב״י וזה שכתב הר״מ ז״ל נדלדלו כעלי החריות. ומשמע הא לא נתקשו כעץ רק תלויות כך למטה ואין עולין מאליהן לשדרה רק ע״י כפיתה כשר הוא וי״ל כה״ג נמי פסול הוא כל שאין עולה מאליהן ומש״ה מצוה ממובחר ליטול לילב שאין עלין תלויות למטה אע״פ שיכול לכפרתן ולאוגדן די״ל כה״ג פסול הוא אבל מדינא כל הראוי לבילה כו׳ כשר ודוקא כחרות שנתקשה וא״א לכפותן פסול אבל כל שעולין מאליהן עם השדרה אע״פ שפרודות קצת משדרה אף מצוה ממובחר ליכא וזהו דהקשה על הר״ב בס״א שכתב שאין פרודות לגמרי דבהא מצוה ליכא רק בתלויות למטה כעלי החריות אלא שאין נתקשו ויכול לכפותן בזה י״א שפסול ומש״ה מצוה לצאת כל הדיעות. ולפי״ז מ״ש הר״ב אם נתקשו עלים וא״י לחברן עם השדרה א״י מה הוא דהא זה בכלל חריות וי״ל עלים נתקשו למעלה אף שעולין בצדדים עם השדרה פסול דאין ראוי לבילה וכפות בעינן עי׳ בהר״ן וצ״ע בכל זה גם במ״מ יש לעיין בפירושו. ומ״ש הט״ז דהיינו כו׳ דקשה ליה דלכאורה הר״מ ז״ל מפרש נדלדלו כפירש״י ז״ל נעקרו ממקום חיבורן בשדרה (ומעורין במיעוט) ופי׳ תלוין למטה הוא פי׳ אחר וחולק ואיך כתב הר״ב דהיינו וז״א דיש לפרש נדלדלו ג״כ תלוין למטה ונדלדלו מאורך השדרה ואף לא נעקרו מחיבורן והבן זה ואין להאריך עי׳ אות ג׳:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Meaning.</b> See Turei Zahav and the Maggid Mishneh there, chapter 8 of Hilchos Lulav, halachah 3. In letter 1 I wanted to explain that there is a distinction whether the tips of the leaves are bent downward or not. Now I see that the correct explanation is this: charayos are leaves hanging downward, and at their points of attachment to the spine they hardened and cannot be forced upward, as if they had fallen and become loose, as the Beis Yosef wrote. This is what the Rambam wrote, that they became loose like the leaves of charayos. It implies that if they did not harden like wood, but merely hang downward in this way and do not rise by themselves to the spine, except through tying, they are valid. One can also say that even in such a case they are invalid whenever they do not rise on their own; therefore it is a choicest mitzvah to take a lulav whose leaves do not hang downward, although one can force and bind them, because one can say that such a case is invalid. As a matter of law, however, anything fit for mixing, etc., is valid, and only something like charus, which hardened and cannot be forced upward, is invalid. But whenever the leaves rise on their own with the spine, even though somewhat separated from the spine, there is not even a choicest-mitzvah concern. This is what he asked on the Rema in se'if 1, who wrote that they should not be fully separated, for in that case there is no mitzvah concern; the concern is only when they hang downward like the leaves of charayos, except that they have not hardened and can be forced upward. In that case some say it is invalid, and therefore it is a mitzvah to satisfy all opinions. According to this, what the Rema wrote, 'if the leaves hardened and he cannot join them to the spine,' I do not know what it means, for this is included in charayos. One can say he means that the leaves hardened above, even though they rise along the sides with the spine; it is invalid, because it is not fit for binding, and we require bound. See the Ran. All this requires analysis, and the Maggid Mishneh's explanation also requires study. What the Turei Zahav wrote, 'that is, etc.,' is because it was difficult to him: at first glance the Rambam explains 'became loose' like Rashi, that they were uprooted from their place of attachment in the spine and remain attached in a minority, while the explanation 'hanging downward' is another explanation that disagrees. How then could the Rema write 'that is'? This is not so, for 'became loose' can also mean that they hang downward and became loose along the length of the spine, even though they were not uprooted from their attachment. Understand this, and there is no need to elaborate. See letter 3.

<b>And similarly.</b> See Turei Zahav, Sukkah 32a: charus is invalid, similar to charus is valid, according to Rashi. See letters 1 and 2. It appears to me, the lowly one, that the Turei Zahav has a special intention here, as I will explain. In the Mishnah, 'separated' is valid and 'split apart' is invalid. Some explain simply: separated means somewhat distant from the spine; split apart means hanging downward like leaves of charayos, but not hardened, and one can force and bind them, and nevertheless it is invalid. This is the Rambam's language there, using the comparison 'like the leaves of charayos.' The baraisa says: charus is invalid, similar to charus is valid, meaning anything fit for mixing; and the Mishnah rules that a similar case is invalid. This is what the Rema wrote, 'and all the more so if they were detached.' Although the phrase 'all the more so' would mean like Rashi, that they were uprooted at their attachment, even though they remain attached in a minority and can be bound, they are invalid. The same applies to leaves of charayos: even though he can force them upward with binding, they are invalid, for we do not multiply disputes between them. In the Maggid Mishneh and Ran one can say that Rashi and the Rambam are saying one thing. Understand that the invalidation is because they do not rise with the spine. If they rise but are separated and hardened so that they cannot be forced upward, that too is invalid. For the choicest mitzvah they should not be fully separated, as the Rema says in se'if 1; but one can say that 'similar to charus' means it began to harden, as the Turei Zahav wrote. Understand this.

<b>And some explain.</b> See Turei Zahav. The Beis Yosef brought Tosafos, Bava Kamma 96a, s.v. 'the tiyomes split.' Leaves are called hotzin in their natural formation, each folded into two, with its front separated and its back joined as if it were one; a chufya is when one leaf separated into two at the back; see there. Tosafos there brought the explanations of the Geonim, Rashi, and the Maggid Mishneh. All speak of those lulavim that end above in two leaves, meaning that at the top of the spine two leaves emerge, each folded and double. According to the Geonim, those two upper leaves must be connected at their backs, while at their fronts they are separated, as two upper leaves. See the Beis Yosef, who wrote that sometimes, opposite their fronts, a red thing is also found between them, joining them as if the two leaves actually appear as one. The back of the leaves is on the back of the spine and the fronts of the leaves are separated like the other leaves; the two upper leaves must be connected at their backs, and see siman 651. Whenever the tiyoma, meaning those two leaves, split from one another at their fronts, even though their backs remain fully stuck together, it is invalid. About this Tosafos wrote that so appears from Baal Halachos Gedolos. The Rosh in Sukkah 32 explained that Baal Halachos Gedolos means that 'the tiyomes split' refers to the back of the leaves, namely the two upper leaves; therefore he speaks of the back, while at their fronts they are separated like other leaves, and there is a red-looking substance between them at the front that joins their backs together. This invalidation is when the majority of the length of their backs split; see the Rosh and Tur. About this Tosafos wrote that in their country, one valid lulav in five hundred was not found where the two leaves were joined at their backs. It is found and common for there to be two leaves at the top of the spine, a common minority, but for their backs to be separated; only one in five hundred would be valid, and it is almost never found. About this they wrote that if from the beginning of their formation they were born with their backs separated, one can say it is valid, as the Rosh concluded. Afterward they brought Rashi's explanation. According to him there is a leniency: as long as the spine above did not split so much that the two leaves at the head of the spine appear divided from each other, it is valid. Rabbeinu Yitzchak's explanation in the name of 'some explain' is somewhat more stringent than Rashi: whenever a little of the spine split so that the two leaves are not adjacent to each other, it is invalid; but as long as they are truly adjacent, even if their backs are not stuck together, it is valid. According to the Geonim, however, whenever the majority of the upper leaves are not actually attached at their backs as one body, they are invalid. About Rabbeinu Yitzchak's explanation in the name of 'some explain,' Tosafos wrote that although there is a small difficulty, that the question should have been asked simply about a split tiyoma of the two upper leaves, for most lulavim do not grow this way, since most have only one leaf at the top of the spine, nevertheless they are comfortable saying that the question was about the minority that is found. This is unlike the explanation of the Geonim, where it is almost never found that the majority of those two leaves are attached at their backs. This is how the matter appears to one who examines the Beis Yosef, and the words of the Turei Zahav are somewhat resolved. The Turei Zahav explains the view of the Geonim this way: initially, two leaves are connected at their backs, just as the other leaves are folded and connected at their backs. So too these two upper neighboring leaves, which are adjacent to each other, with no spine separating them except that at their root a little of the spine separates, must be connected at their backs; when separated, there are four leaves there. Nevertheless, they do not emerge from one root, but one above the other like the other lulav leaves, and it is called tiyoma because their backs are attached. The explanation in the name of 'some say' is that they are not attached at their backs; they are called tiyoma because they emerged from one root in the spine, from one actual place, and if a little of the spine split at their root, it is invalid. About this they also asked that such a thing is not really found, even slightly, from one root; see there. As to practical halachah, the Mechaber and Rema did not bring the view of the Geonim or the 'some say' at all, but only the explanation of the Rif and Rambam, namely the back of the majority of the leaves of the lulav, and the explanation of the middle leaf, where there is only one leaf at the top, one leaf folded in two like the other leaves, in the name of Terumas HaDeshen in the name of Rashi. That means that whenever the majority of the upper leaf separated at the back, one should invalidate it. For the choicest mitzvah, it should not be separated at all; but such a thing is not actually found, that the minority of the tiyoma leaf above is not separated. I was uncertain about a lulav that has two leaves above and is split at the back more than half, and a lulav that has one leaf in such a case split in the majority: which is preferable without a berachah? Likewise, when they are split in the minority, with a berachah, which is preferable? With Hashem's help, in Magen Avraham letter 4 and in the Turei Zahav below I will explain further.
