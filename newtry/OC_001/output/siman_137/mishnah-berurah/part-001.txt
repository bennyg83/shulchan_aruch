(4) And whichever of them. Meaning, whoever reads more is more praiseworthy. If the first reads four verses, there is reason to favor him because we always find that the more important person precedes. The middle reader also has an advantage, as we find that the middle lamp of the menorah is distinguished. If the last reads four, one may say that we ascend in holiness and do not descend. Therefore, we are not particular which of them acts promptly and reads four, since each has a rationale why he is fitting for it. In the time of the Gemara, those called to the Torah read for themselves. Nowadays, when the chazzan reads for them, the later authorities disagree. Some say that he should read three verses for the first and second and four for the last; others disagree. The widespread custom now is that at Minchah on Shabbos and on Monday and Thursday, the entire first section of the weekly portion to be read the coming Shabbos is read, as printed in the siddurim, with the divisions marked for three people. If one departs from this order, it does not invalidate the reading, provided that no person reads fewer than three verses. Shaarei Efraim.

(11) Their law is like, etc. This refers to a case where the five readers each read at least three verses, as required. Otherwise, he certainly returns and reads. Magen Avraham writes that the same applies if one omitted a verse from those read from the second Sefer Torah by the maftir as the obligation of the day, namely the verses of the Mussaf offering. He does not return unless the essential body of those verses was entirely omitted. For example, on Shabbos that is the first day of Chol HaMoed Sukkos, when the maftir reads “On the second day” and “On the third day,” if he omitted “On the third day,” then although he read “On the second day,” it is considered as though he did not read the obligation of the day at all, since we are expert in determining the calendar. However, if he read “On the third day” but omitted “On the second day,” he does not return and read with a blessing. Rather, he opens the Sefer Torah and reads those verses before the congregation without a blessing. Eliyah Rabbah adds that sometimes even omitting a single verse requires repetition with a blessing, namely when that verse contains the principal obligation of the day. Examples are: if on the first day of Pesach he omitted from “You shall offer” through “to atone for you,” since what follows is not as directly related to the day's obligation; if on the first day of Sukkos he omitted any verse from “You shall offer” through the end of the portion; or if on the first day of Chol HaMoed that falls on Shabbos he omitted any verse from the section “On the third day.” Shaarei Efraim agrees.

(13) He must return and read. Regarding the opening blessing, the law is as follows. If he has not yet recited the concluding blessing, then even if the Sefer Torah was already rolled and he intended to recite the blessing, but they remembered that he read only two verses, he returns and reads without another opening blessing. If he already recited the concluding blessing, the first reading has completely ended. He must therefore recite the opening blessing again and begin from the place where he began the previous reading, because those two verses did not count at all, and read one additional verse to complete three. If the two preceding readers each read only three verses, he must read four verses so that the total reaches ten. Even if the next person was already called and ascended to the bimah, that person should remain there while the first recites the blessing again, reads the three verses as explained, and recites the concluding blessing; afterward the next person reads. If the second person already began the blessing, or completed the entire blessing, before they remembered, he should begin from where the previous person began, including the same two earlier verses, complete one additional verse, and recite the concluding blessing. Some say that in addition to the two earlier verses he should read three more. The first person should remain there until the second finishes. Afterward, the first person who had read two verses recites the blessing and continues from where the second stopped. If the first already descended from the bimah before they remembered, another person may be called to complete the three readers. This applies when there was no kohen in the beis midrash and Yisraelim were called. But if this happened to a kohen who read only two verses, and then the levi recited his blessing, and they remembered that the kohen erred, the levi should begin only from where the kohen stopped and read three verses from there onward. He should not repeat the first two verses read by the kohen, because on a weekday the reading is valid even if the beginning of the portion is omitted, and repeating from the beginning would cast aspersion on the kohanim present. After the levi, two Yisraelim should be called to complete the prescribed number, because the first kohen who erred cannot be called after the levi, since a kohen may not read after a levi. The reading of only two verses is considered nonexistent. If this occurred at Shacharis on Shabbos or Yom Tov, where one of the readers read two verses and recited the concluding blessing before the mistake was noticed, some say it is preferable that he not read again himself but that another person be called. On a weekday, since one may not add to the prescribed number of readers, calling another person in his place would appear as though four people were called. On Shabbos, where additions are permitted, it is better to call another person than for the same person to read twice from one Sefer Torah, which should not initially be done, as explained in siman 282. The new reader begins from where the first began, as above, and they complete seven readers besides the one who read only two verses. Others say that even on Shabbos there is no need to call another person.

**** ENGLISH ****
(18) From the beginning of the portion. It does not suffice to begin where he initially stopped, because since they did not read in the order established by the Sages, there was no valid reading. Nevertheless, the later authorities agree that if he merely omitted a verse he does not return, since these portions are read on their respective Shabbosos, as explained above in seif 3 regarding festival portions. However, this applies only if he omitted verses that are not part of the principal obligation of the day, namely up to “the stranger who resides among them.” If he omitted from the principal obligation of the day, Eliyah Rabbah agrees that he returns, as explained above in Mishnah Berurah seif katan 11 regarding the verses of the Mussaf offering.
