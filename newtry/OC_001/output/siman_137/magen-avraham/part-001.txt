**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And two with him.</b> Because fewer than three verses are not read from the Torah. Maharam Lublin, siman 85, writes: On Shabbos, the first day of Chol HaMoed Sukkos, the chazzan read for maftir “On the second day,” rolled the Sefer Torah, omitted “On the third day,” and had already recited the concluding blessing. I ruled that they should reopen the Sefer Torah and the chazzan should read aloud from “On the third day” through the end without a blessing before or after. Since “On the third day” is read only because of doubt, no blessing is recited, as we find regarding sitting in the sukkah on Shemini Atzeres, where we do not recite a blessing. Although one who forgot Parashas Parah recites a blessing, as stated in seif 5, Parashas Parah is different because it is D'Oraisa; see what I wrote in siman 685. Although we are expert in determining the calendar, they were not stringent regarding festival readings, as Terumas HaDeshen, siman 24, writes. One might argue that Terumas HaDeshen discusses only the other festival portions, whereas the Mussaf offerings are obligations of the day and he would require repetition if they were omitted. Nevertheless, since he wrote without qualification, it implies that he does not distinguish. Moreover, even on Shabbos, where Hagahos Maimoniyos writes that one returns if he omitted a verse, it may be that he reads without a blessing. For all these reasons I ruled that it be read without a blessing, though one who rules that a blessing should be recited has not lost. These are his words. However, that rabbi did not see the final chapter of Maseches Sofrim, which explicitly states that if one omitted a verse on Shabbos, he reopens the Sefer Torah, recites a blessing, and reads; see there. Regarding the festival Mussaf portions, however, there is room for doubt. His inference from the unqualified wording of Terumas HaDeshen is not compelling. It appears that he saw only Beit Yosef and not the body of Terumas HaDeshen's ruling. Terumas HaDeshen discusses the practice in some places where the congregation recites the Song at the Sea and the Ten Commandments together with the chazzan and does not listen to the chazzan, and after analyzing the matter writes: “On the seventh day of Pesach and on Shavuos there is not so much concern, since these portions were already read on their respective Shabbosos and resemble the Monday and Thursday readings.” Thus, Terumas HaDeshen did not come to teach the law of festival readings, but mentioned it only incidentally, and nothing can be inferred from his unqualified wording. Tosafos, Megillah 23a, write that if the full prescribed number read on Yom Tov but they forgot to read the obligation of the day, they should reopen the Sefer Torah and another person should read the obligation of the day; the last person who read previously is considered as though he did not read. It appears to me that because Tosafos wrote without qualification, they hold that one returns on all festivals. This is not only on Sukkos, where the Gemara requires reading the festival offerings, but even on the other festivals, where the reading of the day's offerings was instituted by Rabbi Akiva, as Tosafos and the Rosh write there, and as Beit Yosef writes in siman 282 in the name of Shibolei HaLeket. Even so, they must be read, for what difference is there whether they were read immediately or later? No additional blessing is caused by the delay. But if one omitted a single verse, he does not return, for it is no worse than the principal festival portion mentioned in the Gemara, which nevertheless does not require repetition. All the more so is this true of the Mussaf portion. In our case, however, where he omitted “On the third day,” it is as though he did not read at all, since perhaps today is the third day. This is especially so for us, who are expert in determining the calendar and know it is certainly the third day. His statement that no blessing is recited because of the doubt requires study, for every second day of Yom Tov is observed because of doubt, yet we recite many blessings over Kiddush and Havdalah because the Sages instituted them. See Tosafos, Sukkah 44b, and Shabbos 23a. The reason we do not recite a blessing over the sukkah on Shemini Atzeres is as Maharil writes, so as not to violate bal tosif, or because people might belittle Yom Tov, as Tosafos write regarding lulav. Therefore, it appears obvious to me that he should recite a blessing, as stated above in the name of Tosafos. See Levush.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>It does not.</b> This implies that it is permitted, but does not count toward the prescribed number. See siman 282, seif 2. This requires study, for when Parashas Shekalim falls in Parashas Tetzaveh, the Gemara states that six people read from “And you shall command” through “And you shall make a laver,” and the maftir repeats from “When you take” through “And you shall make.” Nevertheless, the maftir counts toward the prescribed number, and so writes the Tur in siman 685. One cannot answer that there too it is because no alternative is possible, for it is possible to add another reader so that the maftir is not included in the prescribed number. It is also possible for the earlier reader to continue three verses beyond “When you take,” and for the seventh reader to repeat from “When you take” and add beyond what the first read. One may answer that the Parashas Shekalim reading must stand alone and not include anything beyond it. Nevertheless, it remains difficult: the sixth reader could read until three verses after “When you take,” and the seventh could repeat from “When you take” and add beyond what the first read. One may answer that if so, people would say the weekly portion ends at “for your souls.” But now, since the maftir adds nothing and such repetition is ordinarily prohibited, it is evident that it is done for Parashas Shekalim. Perhaps for this reason he need not add. This requires study.
