**** ENGLISH ****
Section 1. One must juxtapose [the blessing of] redemption to the prayer, etc. Note: The reason is explained in the Yerushalmi, cited in the Tur, by way of a parable of a king's beloved who knocked at his door; the king went out to seek him, to learn what he wanted, and found that he had already departed, so the king too departed. Based on this, one can explain what the Sages said on the verse, “and I have done what is good in Your eyes,” that it refers to one who juxtaposed redemption to prayer. Many have already wondered how they derived specifically this (and I have already stated my piece on this elsewhere). Now it appears thus: it was difficult for [the expositor] why it says “the good in Your eyes,” which implies “but not in my eyes,” along the lines of what the Sages expounded on the verse “and the matter was good in my eyes,” which Rashi explained as “but not in the eyes of the Omnipresent.” So here too the intent is the reverse: “the good in Your eyes, and not in the eyes of man.” And Scripture is careful about this, saying several times in Parashas Shoftim, “when you do what is right and good in the eyes of Hashem,” doubling the expression, “the good and the right” or “the right and the good.” And there is a teaching in the Yalkut: “the good in the eyes of Heaven and right in the eyes of man,” and another expounds the reverse (see there the dispute of R' Yishmael and R' Akiva). In any case, [Scripture] is careful to specify that it is also proper in the eyes of man, so that it should not appear that, Heaven forbid, it is not good in the eyes of man. If so, here too [David] should likewise have said, “the good and the right in Your eyes I have done”; why did he mention “the good” alone? Therefore he explains that the intent is upon juxtaposing redemption to prayer, for the need for this stems from the reason of the Yerushalmi, as in the parable of the king's beloved who knocked at the king's door and the king went out to learn what he wanted, etc. If so, this applies only to one whom [the king] truly loves and who is esteemed in his eyes, so that the moment he knocks the king himself goes out to greet him at once; [only for such a one] does the reason apply. But for one who is not his beloved, the king does not go out to greet him immediately upon his knock, but waits until he cries out and pleads; and even if he does go out, he goes out only by sending a messenger and the like. And so it is with Him, may He be blessed. In truth, one who is like R' Chiya and his sons, then the moment they said “He makes the wind blow,” their praise was accepted like their prayer, as the Sages said regarding Shmuel HaKatan: “such is the praise of the community,” he said “He makes the wind blow” and the wind blew, etc. If so, [only] the select individuals, the exalted ones, merit that while they are mentioning the praise, He, may He be blessed, immediately goes out Himself to greet them. But the masses do not merit this, that He, may He be blessed, go out to greet them immediately, before the prayer; and even afterward, would that their prayer be accepted by means of an angel. If so, for these the reason of the need to juxtapose redemption to prayer does not apply. And accordingly, even regarding the great righteous, since they require exceeding humility (and Moshe Rabbeinu, peace be upon him, is proof), how could they make themselves His beloved, to esteem themselves at such a height, and how would they not be concerned for arrogance, Heaven forbid, in juxtaposing redemption to prayer? Therefore, were it not that He, may He be blessed, desires this, they would not be permitted to do so with intent; only that He, may He be blessed, desires it, and He regards all Israel as the children of kings and as His beloved, and He Himself requested that they juxtapose redemption to prayer. Therefore [David] said, “and I have done what is good in Your eyes,” for in Your eyes it is good, and not as in my eyes, for I know that I have not attained this level; only since in Your eyes it is good, I did so. Therefore, of necessity, the intent is upon juxtaposing redemption to prayer, and this is well settled, and it is easily understood.
