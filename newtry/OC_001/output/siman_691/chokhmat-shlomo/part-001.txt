<b>Seif 2.</b> In the gloss: after the fact, one should not invalidate a Megillah because of missing and extra letters, for it is no worse than a case where the scribe omitted letters from it, where it is valid. Note: although the Rema ruled this simply, it is not agreed upon. The main proof that even extra letters are valid comes from the rule that if the scribe omitted letters, it is valid, for this view treats extra as comparable to missing. But one who examines Tosafos in the first chapter of Megillah, page 9, s.v. “granted, Torah,” where they cite the Kuntres, will see that they hold a change is worse than an omission: even where an omission is valid, a change invalidates. If so, here too, although omitted letters are valid, if he changed the text by writing extra letters, that should invalidate, for it is a change. Although Tosafos gave another answer, the matter has not left doubt. Therefore it appears that with missing letters it is valid, as the Rema wrote, but with extra letters it is invalid out of doubt; if there is no other Megillah, at any rate one should not bless over it. See my novellae there on the words of Tosafos, and examine this carefully.

Regarding whether one fulfills Megillah reading from writing when alone, and the explanation of the sugya in Megillah 18 concerning “he was writing it,” see my responsum to the holy community of Krakow in my work on Shulchan Aruch, Yoreh De'ah, fourth edition, siman 48. While dealing with this, I was troubled: what is the Gemara’s challenge there to Rabbi Chelbo from Rabbi Meir, who had no Megillah and wrote it? Perhaps Rabbi Meir holds like the opinion in the first chapter of Megillah that Esther was said with ruach hakodesh to be read but not to be written, and that its permission to be written is only Rabbinic. If so, it is not included in the rule that written matters may not be said by heart, and therefore it is permitted to write it not from a written text, all the more so by heart. Other Writings, however, which were given to be written by Torah law, may not be written not from a written text, just as they may not be read by heart. Although the Talmud proves in the first chapter of Megillah that Rabbi Meir agrees that Esther renders the hands tamei and holds that it was said to be written, perhaps there are two Tannaim according to Rabbi Meir, and Rabbi Yehoshua there, who holds it was not given to be written, is disagreeing within Rabbi Meir’s own view. Perhaps this is forced; see the omissions in Sefer HaChayim at the beginning, siman 49, where I asked about this.
<br><b>There, seif 2 in the gloss.</b> And to utter every word with his mouth before writing it, as with a sefer Torah. Note: see the Magen Avraham, who wondered about this. In my humble opinion, it is well understood according to Tosafos in Megillah, page 7, who wrote that its being given to be written is only Rabbinic, and therefore one who read it by heart has not fulfilled the obligation Rabbinically. From their words it is proven that even according to the view that it renders the hands tamei and derives this from “in a book,” it is still only Rabbinic. So too the Maharsha appears to have understood in Chiddushei Halachos; otherwise, what did Tosafos ask from Yoma? One could say that there it speaks according to the view that it was given to be written, namely the view that it renders the hands tamei, while the view that it was not given to be written means only that it does not render the hands tamei, though it agrees that Rabbinically it was given to be written. Therefore the Gemara asks why Chanukah is different, since it too could be written Rabbinically. Necessarily, Tosafos hold that even the opinion that it was given to be written means only Rabbinically.

Although this opinion derives it from “in a book,” one can explain that at first they thought “in a book” cannot refer to the Megillah, only to Neviim. Since it is written “Have I not written for you thirds,” it implies one may not make a fourth division; where there is a prohibition, the Sages cannot permit it actively. But afterward they found that “in a book” can be interpreted about the Megillah. Even though it can also be interpreted about Neviim, since at any rate there is no Torah prohibition, the Sages again had power to enact writing it. All agree, however, that this is only Rabbinic, though it renders the hands tamei Rabbinically. It is preferable to minimize dispute. The Talmud says only that the one who says it does not render the hands tamei must hold it is only Rabbinic; but the reverse is not necessary, for the one who says it does render the hands tamei can still agree that its writing is only Rabbinic.

If so, the matter is clear. For tefillin, a sefer Torah, and the like, where writing is from the Torah, writing from a written text is as though he says it with his mouth. But Megillah itself was said as words of Kabbalah and is like Torah, while its writing is only Rabbinic. Since the Sages were stringent to make it render the hands tamei like Torah, they were also stringent that it not be written except from a written text. Therefore we follow stringency in both directions: writing not from a written text is forbidden because Rabbinically it was given to be written and is considered written matter; but a written text alone is not enough, because from Torah law it was not given to be written, and where it is not from a Torah-level written text he must say it by heart, as with tefillin. They made it like Torah for stringency, not for leniency.

The matter can be understood further: the dispute whether it was given to be written concerns whether it has the status of an independent book, like other books, which have a mitzvah of being written even apart from public reading. One view says it was given to be written independently, and therefore has the status of a complete book to render the hands tamei. The other view says that in its own right it was not given to be written as a book; it was given to be written only for reading from it, so that one not read by heart. Without the need for reading, it has no status of a book and does not render the hands tamei. According to us, although it renders the hands tamei, it was given independent book status only Rabbinically; from words of Kabbalah it was given to be written only for reading. “Write this as a remembrance in a book” means for the sake of reading. Thus a Megillah written for reading has words-of-Kabbalah book status, but the existing Megillah from which one writes, when not at the moment of reading, has book status only Rabbinically. Therefore relying on it alone is like writing not from a written text, and one must also recite by heart as in other cases of writing not from a proper written text. This is a correct approach with Hashem’s help. What the Talmud answers on page 7, that it was said to be read and not said to be written, means it was said to be written only for reading, not as an independent book unrelated to reading, and therefore it does not have book status to render the hands tamei. Examine this carefully.

א"ש דבזה הוי ממ"נ ומוכח מכולם דאין א' מוציא חבירו בפחות מיו"ד רק באינו בקי וא"ש ודו"ק היטב:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>There, seif 8.</b> One does not read publicly from a Megillah written among the Writings, and so on. Note: this is the language of the Rambam in chapter 2 of Megillah, and so too is the language of the Tur: if an individual read from it, and so on. At first glance, their words contradict each other. From “one does not read from it publicly,” the intent must be to discharge the public obligation, implying that whenever it is not a community, even if there are nine and one reads for all, it is valid. But afterward they wrote that an individual reads from it and fulfills his obligation, implying only the reader himself fulfills, but not that he can discharge another even outside a public setting.

Regarding the Rambam and Shulchan Aruch one could say there are three distinctions: publicly, even after the fact one has not fulfilled; an individual reading for himself may read initially; but an individual reading to discharge others should not initially do so, lest we decree because of public reading, though after the fact they fulfill because they are not a community. This can be said for the Rambam, who holds an individual may read initially. But the Tur wrote, “if it was attached with them and he read from it as an individual, he fulfilled,” using after-the-fact language, implying that even an individual himself should not do so initially. His words are therefore difficult: first he implies only the reader himself, not one discharging another, and afterward he implies only public reading is invalid, while private reading for others works.

It seems to me that from here there is proof for the Magen Avraham’s discussion above in siman 689, seif katan 10, whether when alone one can discharge another even if the other is expert, or only if the other is not expert. From here it is proven that the Tur also holds that one cannot discharge an expert fellow when fewer than ten are present. In the Yerushalmi, chapter 2, halachah 4, it says that if it was written as Chumashim, one does not read from it before the many, and Rabbi Tanchuma said this is because of the unlearned. The Korban HaEdah’s explanation that the unlearned will say it is a haftarah is forced, especially according to the Ran, who explains it as a matter of public honor.

Rather, our Talmud says “with a community,” while the Yerushalmi says “before the many.” A community means ten according to the Tur, while fewer than ten is not a community; but “many” can mean even three. Thus the Yerushalmi teaches that even fewer than ten may not read from such a Megillah. The difficulty is that if it were not written as Chumashim, it would imply that one could read for many, though one cannot discharge another with fewer than ten. If it were speaking of ten, it should have said community. Therefore it says because of the unlearned: where they are not expert, one individual can discharge another, and nevertheless if it is written among the Writings he does not fulfill.

One can also explain simply: our Talmud says a foreign-language speaker who heard it in Ashuris fulfilled the obligation, even though he does not know what is being said, because the mitzvah is reading and publicizing the miracle. Thus one who does not understand fulfills only through reading and publicizing. The Yerushalmi says “because of the unlearned,” meaning those who understand the language fulfill when they hear it is the Megillah even from Chumashim; but those who do not understand fulfill only through reading and publicizing, and if there is no visible distinction, there is no awareness of the miracle. Thus both explanations can be true. Publicly, even if all understand, they do not fulfill with a Megillah among the Writings. An individual reading for himself knows what he is reading and fulfills even if he does not understand the language. But to discharge others with fewer than ten: if they are not expert and do not understand, there is no publicizing; if they are expert and understand, then even with a separately written Megillah one person does not discharge an expert fellow with fewer than ten. Therefore the Rambam, Tur, and Shulchan Aruch did not mention this case, because either way it does not apply. This answer sits better, and proves that one person does not discharge another with fewer than ten except where the other is not expert. Examine this carefully.
