**** ENGLISH ****
<b>(There, seif 5)</b> If rain fell, he enters the house, etc. Note: I was asked about a time when rain is falling, but in one sukkah no rain falls into it: should one recite the blessing "to sit in the sukkah" or not? I answered that one should not recite the blessing. The proof is from what the decisors wrote, that even if there is no congealed dish before him he is also exempt. Since the sages gave the measure as "from when the congealed dish would spoil," this implies that this is the reason for the exemption; nevertheless, even if no congealed dish is before him he is exempt. This is because if such a dish were before him he would be exempt, and because another person has such a dish and is exempt, he too is exempt. So too here. At first glance one might bring proof to the opposite from what the Magen Avraham wrote at the end of siman 629, that if rain drips from the roofing one should tie up a sheet, but should not recite the blessing because of the rule that doubtful blessings are treated leniently; this implies that were it not for that doubt, he would bless because no rain is falling into his sukkah. But this is no proof, for one can say that he speaks where the rain has already stopped and rain is no longer falling from heaven. If so, the time is one of sukkah obligation, except that he could not eat because the roofing drips and one who is distressed is exempt; if he made a device so that it will not drip, he is obligated to bless. Therefore the Magen Avraham exempted him only for another reason. But if rain is falling from heaven in a manner that is an exempt time, then even one into whose sukkah the rain does not fall is exempt from blessing, for it is an exempt time. On the contrary, from the above sugya our words are proven: otherwise, according to those in siman 629 who hold that it is permitted to spread a sheet under the sukkah roofing, why is it explained in the Talmud and decisors that when rain falls one may leave? Why should he leave? Let him spread a sheet over himself so the rain will not fall. It is forced to say that they speak where he has no sheet, for the Talmud and all the decisors would not have failed to give this counsel. Rather, perforce, this does not help, and there is no fulfillment of the mitzvah of sukkah in this. Yet they hold that the sheet does not invalidate the roofing. Perforce, because it is a time when rain is falling, it does not help that the rain does not reach him. One can explain the reason: the Levush and the Bach hold that if rain cannot fall into it, it is invalid. If so, even those who validate it appear to do so because whatever is fit for mixing is not held back by the absence of actual mixing. Since it is possible to remove the roofing and have the rain fall into it, and nevertheless its shade would remain greater than its sun, it is fit for mixing, and the lack of mixing does not prevent it. But if rain is actually falling from heaven, and in this sukkah the rain cannot fall, either way he does not fulfill sukkah: if we follow its present state, it is invalid according to the Levush and Bach; and if we follow those who validate because it is fit for mixing, then when the extra covering is removed rain will fall and he is exempt because of the rain. Therefore either way one should not recite "to sit in the sukkah." Especially since, even without this, according to the above view one does not bless when rain cannot fall into it; therefore in such a case one certainly should not bless. Examine carefully, for this is clear and correct in my opinion. See my work on Orach Chayim from the year 607, in the laws of sukkah, siman 629, what I wrote in a responsum to the holy community of Zravna, and examine well.<br><b>(There, seif 5 in the gloss)</b> All this applies specifically on the other days or nights of Sukkot, but on the first night one must eat in the sukkah even if rain is falling, etc. Note: From the words of the Taz it is clear that the custom is to recite "to sit in the sukkah" even when rain fell, for we rule like the Semag, etc. They showed me later authorities in the name of the Peri Megadim, that one should not bless because it is a doubtful blessing. In my humble opinion this does not appear correct. Besides the fact that the custom is to bless on the first night, the language of the Taz indicates that this is the practice, and the language of the Rema also indicates it, for he wrote simply that one is obligated to eat, implying according to its law, with a blessing; otherwise he should have explained. Beyond this, it appears that this is proper and correct, and there is no doubtful dispute here at all. Even those who answer Tosafot's question differently, and not because of rain, nevertheless appear to agree to the law in this case. For one must understand: from where does the Mishnah know that when rain falls or one is distressed he is exempt from sukkah? One cannot say it is from "you shall dwell" in the manner that you live, because in a dwelling one also leaves in such circumstances. If so, the Mishnah should have made the measure depend on this: if such a thing happened in his house, would he leave? Why did it measure from when the congealed dish spoils, and why did it make it depend on that? Even if it is because of "you shall dwell" in the manner that you live, one may say that the reason of the verse is also this: since eating on the other days is optional, if he wished he would not eat and would not be obligated in sukkah; the Torah decreed only that if he wants to eat or sleep, then he must do so in the sukkah. This is understandable when the eating or sleeping is pleasurable for him, for then the eating is considered benefit, and where he wishes to benefit we require sukkah. But when he is distressed, or when rain fell to the measure that the congealed dish would spoil, this eating or sleeping is not pleasure for him but distress; wherever it is distress, it is not considered eating or sleeping, and it is as though he did not eat or sleep, so he is automatically exempt from sukkah. This works for the other days, which are optional. But on the first night, when eating is obligatory, the matter does not depend on his enjoyment at all; even if he does not enjoy it, he is obligated in sukkah, just as one who has no appetite for eating is still obligated to eat in the sukkah. Therefore what does it matter that he is distressed by this eating? In the end he is obligated in sukkah. One can also say that since it is an obligation upon him to eat, the mitzvah itself is a benefit to him, because he fulfills the mitzvah; therefore he is obligated in sukkah. Although mitzvot were not given for benefit, where bodily benefit accompanies the benefit of a mitzvah, we say mitzvot were given for benefit; in any event, his distress is offset by the pleasure of fulfilling the mitzvah, and he is not considered distressed. Why did they not answer Tosafot's question this way? On the contrary, their difficulty was that because of rain the verbal analogy should not have been needed, since even without it he is obligated because of Yom Tov, and automatically requires a sukkah. Since this eating is considered distress and also does not depend on his enjoyment, but is an obligation upon him, he is automatically obligated in sukkah, and we do not need the verbal analogy for this. But regarding the law, they agree that even if rain fell he is obligated in sukkah, and there is no doubtful blessing. Another reason appears: although the Rema wrote in the name of Hagahot Maimoni that one who is exempt and does it is called a commoner, this appears to apply only on the other days. We see that there are several opinions, and so is the practice, that women recite blessings over positive time-bound commandments. Why did they not write to prevent them because they are commoners, and that everyone agrees they may perform them without a blessing and we do not protest? Why do we not say this is an act of commoners? Perforce, with other positive time-bound commandments, since they are at least obligatory for men, even one who is not obligated is not called a commoner if he performs them. But with sukkah, even for every person without rain it is not an obligation but optional, for if he wants he does not eat; and since it is only optional, when rain falls too, if he does so he is a commoner. If so, on the first night, where in a place without rain there is an obligation to eat in the sukkah, then even where rain is falling it is no worse than women; he can perform it as one who is not commanded, and in any event is not called a commoner. If so, he can also bless, just as women bless over positive time-bound commandments, and there is no blessing in vain. Thus there is again a double doubt to be lenient: perhaps the law follows those who hold that the verbal analogy applies to the obligation of sukkah when rain fell; and even if the law follows the other decisors, perhaps the halachah follows those who hold that women may bless over positive time-bound commandments, and the same applies here. Therefore the primary law is like the custom of Israel and like the view of the Turei Zahav, to bless over the sukkah on the first night in this case. So we acted in practice. May our words be accepted before the Master of all, amen. Examine carefully.

<b>(There, seif 7 in the gloss)</b> Anyone who is exempt from the sukkah and does not leave from there receives no reward and is only a commoner. Note: See Magen Avraham, siman 472 in the laws of Pesach, what he wrote in the name of the Taz, and that one must consider the matter, etc. In truth, it is explicit in the Mishnah regarding R. Tzadok, "bring them up to the sukkah," because he was stringent upon himself; if so, it is proven that one may be stringent upon himself. At first glance one might distinguish: where the person is in any case one who is subject to the obligation, such as R. Tzadok, who was obligated in sukkah and the exemption was only from another side, namely that this food is exempt from sukkah, since in any case the person is subject to the obligation he may be stringent upon himself. But in the Rema's case, when rain fell, he himself is exempt; therefore it is not proper to be stringent and he is called a commoner. However, one who examines the Yerushalmi in chapter 2 of Sukkah, in the Mishnah that agents of a mitzvah are exempt from sukkah, where it says that Rav Huna went to Ein Tav, etc., until he entered the sukkah of R. Yochanan the scribe, etc., should see there at length. There he was an agent of a mitzvah, and there was also water; so he was exempt and the water was exempt, and nevertheless he was stringent upon himself. It is also difficult: how do women recite blessings over positive time-bound commandments? If it is an act of commoners, how can they bless, and would this not be a blessing in vain? Perforce, one may be stringent. Perforce one must say that only when rain falls, since it is like a cup poured in his face and they have shown him from heaven that they do not want his service, he should not be stringent if they do not want him; but generally it is permitted and proper to be stringent. This is clear in my opinion. I also see proof for the words of the Magen Avraham in siman 472 mentioned above, that one should not be stringent in a matter that is permitted, from what is stated in the Yerushalmi, Sukkah 2:10: Rabban Gamliel entered and left all night. R. Eliezer, one student from R. Mana, ruled for one of the women's relatives: he left; we do not burden him to return. Did he not hear that Rabban Gamliel entered and left, that R. Eliezer entered and left all night? End quote. The Korban HaEdah explained there that the student who ruled that we do not burden him did not know of the incident of Rabban Gamliel, etc. At first glance this is difficult: from where do we know he did not know? Perhaps he knew, but Rabban Gamliel and R. Eliezer were stringent upon themselves. We find similarly in the Mishnah there above, in the incident where they brought Rabban Gamliel a bucket of water and two dates and he said, "bring them up to the sukkah"; the Ran wrote that this teaches that if one wishes to be stringent upon himself he may be stringent and there is no arrogance; see there. Thus Rabban Gamliel was stringent upon himself. If so, perhaps here too he was stringent upon himself. Perforce, if one is exempt he may not be stringent. The case of bringing a bucket of water up to the sukkah is when the person is obligated and subject to sukkah, and the impediment is only from the item, that this item has no sukkah obligation; in such a case he may be stringent, for it is as though he says: for me this is considered like bread and like something obligated in sukkah, and for stringency his considering it significant is effective, and he is not called a commoner. But when rain falls, he himself is exempt from sukkah; and likewise there with reclining, he himself is totally exempt. In that case, considering it significant is irrelevant, and therefore he is called a commoner. This is well proven from Rabban Gamliel and R. Eliezer entering and leaving all night. This is a correct principle in my humble opinion; examine it. The Yerushalmi did not bring the baraita cited in our Talmud, which states explicitly that if he was sleeping, we do not burden him to go up. It appears that from this there is proof for the Magen Avraham's view, that specifically if he had already lain down, but otherwise he is obligated to go up. Therefore it is well: the baraita speaks where he had already lain down and slept, and therefore we do not burden him; but the incident of Rabban Gamliel and R. Eliezer was that they had not yet lain down, but only went out, and therefore they entered and left all night. That student, however, ruled even before he had lain down that we do not burden him; therefore it said: he had not heard that Rabban Gamliel entered and left all night. If so, this law of the Magen Avraham is explicit in the Yerushalmi; examine it. Now we learned in tractate Yoma, chapter 8: the large date they spoke of is more than an egg's bulk, etc.; see there to the end. From this sugya one may learn what I was uncertain about: since for sukkah we require a measure, and with less than the measure one does not recite "to sit in the sukkah," if he intends to eat the measure but does so with a delay longer than the time of eating a peras, and all the more so if he delayed between one eating and the next the time of eating a peras, is he obligated to recite "to sit in the sukkah"? All the more so if he changed his mind: at first he intended to eat less than the measure and did not recite "to sit in the sukkah," and afterward changed his mind to eat more up to the measure. If it is within the time of eating a peras, he certainly must recite "to sit in the sukkah"; but if he did not delay the time of eating a peras, is he obligated or not? At first glance it would appear simple that whenever he delayed the time of eating a peras, it is not considered one eating, and certainly he does not bless. But from the Talmud the opposite appears proven. It is difficult: what does the Talmud ask according to the one who says a date is more than an egg's bulk, from what is stated regarding Rabban Gamliel, that it was not because the law was so but because he wanted to be stringent upon himself? What is the question? Say that the intent is this: in Pesachim, chapter 3, we say that our women bake kav by kav for Pesach, and Rav Yosef said to him: What is your intent, stringency? It is a stringency that leads to leniency, for it exempts it from challah, etc. If so, it implies that one may not make devices to exempt oneself from a mitzvah. In Chullin, chapter Kisui HaDam, we say the opposite: what should he do? He should slaughter by tearing or uprooting in order to exempt himself from covering, which proves that one may make devices to exempt oneself from a mitzvah. So too in Yoreh De'ah siman 25; I wrote elsewhere to resolve this. In any event, it is proven that there is a concept that one should not make devices to exempt himself from a mitzvah; so too in Menachot, chapter 3, regarding a linen garment in summer: what will become of tzitzit? Sometimes the opposite is true, that one may use a stratagem to exempt himself. If so, perhaps the Mishnah's intent is this: Rabban Gamliel certainly could have delayed between one eating and the next the time of eating a peras and thereby not need a sukkah. This is what it means, "not because the law was so": not that it is forbidden to use a device to remove oneself from a mitzvah, for by law he could use a device to exempt himself, but he wanted to be stringent upon himself. What then is the Talmud's question? There is an even greater difficulty: who says that what does not combine outside the time of eating a peras applies to a prohibited matter and the like, where there is no blessing to combine it and therefore it combines only within the time of eating a peras; but for a matter over which one recites a blessing, since he eats everything on the basis of the first blessing, perhaps the blessing combines it to be the manner of eating. With this it fits that in siman 475 the Mechaber wrote that one should not delay between one eating and another the time of eating a peras, while in the laws of Yom Kippur he wrote that from the beginning of eating until the end of eating should not be the time of eating a peras. Since on Yom Kippur there is no blessing, for it is prohibited eating, it must not be from the beginning of eating until the end the time of eating a peras. But with matzah, where there is a blessing, the concern is only that he not delay between one eating and the next the time of eating a peras. Examine this. Yet one may say that although the blessing combines it, blessings are rabbinic, and from the Torah there is no combining; therefore it is forbidden to interrupt in it. This is true with matzah, for otherwise from the Torah he has not fulfilled. But with sukkah it is the opposite: although from the Torah it is not one eating, rabbinically it is one eating, and therefore he is obligated in sukkah rabbinically. According to this, one could still say that Rabban Gamliel could have eaten less than the measure each time, blessed each time anew, and changed his mind only once, and if he delayed the time of eating a peras he would be exempt from sukkah. But if so, he would cause an unnecessary blessing, which is forbidden to cause, as the Magen Avraham wrote in siman 215; therefore he was forced to eat together. Yet one may say: if he goes to the sukkah he will also become obligated to bless over the sukkah, which is also an unnecessary blessing, since he could eat less than the measure and be exempt without it. If so, either way he causes an unnecessary blessing; he could have eaten less than the measure. This is what it means that they brought them up to the sukkah and did not eat less than the measure each time: not because the law was so, because causing an unnecessary blessing is forbidden, for this too would be an unnecessary blessing if they brought them up to the sukkah when it was possible to be exempt without it; rather, he wanted to be stringent upon himself. What then is the Talmud's question? Perforce, it is proven that sukkah does not depend on delay; rather, whenever he eats the measure, even with a great interruption, he is obligated in sukkah. Examine carefully.<br><b>(There, in the gloss)</b> And when he leaves the sukkah because of rain, he should not kick and leave, but should leave submissively, like a servant who pours a cup for his master and his master pours it in his face. Note: In the Talmud this was a question: who poured for whom? It resolves there: like a servant who poured a cup for his master, and his master poured a pitcher in his face. At first glance it would appear that there is a practical legal difference from this question, whether he may be stringent upon himself or not. If we say it is like the servant poured the cup in his master's face, then although by law he is exempt because he is distressed, nevertheless if he wants to be stringent upon himself and reflect in repentance, he may sit in the sukkah. But according to the wording that the master poured a pitcher in the servant's face, since they show him that they do not want his service, it is not proper to serve against the master's will, and it is forbidden to be stringent upon oneself. However, according to those who hold that one who is exempt and nevertheless performs is called a commoner, one may say there is another practical difference: if rain fell on the first night. According to the view that it is as though the servant poured in his master's face, then on the first night, when he is specifically obligated in sukkah, even in rain he must eat in the sukkah, for since one who is distressed is obligated then, he is obligated even in rain. But according to the wording that the master poured in his face, then they are showing him that they do not want his service, and it is not proper to serve against the master's will; even on the first night he is exempt from sukkah. And the practical difference we say, that on the first night he is obligated in sukkah, will be explained according to the other commentators, that on other Yom Tov days there is no obligation to eat bread, or that the practical difference is that an olive's bulk suffices. Those who hold that the practical difference is when rain fell will hold that one may be stringent upon himself in other mitzvot, and we do not say that one who is exempt and performs is called a commoner. Therefore the practical difference from this is that here he certainly may not be stringent, since it is as though his master poured a pitcher in his face. There are practical differences for both sides. So it appeared at first glance to say, but it still requires examination. Perhaps Hashem will grant me merit at another time to explain this further; examine carefully.
