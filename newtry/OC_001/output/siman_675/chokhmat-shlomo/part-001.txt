<b>Section 1.</b> Kindling performs the mitzvah and not placement, etc.; therefore, a lantern that was burning all day, etc. Note: Rambam's language on this is: a lantern that was burning the entire day, on Motzaei Shabbos one extinguishes it, blesses, and kindles it, for kindling is the mitzvah and not placement. Until here. I saw that Pri Chadash asked against him, because his language implies that if placement performed the mitzvah, one would not need extinguishing, and this is against the Talmud, which asks there: if you think placement performs the mitzvah, he should have said he extinguishes it, lifts it, kindles it, and places it. If so, it implies that even according to the one who says placement performs the mitzvah, extinguishing is required. The two answers Pri Chadash gave do not seem correct. The second answer, that Rambam is speaking where he kindled it for the sake of Chanukah, based on Tosafos there, certainly does not seem correct. If so, he should have explained this, especially since the Talmud asks, he should have said he extinguishes and lifts, which implies that since it says merely a lantern that was burning, it is not speaking of a Chanukah lamp. If the plain meaning is a Shabbos lamp, then Rambam, if he is speaking of Chanukah, should have explained this. Also, why did he leave the law explicit in the Talmud, which speaks of a Shabbos lamp, and take another law that is not explicit in the Talmud? The first answer of Pri Chadash, that Rambam's intent is only as an inference, that we do not require placement, but if placement performed the mitzvah we would also require placement, also does not seem correct. Something like this Rambam should have explained, not relied on an inference. Rather, it seems that it is well understood. Tosafos themselves asked there: if placement performs the mitzvah, why is extinguishing necessary? Is it worse than if a deaf-mute, mentally incompetent person, or minor kindled it? In my humble opinion it is well understood. This is comparable to slaughter, where we hold that intent for slaughter is not required, and even if one dropped a knife without intent, it is valid. However, if it fell by itself, it is invalid, for an act is at least required. If a deaf-mute, mentally incompetent person, or minor slaughtered, it is valid, proving that their act is considered an act, only they lack intent. They have an act, and it is better than where the knife fell by itself. If so, one may say the same regarding Chanukah. Even according to the initial assumption that placement performs the mitzvah, that means only that we do not require kindling for the sake of the mitzvah, and automatically kindling by a deaf-mute, mentally incompetent person, or minor is effective. Although they lack intent, and even according to the one who holds that mitzvos require intent, he agrees that for rabbinic mitzvos intent is not required. This means only if the person is capable of intent, for since it is fit for mixing, lack of mixing does not prevent fulfillment. But since a deaf-mute, mentally incompetent person, and minor lack intent, their kindling would not be effective if kindling performs the mitzvah. Also, since they are not obligated, they cannot discharge others. Nevertheless, if placement performs the mitzvah and not kindling, then kindling by a deaf-mute, mentally incompetent person, or minor would be effective. However, if it kindled by itself, it certainly would not be effective, for at least a human act is required in the kindling, and spontaneous kindling is not effective. Therefore the same applies if it burned from erev Shabbos, since the first act has already gone away, similar to what we say in Berachos regarding fire kindled by a non-Jew, that the first column of flame has gone away, and likewise if one turned water with a wheel and slaughtered, that the first force has gone away and afterward it is automatic. Here too, the first force has gone away, and afterward it is like it burned by itself. Therefore it is worse than kindling by a deaf-mute, mentally incompetent person, or minor, for at least an act is required, and this is automatic. The question of Tosafos is resolved; examine it. According to this it is well understood. There is a dispute in the Talmud whether one's fire is because of his arrows or because of his property, and we hold it is because of his arrows. Nimukei Yosef in chapter 2 of Bava Kama asked: if one's fire is because of his arrows, it should be forbidden to light a lamp on Shabbos even if he lights it on erev Shabbos. He answered that it is as though he did everything at a permitted time; see there. If so, at any rate it is his act, and it is all considered his act. Therefore it is a human act, and it need not be extinguished, for it is no worse than kindling by a deaf-mute, mentally incompetent person, or minor. Thus the Gemara, which asks that he should have said he extinguishes and lifts, means that otherwise it could not have asked why extinguishing is necessary, for one could say that Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi holds like the view that one's fire is because of his property, and if so there is no human act and it is like being lit automatically, worse than kindling by a deaf-mute, mentally incompetent person, or minor. Therefore it asks: even if so, at any rate lifting is also required. But according to us, who hold that one's fire is because of his arrows, all kindling is like his act, and if so it is no worse than kindling by a deaf-mute, mentally incompetent person, or minor. Therefore Rambam wrote that only because kindling performs the mitzvah must he extinguish it. This is well understood; examine carefully. This appears correct in resolving Rambam's words with the help of Heaven.<br><b>There in the same section.</b> That if he kindled it inside and took it outside, he did not fulfill, etc. Note: see my work on Orach Chayim from the year 5596, where I wrote to resolve the words of the Tur. See Bach and Turei Zahav, who wondered why this reason is needed, since we hold that kindling performs the mitzvah. See what I wrote in my work on Orach Chayim from the year 5609, siman 673, to resolve this. It further seems that a practical difference emerges from this regarding a courtyard with two entrances, where one must kindle at both. Ran wrote that since this is only because of suspicion, one need not bless. In my humble opinion this does not seem correct, for the baraisa taught: if he kindled it inside and took it outside, he has done nothing. Since it says he has done nothing, this proves that he must bless. The Talmud concludes that the reason is because one who sees says that he kindled it for his own need. How then does it say he has done nothing? It proves that because of suspicion one must bless. According to this, it is well understood why the Tur and Shulchan Aruch stated this reason: to prove from it that if one must bless because of suspicion, since it says he has done nothing, the same applies with two entrances, that one must bless even though it is because of suspicion. However, perhaps one can distinguish: in this suspicion, that he kindled it for his own need, he has made no publicizing of the miracle at all, and therefore he must bless. But with two entrances, although they suspect him of not kindling the Chanukah lamp, nevertheless in truth he performed publicizing of the miracle, and therefore one may say he need not bless. So one may say. However, the Tur may hold that they are equal; examine it. I was uncertain in a case where the whole world used only wax and tallow candles, and their custom was not to use olive oil, and now one person kindled with olive oil; or if he was among the mehadrin and held many lamps in his hand corresponding to the number of days. Do we say that in this case the idea that one who sees says he took it for his own need does not apply, because this kind of lamp is not normally used for his own need, and people do not normally use many lamps, since one would suffice, so perforce it will be clear that it is for the Chanukah lamp? Or even in such a case do we decree because the Rabbis did not distinguish in their enactment? This requires examination as practical law; examine it.

<b>There, section 3.</b> And there is one who says that a minor who has reached the age of education is permitted, etc. Note: see Magen Avraham below, siman 689, and in the laws of Megillah, where he asked that the words of Shulchan Aruch contradict one another. There he ruled plainly that a minor never discharges an adult in any way, and did not bring the view of the Itur; here he brought the view of the Itur, who holds that a minor who has reached education discharges an adult. He wrote that it is forced to distinguish between Chanukah lamp and Megillah, because in Ran it implies that they are equal; see there. It seems in my humble opinion that this can be properly resolved. The reason that one person discharges another is disputed: some hold it is because of mutual responsibility, and some hold it is because of agency. In our novellae to the laws of Megillah, siman 589, chapter 4, we asked that the words of Tosafos contradict one another. In Berachos they wrote that with Birkat Hamazon a minor cannot discharge an adult because two rabbinic levels do not come and discharge one rabbinic level. We wrote that this is understandable according to mutual responsibility: since they are not alike and their obligations are not equal, this one cannot discharge that one. But according to agency, it is as though he himself blesses; why should two rabbinic levels not be effective to discharge one rabbinic level? Tosafos in Gittin hold that in a matter to which the minor himself is connected, a minor can have agency. If so, since the minor is at least obligated in Birkat Hamazon, he is connected to it, and is considered the agent of the adult. Why then should the minor not discharge him? See there for what I wrote about this. But now I saw that it is well understood according to the words of Maharit in a responsum, who holds that an agent is not effective for an oath because words are not handed over to an agent; see there. In truth, elsewhere I wondered about him, for one may say that words are not handed to an agent means that the agent cannot make another agent, but he himself can be an agent even for words. Nevertheless, Maharit at least holds that words are not handed to an agent and agency is not relevant there. If so, it is well understood. There, with Birkat Hamazon, which is only words, agency is not relevant, only mutual responsibility; therefore two rabbinic levels do not discharge one rabbinic level. But where there is an act, such that one can appoint an agent, one may properly say that two rabbinic levels discharge one rabbinic level because of agency. If so, this is fine regarding Chanukah, which is an act and agency applies. But Megillah is words, and agency does not apply; therefore two rabbinic levels do not discharge one rabbinic level. It is well understood. The view of Ran and Kol Bo is that an agent can nullify another person's chametz. But Ran brings there other views that nullification through an agent is not effective; they disagree about the aforementioned reasoning of Maharit, whether agency applies to words. According to this, Ran follows his own view: since he holds that agency applies to words, Megillah is indeed equal to Chanukah. But for us as practical law, although in the laws of Pesach the ruling follows those who say an agent can nullify, nevertheless regarding Megillah he properly ruled that no minor discharges, because there is a double doubt for stringency. Perhaps the halachah follows those who hold that even regarding Chanukah, which is an act, two rabbinic levels do not discharge one rabbinic level. And if you say the halachah follows Baal HaItur, who holds that in Chanukah two rabbinic levels discharge one rabbinic level, one may say that his main reason is agency; if so, perhaps the halachah follows those who hold that words cannot be made subject to agency, and therefore in Megillah the only reason is mutual responsibility, and with that certainly two rabbinic levels do not discharge one rabbinic level. Therefore, since there is a double doubt for stringency, he did not take the view of Baal HaItur into account at all. But regarding Chanukah, which is an act and agency is relevant, he properly brought the view of Baal HaItur, for here one can say that since the reason is agency, two rabbinic levels can properly discharge one rabbinic level. This reasoning seems correct in my humble opinion, and it is well understood; examine carefully.
