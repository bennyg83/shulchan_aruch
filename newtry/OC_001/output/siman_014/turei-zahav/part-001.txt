<b>שאלה כשהיא מצוייצת כו'. </b> זהו דעת הרא"ש דלא אמרו טלית שאולה פטור מציצי' היינו שא"צ להטיל בה ציצית אבל השואל טלית מצוייצת אדעתא דהכי השאילה לו שיברך עלי' ואם א"א לו לברך אא"כ תהי' שלו אמרי' דהוה כנתנו לו במתנה ע"מ להחזיר מידי דהוה אשואל חלוק מחבירו והודיעו שהלך לבקר חולה ומת לו מת רשאי לקרוע הבגד עכ"ל. וזהו ג"כ טעם ס"ד דמותר לברך על לוקח טלית מקופלת שמצא אמרינן מסתמא ניחא ליה והוה ליה כאלו הקנה לו ע"מ להחזיר וכתב עוד שאותן המשכימין להתפלל בסליחות ומתעטפים בטלית שאולה כדי להפטרמברכה טועים הם דחייב בברכ' כמ"ש: <b>ונ"ל </b> דהא דמחייב הרא"ש בברכת הטלית שאולה מצוייצת היינו בטלית המיוחד למצות ציצית כי הנהו טליתות של בה"כ או מה שקורין ד' כנפות דבזה ודאי לא מקנה לו אלא לעשות המצוה ולברך עליה כדאמרי' אבל במלבוש של ד' כנפות שאדם לובש בו דרך מלבוש אלא שמטיל בו ציצית משו' שיש לו ד' כנפות השואל לחבירו מלבוש כזה אין השואל מברך עליו דאין לנו בירור שהשאיל' משום מצוה דלמא למלבוש בעלמא השאיל' לו וזה דומה לטלית של אחרים שאין חייב השואל לעשות בה ציצית אע"ג דהשאיל' לו מ"מ אמרי' דרצונו היה להשאילו למלבוש לחוד כדאמרן כנ"ל וכ' רש"ל פ' כ"ה סי' נ"ג על דברי הרא"ש האלו דשאני גבי שואל מחבירו כו' לענין קריעה שא"א לו לילך ערום וע"כ המשאיל הקנהו לו וכן גבי אתרוג דבעי לברך דא"א זולתו אבל לא בטלית שאינו מחויב ללבוש אותו משום ציצית. והאריך שם ומסיק דמ"מ ראוי לכל י"ש שלא ילבש טלית שאינו שלו ואם אין לו טלית אחר ילבשנו בלא ברכה כי הברכות אין מעכבות. עוד אומר אני אפילו לדעת המברכים על טלית שאולה היינו הטלית הלובש בעת התפלה עם הנחת תפילין שהוא לזכרון כמו תפילין כו' אבל מי שלובש כדי לעלות לתורה או לירד לפני התיבה בודאי יראה שאין לברך עליה כלום עכ"ל ובעיני ג"כ תמוה כראי' של הרא"ש מחלוק שקורע דשם קורע כדין דשאל לו לענין זה וכל אדם יכול לעשות במה ששאל לאותו ענין ששאל לו משא"כ בציצית שאף א"ת ששאלו לברך עליו מ"מ אין יכול לברך על טלית שאולה אא"כ במתנה דוק' ומזה אין לו ראי' מחלוק דנימא דנתכוין לשום מתנה ועמ"ש בסעיף שאח"ז:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>A borrowed garment when it is already fringed, etc.</b> This is Rosh's view. The rule that a borrowed tallis is exempt means that the borrower need not attach tzitzis. If he borrows a tallis that already has tzitzis, the lender lent it so that he could recite the blessing. Since he cannot recite the blessing unless it belongs to him, we regard the lender as having given it as a gift on condition that it be returned. This resembles one who borrows a garment, tells the lender that he is going to visit a sick person, and then the sick person dies. The borrower may tear the garment for mourning.

This is also the reason for seif 4, which permits reciting a blessing over a folded tallis found without its owner. We presume the owner consents and treat it as though transferred as a gift on condition that it be returned. Rosh further writes that those who rise early for Selichos and wrap themselves in borrowed talleisim intending thereby to avoid a blessing are mistaken, for they are obligated to recite the blessing.

It appears to me that Rosh's obligation to recite the blessing applies specifically to a tallis designated for the mitzvah of tzitzis, such as synagogue talleisim or a garment called arba kanfos. In such a case, the lender certainly gave it only so that the borrower could perform the mitzvah and recite the blessing. An ordinary four-cornered garment worn as clothing, to which tzitzis were attached merely because it has four corners, is different. A borrower does not recite a blessing, because there is no proof that it was lent for the mitzvah; perhaps it was lent merely as clothing. This resembles an ordinary borrowed garment, which the borrower is not obligated to fringe.

Maharshal writes in Yam Shel Shlomo, chapter Kol HaBasar, siman 53, that Rosh's proof from the borrowed garment torn for mourning is not comparable. There, the borrower cannot go naked, and therefore the lender necessarily transfers sufficient ownership. The same applies to an esrog, because the mitzvah cannot be fulfilled otherwise. A tallis, however, need not be worn at all. Maharshal concludes that every God-fearing person should avoid wearing a tallis that is not his. If no other tallis is available, he should wear it without a blessing, since blessings do not prevent fulfillment. He adds that even according to those who recite a blessing over a borrowed tallis, this refers to the tallis worn during prayer together with tefillin, which serves as a reminder like tefillin. One who wears it only to receive an aliyah or lead the prayers should certainly not recite a blessing.

Rosh's proof from the torn garment is also difficult in my eyes. There the borrower tears it lawfully because he borrowed it for that purpose, and every person may use a borrowed item for the purpose for which it was borrowed. Tzitzis is different. Even if it was borrowed for the purpose of reciting the blessing, one cannot recite a blessing over a borrowed tallis unless it was actually given as a gift. The torn garment provides no proof that the lender intended a gift. See what I write in the following seif.

וכ"ש אם נוטל אותו שלא מדעת חבירו כנ"ל. ואע"פ שכתב הרא"ש שראה נוהגין לברך מ"מ הרי כתב הרא"ש עצמו הבאתיו בסעיף י"ג שראה בימי הסליחות שאין מברכין על טלית שאולה. אלא שכתב שהם טועים ולפי מ"ש יפה הם עושין ע"כ אין ראיה ממנהג כיון שהם מוחזקים:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>It is permitted to take, etc.</b> Meaning, without the owner's knowledge. For the same reason Rama rules below in siman 649 that one may take another person's lulav without permission after the first day. Terumas HaDeshen, siman 100, compares that case to this one, because no damage is expected and a person is pleased for a mitzvah to be performed with his property.

It is difficult why Terumas HaDeshen and Rama permit it only after the first day, but not on the first day. Tzitzis is also D'Oraisa, yet we rely upon the owner's presumed consent and recite a blessing without his knowledge. One may answer that the Torah does not prohibit wearing a garment without tzitzis. It merely obligates a person to attach tzitzis to his own garment, as explained above in siman 13 in the name of Mordechai and Ri. Here that obligation does not apply because the garment is not his.

Yet siman 8, seif 9, is more stringent regarding the blessing over tzitzis than regarding wearing, requiring inspection specifically because of the blessing. If the presumption that a person is pleased for a mitzvah to be performed with his property is strong enough for the blessing over tzitzis, it should all the more suffice for the first-day lulav obligation. We should presume that the owner certainly gives it as a gift on condition that it be returned.

One may distinguish that on the first day, when the obligation is D'Oraisa, the owner is more particular that the esrog not be damaged. Even a slight deficiency or puncture can invalidate it, so presumed consent is ineffective. Still, why is an explicitly borrowed lulav invalid on the first day? Since the lender knows that the borrower can fulfill the obligation only if it is given as a gift on condition that it be returned, we should presume that was his intent. A gift on condition that it be returned is effective for an esrog, as Kiddushin 6b teaches.

One must apparently say that since he used the language of lending, we do not reinterpret it as a gift. But why is this different from Rosh's ruling above that one who borrows a fringed tallis must recite a blessing because we presume the lender intended a gift on condition that it be returned? The same difficulty arises from Rosh in the first chapter of Kiddushin concerning a borrowed ring. If one lends a ring to his friend knowing that he wishes to betroth a woman with it, the betrothal is valid. We testify that he intended to transfer it with whatever legally effective language was necessary, at least as a gift on condition that it be returned, since ordinary people do not know the technical law. This is a full betrothal, as ruled in Even HaEzer 28. All the more so should the same reasoning validate the first-day lulav obligation.

It appears that kiddushin differs from lulav. A borrowed or stolen lulav is invalid because of the Scriptural decree, “for yourselves,” meaning your own. The Torah therefore invalidated every case expressed as a loan, and we may not reinterpret the language as a gift. In kiddushin, by contrast, borrowed or stolen property is invalid by logic, because the man must give the woman money that belongs to him. Since no verse excludes the case, we may assess the lender's intent and say that he meant to use any legally effective formulation. See what I wrote in siman 368 concerning Rosh's words.

This, however, renews the difficulty regarding tzitzis, where the verse “your garment” excludes a borrowed garment. Sukkah 27b likewise teaches that without the verse “every citizen,” a borrowed sukkah would have been invalid like a stolen one because the Torah says, “You shall make for yourself.” Thus, tzitzis appears precisely comparable to a borrowed lulav. This requires extensive study.

Although I am unworthy even to join Maharshal in disputing Rosh and those who follow him, this difficulty compels us to be concerned for a doubtful blessing and not recite a blessing over a borrowed fringed tallis, in accordance with Maharshal. This is all the more true when one takes it without the owner's knowledge. Rosh writes that he observed people during Selichos not reciting a blessing over borrowed talleisim and called them mistaken. According to what I have written, they acted properly. Their established practice therefore cannot serve as proof for the opposite ruling.

<b>It is forbidden to study, etc.</b> In Choshen Mishpat, siman 72, I explained, with Hashem's help, the permission for a Torah scholar to study from sefarim deposited with him, in accordance with Rama there in siman 292. The same applies when the sefarim were given as collateral to a Torah scholar, for the rationale is identical. I explained the reason there.
