<b>הנה</b> בגיטין נ״ט ב׳ יליף ר״ח ב״א מוקדשתו (ויקרא נ״א ח׳) וכ״ה בפוסקים ועמ״א ר״א אות ד׳ האריך דד״ת הוא והר״מ ז״ל במנין המצות ל״ב לחלוק כבוד לזרעו של אהרן ולהקדימו לכ״ד שבקדושה שנאמר וקדשתו ורש״י בחומש נקיט קדוש יהיה לך דקדשהו בע״כ עיי״ל רא״ם ופרישה כאן דנקיט רישיה ומקדוש נפקא ורב מתנה יליף ויתנה לכהנים בני לוי ואל כל זקני ישראל (דברים ל״ט ט׳) ור״י כו׳. והנה הנ״מ בין הנך לימודים י״ל בד׳ אנפי או דכהנים בני לוי רק לקריאת התורה הא לשאר דברים לא וקדשתו לכ״ד שבקדושה (וקדושה מנה יפה מעשר עני וצדקה עתוס׳ שם ד״ה וליטול ומ״ש בסעודה א״י מהו קדושה ואפשר סעודת מצוה חבירים כו׳) ב׳ דמקרא ויתנה י״ל כהן קודם ללוי ולוי לישראל דהכי כתיב קרא ומיהו בירושלמי עלה דהך יליף רשב״י מכהנים בני לוי ואח״כ זקני ישראל ולא יליף מיתורא דבני לוי א״כ אין עדיפות לוי לישראל וע׳ מפרש שם בתוס׳ (ד״ב) השלישי י״ל כהן בעל מום מקרא דכהנים בני לוי הנך נמי כ״ה כמו אמור אל הכהנים אפי׳ בעלי מומין במשמע משא״כ לחם אלקיך הוא מקריב י״ל דבעל מום ליתא לעשה וי״ל מקריב אכילה נמי בכלל (ער״מ פ״ד מה׳ כלי מקדש הא״ב ובמנין המצות ל״ב מצות עשה לחלוק כבוד לזרעו של אהרן) הרביעי הוא אם נאמר הכהנים בני לוי ואח״כ אל זקני ישראל שמעינן דכהן קודם אפי׳ ע״ה לזקן ת״ח מסנהדרין דסתמא כתיב משא״כ וקדשתו כי קדוש הוא י״ל קדושת התורה יותר חמורה מקדושת הכהן ודוקא בשוין מ״ע הוא ע׳ מפרש שם וע׳ פי׳ המשנה להר״מ בגיטין דוקדשתו בשוין הא ת״ח יותר קדושת התורה עדיף וממ״ש בפ״ד מה׳ כ״מ ה״ב צריך כל אדם משמע אף ת״ח יותר ממנו וי״ל. והנה המ״א הניח בהנחה קיימת בר״א ד״ה והא דסימן קכ״ח סעיף מ״ם נושא נשים בעבירה אין קורא ראשון ולישראל עולה ויש כח בשוא״ת (ונאמר דזה שוא״ת הוי) או כיון שחילל קדושתו ורשע לאו קדוש הוא ולפ״ז כהן רשע משאר עבירות ודאי אם יש כהן אחר קודם וא״ל הוא קודם לישראל מקרא דוקדשתו וקשיא לי כיון דת״ח יותר מכהן הוא קודם דקדושת התורה עדיפא כ״ש כהן רשע וישראל כשר הוא קודש יותר וכ״ת אה״נ תרוץ לי ההיא דקכ״ח סעיף מ״ם דמשמע דווקא עבירות דכהונה וצ״ע:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Behold,</b> in Gittin 59b Rabbi Chiya bar Abba derives the law from “and you shall sanctify him” (Vayikra 21:8), and so rule the poskim. See Magen Avraham, siman 201, seif katan 4, who explains at length that this is a Torah law. Rambam likewise counts as positive commandment 32 the obligation to honor the descendants of Aharon and give them precedence in every sacred matter, as it is stated, “and you shall sanctify him.” Rashi on the Torah cites the words “he shall be holy to you,” meaning that one sanctifies him even against his will. See also Re’em and the Perishah here, who explain that Rashi quoted the end of the verse, while the law is derived from “and you shall sanctify him.” Rav Mattanah derives it from “and he gave it to the kohanim, the sons of Levi, and to all the elders of Israel” (Devarim 31:9), and Rabbi Yitzchak, etc. Four practical differences may be suggested between these derivations. First, the verse “the kohanim, the sons of Levi” may establish precedence only for Krias HaTorah, but not for other matters, whereas “and you shall sanctify him” applies to every sacred matter. Sacred matters include giving him the better portion from maaser ani and tzedakah. See Tosafos there, s.v. velitol. As for the Gemara’s reference to a meal, I do not know what sanctity is involved, unless it refers to a seudas mitzvah among scholars. Second, from “and he gave it to the kohanim, the sons of Levi,” one might derive that a kohen precedes a levi and a levi precedes a Yisrael, since that is the order of the verse. However, in the Yerushalmi on this passage, Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai derives only that the kohanim, the sons of Levi, precede the elders of Israel; he does not derive precedence of a levi over a Yisrael from the apparent redundancy of “the sons of Levi.” It would therefore follow that a levi has no precedence over a Yisrael. See the commentary there in Tosafos. Third, a blemished kohen may be included in the verse “the kohanim, the sons of Levi,” just as “speak to the kohanim” includes even blemished kohanim. By contrast, from “for he offers the bread of your God,” one might say that a blemished kohen is not included in the positive commandment. Yet “offers” may also include eating. See Rambam, Hilchos Klei HaMikdash 4:1-2, and his count of positive commandment 32, to honor the descendants of Aharon. Fourth, if the law is derived from “the kohanim, the sons of Levi,” followed by “all the elders of Israel,” it appears that a kohen precedes even if he is an am haaretz and the other is an elderly talmid chacham, since the verse is stated without qualification. By contrast, from “and you shall sanctify him, for he is holy,” one may say that the sanctity of Torah is greater than the sanctity of kehunah, and the positive commandment applies only where they are equal. See the commentary there and Rambam’s commentary to the Mishnah in Gittin, where he explains that “and you shall sanctify him” applies where they are equal, but a talmid chacham takes precedence because the sanctity of Torah is greater. Yet Rambam writes in Hilchos Klei HaMikdash 4:2 that every person must honor him, which implies even a talmid chacham greater than he. This can be resolved. Magen Avraham assumes as established in siman 128, seif 40, that a kohen who marries a woman forbidden to him does not receive the first aliyah, while a Yisrael does. This may be because one may suspend a positive commandment through inaction, assuming this is considered inaction, or because he has profaned his sanctity and, as a wicked person, is no longer holy. According to this, regarding a kohen guilty of other sins, if another kohen is present, the other certainly precedes him. But if no other kohen is present, perhaps he still precedes a Yisrael by virtue of “and you shall sanctify him.” Yet I find this difficult: if a talmid chacham precedes a kohen because the sanctity of Torah is greater, then all the more so a righteous Yisrael should precede a wicked kohen, since the Yisrael possesses greater sanctity. If one answers that this is indeed so, then explain the ruling in siman 128, seif 40, which implies that this applies specifically to transgressions of kehunah. This requires study.

There is therefore no violation of a positive commandment. See Magen Avraham, seifim katanim 6 and 20, where this will be explained, with Hashem’s help.

<b>And if.</b> See Taz in siman 131, seif katan 10, that all seven days of celebration are like a festival. The same applies to a widower and widow for three days. One who remarries his divorcee may likewise have the status of a festival for himself, even if she had married another in the interim. Although siman 546, seif 2, permits remarrying one’s divorcee during the festival, see Magen Avraham there. This requires study. Regarding what he wrote in the name of Ritzba, see Beit Yosef in the name of Mordechai: where there are three bridegrooms, one kohen, and two Yisraelim, the kohen reads twice. Hagahos Maimoniyos, in the name of Ritzba, discusses two or three bridegrooms who are Yisraelim. It appears that he disagrees with Mordechai, for according to Mordechai no other person may be called and the kohen reads twice, so among the Yisraelim only one would receive an aliyah. According to Hagahos Maimoniyos in the name of Ritzba, the kohen and levi are called, followed by the two bridegrooms. Since the kohen and levi are properly called, both bridegrooms may also be called. If there is only one bridegroom, however, one should not add a fourth aliyah merely for him, since there is no necessity to do so. Rather, let him receive the third aliyah. Moreover, a bridegroom precedes other Yisraelim, as Peri Chadash proves from Moed Katan: from where do we know that a bridegroom sits at the head? As it is stated, “like a bridegroom who wears priestly splendor.” Therefore, where there is no kohen, the bridegroom is called first. See there. If one married a divorcee and another a chalutzah, the two are equal, unlike Knesses HaGedolah, because both are Yisraelim. If one married a virgin and the other a non-virgin, they may likewise be equal according to Beit Yosef. Levush writes that the custom is not to follow this rule. This appears to refer to the baalei bris, as Peri Chadash also implies. Magen Avraham, seif katan 2, however, understands that even regarding a bridegroom the custom is not to follow it, and Bedek HaBayis likewise states that the custom is not to do so even for a bridegroom. See Even HaEzer, siman 64, seif 2. It requires study whether a widower who marries a non-virgin is forbidden to perform work and whether the days are regarded as a festival for purposes of mourning, for a baal bris observes mourning during the seven days. See Eliyah Rabbah, siman 132, seif katan 4, and Peri Chadash here. This requires study regarding several practical laws.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>A kohen.</b> See Taz and the commentary to the Yerushalmi, Gittin, chapter 5, where Rabbi Yehudah follows his own view and derives the law from “the kohanim, the sons of Levi, shall approach, and all the elders of Israel.” This indicates that the kohen precedes the elders of Israel. By contrast, according to “and you shall sanctify him,” one may say that the sanctity of a talmid chacham and of Torah is greater. See there. Levush cites Rav Mattanah’s derivation together with “and you shall sanctify him,” whereas Nechalas Tzvi, seif katan 5, cites only “and he gave it to the kohanim” in Devarim 31. I do not know why all of them also cite “and you shall sanctify him,” since there are many practical differences, as I wrote at the beginning of the siman. Bach and Perishah answer that we require a case where everyone submits to the scholar because of his extraordinary wisdom, something not found nowadays. If they submit to him for some other reason, it does not suffice. If he is preeminent in his generation, the lesser person must acknowledge that he is greater. See there. Therefore, the custom is that the kohen receives the first aliyah. In seif 4 the Mechaber omitted the word “today,” which appears in Rambam, following the approach by which he resolved this in Beit Yosef. Even if the kohen waives his honor, he may not do so nowadays, even on Monday and Thursday.

<b>However.</b> See Taz. In my humble opinion, our master intended a particular interpretation, as I shall explain. One who studies Rashi to Gittin 59b, s.v. nitpardah hachavilah, finds two versions, and each may be understood in three ways. The plain sense of the first version is stringent: a levi is not called at all, whether first, second, third, fourth, or later. Although a Yisrael may receive the first aliyah in place of a kohen, a levi possesses distinct importance; if this became habitual, people would say that his importance derives from being a kohen. The second version is entirely lenient: a Yisrael is called first and a levi second, while the case in Kesubos concerns people of equal stature. See Tosafos, s.v. nitpardah. This is the Rosh’s view according to Bach. When the Rosh writes that the levi should not be called “in his place,” Bach explains that this means in the place of the kohen. The Rosh’s proof from Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi establishes only that where they are equal, the levi specifically is called first; this cannot be proved from Kesubos. Taz, however, regarded Bach’s insertion of “meaning” as forced and understood “in his place” literally, meaning the second position, thereby deriving two stringencies. According to the first version, the levi is not called at all, in any position. According to the second version, he is not called in his normal second position, but may be called first, whichever they choose. Alternatively, a Yisrael is called first and no levi after him, or a levi is called first. The passage in Kesubos proves nothing, and the proof comes only from Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi. What the Rosh writes concerning equality is somewhat difficult, for precedence over a kohen is not directly relevant here. According to this understanding, the second version prohibits the levi only in his regular position, but permits him third or fourth. A third explanation is that according to the first version he is never called third or fourth, and this also includes a case where a great Yisrael is present, for the levi is nevertheless called first. According to the second version, the levi may be called second when a great Yisrael was called first, while Kesubos refers to equals. This accords with the written tradition cited by Beit Yosef, and is also the view of Magen Avraham, seif katan 10. Taz understood the Rosh as containing the two stringencies of the first and second explanations. He therefore raised three objections: first, Rama should have written “some say,” since he follows the Rosh and Tur; second, according to the second version the levi may be called third or fourth, as indicated by the phrase “in his place”; third, the ruling should have specified that where they are equal the levi must be called first according to the Rosh. Taz therefore explains that Rama rules stringently like the first version, that the levi is not called third or fourth, and even if a great Yisrael is present, the levi does not receive an aliyah, lest people say that he is a kohen. But where they are equal, Rama relies on the lenient second version and the levi specifically is called first. The word “may” means that such a case can occur, and then the levi precedes. If the Yisrael is greater, the levi is not called first, nor is he called third or fourth, unlike the Rosh. Rama therefore did not write “some say.” Magen Avraham holds similarly, as will be explained there, with Hashem’s help. This is not the place to elaborate further.

<b>The custom is.</b> See Taz. I must elaborate here concerning Gittin 59b: when a kohen is called after another kohen, there is concern for a defect in the first; when a levi is called after another levi, there is concern for a defect in both, as I wrote in seif katan 8. Accordingly, when a second kohen is called after an intervening levi and Yisrael, the first concern is absent, but the second concern remains, namely that the second kohen may be a chalal, although he is receiving the fourth aliyah. Beit Yosef gives three answers. First, afterward another levi and Yisrael are also called. According to this, calling the last aliyah or maftir to a kohen would be forbidden. The same should apply to a levi in our case, unlike Rabbi Yehudah cited by Beit Yosef, who permits him according to two explanations. The Mechaber in seif 6 rules that a levi may not be called. This appears to mean even third or fourth, although he omitted the word “at all.” See what I wrote in seif katan 6, that there is no reason to distinguish between a case where a kohen is present and one where he is absent. Peri Chadash asks that there remains concern for those who leave between one reader and the next. The word “first” there appears to be a scribal error. It may mean the first, namely the second kohen, and the second, namely the second levi. Those present in the synagogue know that the kohen is valid, for otherwise how could a levi have been called after a Yisrael? Likewise they know the second levi is valid, for otherwise how could a Yisrael have been called after a kohen? We are not concerned that two people are disqualified. The concern is only for those who leave, who may say that the first, meaning the second kohen, is disqualified. In the Gemara too, the concern is for those who leave between one reader and the next, since it is permitted to leave then. Alternatively, they leave before the scroll is rolled, assuming that he is the sixth reader. See there. Bach asks that since the Tur writes that a Yisrael intervenes, it implies that no levi was present and the kohen read twice. The Tur therefore teaches a greater novelty, that even though only one Yisrael intervenes, we are not concerned. An even greater case would be a levi called after another levi with only a Yisrael between them, where we are not concerned for a defect in the first. Beit Yosef alludes to this when he writes, “If there is no levi, the kohen reads twice,” implying that the interruption of a Yisrael alone is enough. According to this, however, there should be concern that the second kohen is disqualified, since no levi is present. Bach holds that we do not permit so much as allowing the second kohen to read twice. See Magen Avraham, seif katan 14. He answers that the Tur discusses a case resembling the Gemara, where there is no second concern. In the Gemara, the kohen was established only through his father; in the Tur’s case, the second kohen was established through both his father and mother, so only the first defect is possible, and an interruption removes the concern. According to this, the last aliyah or maftir may be given to a kohen. Beit Yosef gives a second answer: the gabbai announces regarding the second, “Although he is a kohen.” Under the strict law of the Gemara this would still be forbidden because of those entering, and this is why a levi after a levi creates concern for both. Nowadays, however, regrettably, there is no concern for those entering. This announcement therefore suffices for a second kohen and likewise for a levi. An intervening Yisrael is still required so that we not openly depart from the explicit law of the Gemara. The Shulchan Aruch necessarily follows this second answer. First, it does not require a kohen, levi, and Yisrael after the second kohen. Second, in a case of a levi after a levi with a Yisrael between them, the concern regarding the second is that he is a Yisrael, yet Rama writes “therefore,” whereas according to the first answer the last aliyah or maftir should be forbidden. This shows that he follows the second answer. The announcement “although he is a kohen” also removes the concern in a case of levi after levi. The concern for a defect in the first is absent because people would immediately have protested. Yet this explanation is incomplete. Rather, regarding the second person, the announcement “although he is a kohen” removes any concern for either of them. Perishah offers a similar but different explanation. Nowadays, kohen after kohen remains forbidden because of concern for the first, just as in the days of the Gemara. There is no concern regarding the second because they announce that he is a kohen, without the word “although.” When a Yisrael intervenes, people would immediately have protested. The difficulty remains regarding levi after levi, where the Gemara says there is concern for both; why not announce regarding the second that he is a levi? The answer is that without an interruption there is proof that one of them is disqualified, whereas with an interruption there is no such proof. Beit Yosef’s third answer begins, “It may also be said that this custom,” etc. Taz understands this as a distinct answer, not part of the first. Taz’s words are now clear. He cites Beit Yosef’s question and first answer, raises Bach’s objection, and notes that Beit Yosef alluded to it by writing, “If there is no levi, the kohen reads twice.” We explained Bach’s objection as referring to one whose father and mother are established, parallel to the Gemara. Taz then cites the third answer, as he understands it, and asks why the case should not still be forbidden. He answers that Chazal instituted the order kohen, levi, Yisrael in the Mishnah, and any deviation causes misunderstanding. Likewise, levi after levi is permitted when a kohen is present, but prohibited when no kohen is present, as in seif 6. See siman 138, seif 1.
