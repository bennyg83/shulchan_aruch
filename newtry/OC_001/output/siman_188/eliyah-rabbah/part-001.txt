[3] <b>One should not mention, etc.</b> Rama writes: “Some say that even when saying Yaaleh VeYavo, one should not conclude ‘King, gracious and merciful,’ but should omit the word ‘King.’ This is a sound view, though I have not seen the custom followed.” Levush apparently omitted this because he left the Jewish custom in place, for a Jewish custom has the force of Torah. This is unlike some people whom I have recently seen changing our custom. Taz and Magen Avraham also write that the word should be recited because it is an independent phrase. It further appears to me that there is another reason why “King” is not said in Boneh Yerushalayim: it is a blessing adjoining another blessing. Since “King” is nevertheless mentioned in the Amidah, which is likewise composed of adjoining blessings, they did not wish to alter the wording in Bircas HaMazon, lest people err and omit it in the Amidah as well, thinking that it is omitted because the blessing adjoins another blessing. Tosafos on page 49 clearly does not accept the reason that earthly kingship should not be equated with heavenly kingship, and the same is evident from the Rashba cited by Beit Yosef. Rashba asks that “King” is mentioned many times in adjoining blessings, which shows that these references are not considered a formal mention of kingship. In my humble opinion, one may answer that Boneh Yerushalayim is different. Since heavenly kingship must be mentioned there after earthly kingship has been mentioned, people might mistakenly think that this constitutes a complete mention of kingship and would then mention kingship in other adjoining blessings as well. Yaaleh VeYavo, however, is infrequent, so they will not err. Moreover, they know it is not considered a formal mention of kingship because it is also recited in an adjoining blessing of the Amidah.

[ו] <b>ברוך אתה ה' מקדש וכו'.</b> משמע שאין חותמין במלכות רק ברוך אתה ה' ולא אלהינו מלך העולם, וכן בלחם חמודות דף ס"א תמה על שכתב דחותמין במלכות דהיכן מצינו מלכות בחתימה. ובשיירי כנסת הגדולה השיג על הלחם חמודות דלא ראינו וכו', אבל מצאתי בכסף משנה פרק ב' מהלכות ברכות על מה דפסק הרמב"ם דלא חתמינן בראש חודש זה לשונו, ונראה מדברי רבינו דבאינך חותם בהן בשם ומלכות, וכן דעת רוב המפרשים וכן משמע מדספקא ליה בראש חודש, אלמא דבאינך פשיטא ליה דחתים עד כאן לשונו. אבל דבריו תמוהין דעיינתי בכל הפוסקים ראשונים ואחרונים ואין מי שהזכיר מלכות בחתימה אלא רבינו יונה. והנראה לעניות דעתי אחר שנדקדק בהרי"ף וסמ"ג וכלבו שכתבו נוסח הברכה ברוך אשר נתן וכו' ברוך אתה ה' מקדש השבת וכן הוא בקצת נוסחאות ברמב"ם, וכיון שלא הזכירו בפתיחה שם אלא בחתימה משמע ליה דסבירא להו דאין מזכירין בפתיחה כלל, אלא בחתימה וסבירא ליה דהוא הדין דחותמין במלכות כיון שלא פתחו בה, ואולי דזה נמי דעת רבינו יונה שפירש דבריו על דברי הרי"ף ודו"ק וצריך עיון. ומכל מקום שאר כל הפוסקים הזכירו בפתיחה שם ומלכות ובחתימה שם ולא מלכות כדברי לבוש ושולחן ערוך וכן עיקר. ואגב אפרש דברי כסף משנה ואסלק ממנו התמיא נפלאה דסלקא לשיירי כנסת הגדולה דאף דאין חתימה בשם ומלכות מכל מקום הספק החתימה בראש חודש עומד עיין שם שהאריך. ונראה לעניות דעתי בהקדים עוד שני קושיות על כסף משנה חדא הא ספק דראש חודש מפורש בש"ס [ברכות] דף מ"ט, ועוד הא לרמב"ם אין ספק פסק כסתם דאינו חותם. לכן נראה לי דהכי פירושו דסבירא ליה אי היה החיתום בלא שם ומלכות אם כן אף דש"ס מסופק מכל מקום אנן נפסוק לאומרן מספיקא, אבל אי הוא בשם ומלכות ספיקן לחומרא שלא להוציא שם שמים לבטלה כדמשמע בלבוש סוף סימן ח', ואם כן הכי קאמר מדספקיא ליה בראש חודש רצה לומר שפוסק מספיקא דשם דאינו חותם אם כן על כרחך כל חיתום הוא בשם ומלכות כן נראה לי ודו"ק. כתב רש"ל מהרא"ש ואם אינו יודע לברכה זו חוזר לראש ברכת המזון אבל אם אינו יודע לברכת ראש חודש אינו חוזר. כתב הט"ז אם יודע התחלה והסיום אף שאינו יודע שאר הנוסחא כראוי אומרה ואין צריך לחזור, עד כאן. ומבואר מזה דבכל מקום שטעה בברכת המזון חוזר לראש ברכת המזון חוץ אם טעה בברכה רביעית אינו חוזר אלא להטוב ומטיב, וכן כתב מגן אברהם, ועיין לעיל סימן קפ"ג ס"ק י"ד:

[6] <b>Blessed are You, Hashem, Who sanctifies, etc.</b> This implies that the conclusion contains no mention of kingship, but only “Blessed are You, Hashem,” and not “our God, King of the universe.” Lechem Chamudos, folio 61, likewise expresses surprise at the statement that the blessing concludes with kingship, for where have we ever found kingship in the conclusion of a blessing? Sheyarei Knesses HaGedolah challenges Lechem Chamudos on the grounds that we have not seen this, etc. I found, however, that Kesef Mishneh, chapter 2 of Hilchos Berachos, writes concerning Rambam’s ruling that the Rosh Chodesh formula has no conclusion: “It appears from the words of Rabbeinu that the other formulas conclude with Hashem’s Name and kingship. This is the view of most commentators, and it is also implied by the fact that he was uncertain only about Rosh Chodesh, which shows that regarding the others it was obvious to him that they have a conclusion.” His words are astonishing, for I examined all the early and later poskim and found none who mention kingship in the conclusion other than Rabbeinu Yonah. In my humble opinion, however, after examining Rif, Semag, and Kol Bo, who give the formula, “Blessed is He Who gave, etc. Blessed are You, Hashem, Who sanctifies Shabbos,” and likewise certain versions of Rambam, it appears that because they did not mention Hashem’s Name in the opening but only in the conclusion, Kesef Mishneh understood that they hold that Hashem’s Name is not mentioned at all in the opening, but only in the conclusion. He therefore understood that the conclusion likewise includes kingship, since the blessing did not begin with it. Perhaps this is also Rabbeinu Yonah’s view, since he explains the words of Rif. Examine this carefully; it still requires study. In any event, all the other poskim mention Hashem’s Name and kingship in the opening, and Hashem’s Name but not kingship in the conclusion, in accordance with Levush and Shulchan Aruch, and this is the principal ruling. Incidentally, I will explain Kesef Mishneh’s words and remove the great difficulty raised by Sheyarei Knesses HaGedolah. Even if the conclusion does not contain both Hashem’s Name and kingship, the doubt concerning whether the Rosh Chodesh formula has a conclusion still remains; see his lengthy discussion. In my humble opinion, two further questions must first be raised against Kesef Mishneh. First, the uncertainty concerning Rosh Chodesh is explicit in Berachos 49a. Second, Rambam himself has no uncertainty, for he rules in accordance with the anonymous view that it has no conclusion. It therefore appears that Kesef Mishneh means as follows. If the conclusion did not contain Hashem’s Name and kingship, then even though the Gemara is uncertain, we would rule that it should be recited because of the doubt. If, however, it contains Hashem’s Name and kingship, the uncertainty is resolved stringently by refraining from reciting Hashem’s Name in vain, as implied by Levush at the end of siman 8. Thus, when he says that the matter was uncertain regarding Rosh Chodesh, he means that because the ruling resulting from the doubt is not to conclude, it necessarily follows that every such conclusion contains Hashem’s Name and kingship. This appears correct; examine it carefully. Maharshal writes in the name of the Rosh that if one does not know this blessing, he returns to the beginning of Bircas HaMazon, but if he does not know the Rosh Chodesh blessing, he does not return. Taz writes that if he knows the opening and conclusion, even if he does not know the remainder of the text properly, he recites it and need not repeat Bircas HaMazon. This demonstrates that whenever one errs in Bircas HaMazon, he returns to the beginning of Bircas HaMazon, except when he errs in the fourth blessing, in which case he returns only to HaTov VeHaMeitiv. Magen Avraham writes likewise. See above, siman 183, seif katan 14.

**** ENGLISH ****
[8] <b>And Yom Tov for gladness, etc.</b> Lechem Chamudos and Malbushei Yom Tov write that Shulchan Aruch and Levush omit the words “to His people Israel” here, whereas Rosh and Tur include them. I found support for Shulchan Aruch and Levush in Kol Bo, folio 43, and Rabbi David Abudraham. Nevertheless, Tosafos on page 43, Semag, and Beit Yosef have the wording: “Blessed are You, Hashem our God, King of the universe, Who gave Shabbosos for rest and Yamim Tovim to His people Israel as a sign and covenant and for rejoicing,” etc. This is more correct, since “to His people Israel” then applies to both Shabbos and Yom Tov. Baal Halachos Gedolos, folio 13, also has this wording. Lechem Chamudos further comments on the Rosh’s wording, “for rejoicing, for gladness, and for remembrance”: “I have already written that some omit ‘for remembrance.’” His words are difficult, for the poskim who say this write it only regarding Yom Tov alone, not when Shabbos and Yom Tov coincide. One must say that he holds there is no reason to distinguish between Yom Tov alone and Shabbos together with Yom Tov. Bach, however, gives a reason: since Shabbos commemorates Creation and Yom Tov commemorates the Exodus from Egypt, we add “for remembrance,” which refers to both Shabbos and Yom Tov. See there. I also examined all the poskim and found no one who disagrees when Shabbos and Yom Tov coincide. On the contrary, Tur and Kol Bo, who disagree regarding Yom Tov alone, nevertheless write that when Shabbos and Yom Tov coincide one says “for remembrance.” Knesses HaGedolah writes that on Rosh Hashanah the conclusion is “Who sanctifies Israel and the Day of Remembrance.” Magen Avraham, however, writes that one does not conclude, just as on Rosh Chodesh, because some hold that it is a mitzvah to fast on Rosh Hashanah, and therefore one who fasts then need not observe another fast for having fasted on a festival, as stated in siman 597. It appears to me, however, that one who eats on Rosh Hashanah is obligated according to all authorities to eat bread, and for him it is like any other Yom Tov. Moreover, even regarding Rosh Chodesh some hold that one concludes. Since many poskim forbid fasting on Rosh Hashanah, one may certainly rely upon them. It therefore appears that if he began HaTov VeHaMeitiv, he must return to the beginning, as on any other Yom Tov. A sick person who ate on Yom Kippur and forgot Yaaleh VeYavo, however, should say: “Blessed are You, Hashem our God, King of the universe, Who gave holy days to Israel, this Day of Atonement,” and should not conclude the blessing, for it is like Rosh Chodesh and failure to mention it does not require repetition.
