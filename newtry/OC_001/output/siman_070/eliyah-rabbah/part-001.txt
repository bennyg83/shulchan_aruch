[2] <b>And according to Rashi, even, etc.</b> The Olas Tamid discusses this at length and raises objections. See there, and see the novellae of the Rashba, where the matter will be clarified. The Levush writes that one who is engaged in Torah study must interrupt for Krias Shema and its blessings. Nevertheless, even when the time for Krias Shema has arrived, as long as sufficient time remains to recite it later, he may continue studying and recite it afterward. This is difficult, for below, siman 89, seif 6, he writes that it is prohibited to study once the time for Krias Shema and prayer has arrived. One may answer that here he discusses one who already began, and we say that he need not interrupt. He likewise writes at the end of siman 106 that if sufficient time remains, he does not interrupt. This is strained and requires study. It is also difficult, for the Mishnah in Shabbos 11a states that one interrupts for Krias Shema, and this applies even where sufficient time remains, as the Rosh and Ran write in the first chapter of Shabbos and in the chapter Lulav HaGazul, and as the Beit Yosef, Shulchan Aruch, and Levush rule in siman 652. One may nevertheless answer that the rulings here and at the end of siman 106 discuss one who began before the prohibited time, and therefore he does not interrupt, as below at the end of siman 652. This too is strained. It is also difficult that the language of Rambam in chapter 2 of the laws of Krias Shema, and of Rabbeinu Yerucham on page 24, who write that if one was engaged in Torah study and the time for Krias Shema arrived, he interrupts, implies that he interrupts even if he began before the prohibited time. The Beit Yosef similarly infers this at the end of this siman. Moreover, below at the end of siman 652 I challenged the ruling concerning one who began permissibly. See there, and see what the Soles Belulah writes on this matter. In my humble opinion, the Levush understood the Beit Yosef at the end of siman 106, who cites the Baal HaMaor as brought by the Ran, as ruling that one does not interrupt when sufficient time remains. With due respect, however, he did not reach the depth of the matter, for the Baal HaMaor must refer only to prayer, which is D'Rabbanan. For Krias Shema, which is D'Oraisa, one interrupts even when sufficient time remains, as stated above. Proof of this is that otherwise the Ran's own words in the first chapter of Shabbos would contradict one another, and they would also contradict his words in the chapter Lulav HaGazul. This is even clearer to one who studies the Ran himself in the first chapter of Shabbos, for the Baal HaMaor refers specifically to prayer. The Beit Yosef cites his words only briefly. See below, siman 93.

[3] <b>With communal needs, etc.</b> This is because a communal positive obligation takes precedence. The Olas Tamid objects from the Yerushalmi: one who is occupied with communal needs is considered as though he were occupied with words of Torah, yet he interrupts and recites Krias Shema. In my humble opinion, this presents no difficulty. The Beit Yosef means that he is regarded as one who teaches Torah to the public, a case he mentioned immediately beforehand, where Rabbeinu HaKadosh did not interrupt while teaching the public. This is also implied by the Levush, who writes that it is like teaching Torah to the public and that a communal positive obligation takes precedence. The Olas Tamid also overlooked what the Kesef Mishneh writes in chapter 2 of the laws of Krias Shema, that Rambam interprets the Yerushalmi specifically with regard to prayer. Were it referring to Krias Shema, it should have said that one engaged in communal needs interrupts, just as one engaged in Torah study interrupts. Thus the Beit Yosef himself raised this objection and therefore interpreted the passage as referring to prayer. In truth, this creates a major difficulty, for the Beit Yosef's statements contradict one another. I am even more astonished at the Lechem Mishneh there, who writes that the Beit Yosef in siman 70 interprets it as referring to prayer and then argues against him at length. I do not understand his difficulty, and the remainder of his discussion also calls for objection, but this is not the place to elaborate.

[5] <b>And according to the Raavad, etc.</b> The Levush writes this, and the Malbushei Yom Tov comments: See below, siman 235, seif 5, where the ruling is stated anonymously in accordance with the Raavad. In my humble opinion, here the case is one in which he began before the time of obligation arrived, as the Beit Yosef writes in his second answer. The Levush therefore carefully writes, and the time for Krias Shema arrived, implying that he began beforehand. In siman 235, however, the case is one in which he began after the time arrived, as it explicitly says: If he began eating after the time for Krias Shema arrived, he interrupts. It is therefore clear that, to the contrary, the anonymous ruling follows Rambam. The Bach understood the Shulchan Aruch as ruling according to the Beit Yosef's first answer, and therefore objected that the second answer is primary. He was not precise, for the Shulchan Aruch copies Rambam's language, and the Beit Yosef already proved, as did the Bach himself from Rambam's language, that Rambam refers to one who began before the time arrived. The Bach likewise concludes this way in chapter 2 of the laws of Krias Shema. This also creates a difficulty with Magen Avraham, who writes in astonishment against the Beit Yosef that even the Raavad agrees that if one began before the time he does not interrupt, for no one disputes this, and that the Raavad merely failed to analyze Rambam's words and thought that Rambam referred to one who began after the time arrived. I find his words astonishing. Would the Raavad err regarding something clear and demonstrable from Rambam? Moreover, his assertion that no one disputes this is incorrect, for below, siman 652, I wrote that Tosafos in Sukkah 38a, s.v. What, implies that even if one began before the time, he interrupts. I also wrote in this siman that Rambam and Rabbeinu Yerucham hold that even if one began studying before the time, he interrupts for Krias Shema. One may nevertheless distinguish that eating is more severe than Torah study, for with Torah study he does not interrupt, since it is not truly an interruption, as Krias Shema itself consists of words of Torah, whereas with eating and the other activities he does not interrupt, as the Kesef Mishneh writes there. Below, in siman 489 regarding counting the Omer and in siman 652, the Shulchan Aruch likewise rules anonymously that if one began before the prohibited time, he does not interrupt. The Beit Yosef in siman 235 cites the Mordechai and Ran to the same effect, and this is also implied by the Shulchan Aruch and Levush there. This is the primary ruling, in accordance with Rambam's reasoning, and Magen Avraham agrees.
