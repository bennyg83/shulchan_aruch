<b>(There, se'if 6)</b> The whole congregation receives forty lashes after the Minchah prayer, etc. Note: I will copy here one cluster of thoughts that came to my mind; would that this saying be fulfilled in us. The Sages said in Makkos 23: all those liable to kareis who received lashes are exempted from their kareis, the words of Rabbi Chananya ben Gamliel. I saw in Sefer Orach Chayim on Pesachim, Frankfurt an der Oder edition, in the introduction, that he brought the Baal ha-Maor's question against the Rambam: why did he rule like Rabbi Chananya ben Gamliel, since it is evident that his colleagues disagree with him? For it is explicit at the beginning of chapter Elu Hen ha-Lokin that Rabbi Akiva holds those liable to kareis are included in forty lashes, for if they repented, the Heavenly court forgives them. That is, do not say: how can we make him liable for two punishments? Therefore he said that if he repents they forgive him, and there will not be two punishments. If it were like Rabbi Chananya ben Gamliel, even without this there are not two punishments. It is proved that his colleagues disagree with Rabbi Chananya ben Gamliel, so why did the Rambam rule this way? The Ramban answers his question that he holds like Rabbi Chananya ben Gamliel, and the intent is this: since we rule that one liable to two deaths is judged with the more severe one, why do we lash him? Let him be judged with the more severe, with kareis. Therefore he said that lashes are more severe, for with kareis, if he repents, they forgive him, while with lashes repentance does not help. In my poverty, I say: although I am dust under their feet and am considered nothing compared with the Baal ha-Maor and Ramban, nevertheless in this their words are not correct. First, what the Baal ha-Maor wrote, that he came to give a reason why we punish him with two punishments, is not correct, for the rule that you do not obligate him for two punishments applies only by human court. We learn this from "according to his wickedness," for one wickedness you may obligate him, and you may not obligate him for two wickednesses. That means the court cannot obligate him in two. But where one is by human hands and one is by Heaven, it is not considered two punishments. Also, the answer is not correct, for what difference does it make that if he repents they forgive him? In the end, perhaps he will not repent, and there will be two punishments. The Ramban's answer, that he comes to prove that lashes are more severe than kareis, is astonishing, and with his honor in its place, the words of the Talmud in Kesubos 33b escaped him for the moment. It says there: perhaps lashes are more severe even than death by court, as they said, "had they beaten Chananya, Mishael, and Azaryah, they would have worshiped the image." If so, it is proved that lashes are more severe even than death by court, for repentance also does not help death by court. Why would Rabbi Akiva need the reason of repentance? It is true that the Talmud rejects this, saying there is a difference between something with a fixed limit and something with no fixed limit. Nevertheless, it is difficult: the Talmud should have brought support from that Mishnah, where Rabbi Akiva says lashes are more severe because repentance does not help, proving that without this lashes would not be severe but lighter than kareis. If so, regarding death by court, for which repentance also does not help, lashes are certainly lighter. Why did it not prove this from that Mishnah? Perforce, this is not the Mishnah's intent. The Mishnah's intent appears as follows, with the help of Heaven. On the contrary, Rabbi Akiva's words agree with Rabbi Chananya ben Gamliel. It is known what is written in Yoma chapter 8 regarding the four divisions of atonement: there is a sin for which repentance alone helps, and there is a sin for which repentance alone does not help, but afflictions or death are needed. For those liable to kareis, repentance alone does not help, but afflictions are needed. Therefore Rabbi Akiva said that those liable to kareis are included in forty lashes, meaning because through lashes he will be exempt from kareis. He came to give the reason how through lashes he will be exempt from kareis. Therefore he said that the lashes will help him for this: if he would not be lashed, he would be liable to kareis even if he repented, for repentance is not enough to atone for those liable to kareis. But once he is lashed, then if he repents together with the lashes, the Heavenly court forgives him. Therefore we lash him, for then it is in his hand to repent and be exempt from kareis. Thus, on the contrary, Rabbi Akiva holds like Rabbi Chananya ben Gamliel, and the ruling of the authorities like Rabbi Chananya ben Gamliel is well settled; examine, and this is fine with the help of Heaven.<br><b>(There, se'if 6 in the gloss)</b> Yom Kippur atones only for those who repent and believe in its atonement, but one who kicks against it and thinks in his heart, "what does this Yom Kippur help me?" it does not atone for him. The language of the Yerushalmi, Yoma chapter 8, halachah 7, is as follows: one who says, a burnt-offering does not atone for me, it atones; it is impossible that it atone for me, it does not atone. Against his will, Yom Kippur does not atone? It atones. It is impossible that it atone for me? It atones against his will. Rabbi Chananya son of Rabbi Hillel said: not all from this law; can a person say to the king, you are not king? End quote. Necessarily this Yerushalmi follows Rabbi, who holds that whether he repented or did not repent, Yom Kippur atones. But what it says, "not all from this law," is incomprehensible; see Korban ha-Edah, who pressed to explain this. It seems in my humble opinion that the intent of the Yerushalmi is based on what we read in Shevuos 13, that Rabbi agrees regarding kareis of the day; see there. This is the intent of the Yerushalmi when it says that although Yom Kippur atones even without repentance, nevertheless not all from this law: not for everything does Yom Kippur atone against his will, but for kareis of the day it does not atone. This is like saying that a person cannot say to the king, you are not king. So too, if he desecrates the very body of Yom Kippur and performs labor or eats on it, it does not atone. This is clear in the intent of the Yerushalmi. Or one can say further that the Yerushalmi's intent in saying "and not all from this law" is this: certainly one who denies and says Yom Kippur does not atone, this itself is a sin, for he does not believe in the Torah, where it is written, "for on this day He shall atone for you." Therefore Yom Kippur atones for him for all the transgressions in the Torah, even against his will, but for this sin itself, that he does not believe Yom Kippur atones, it does not atone, for this is like the kareis of the day. Therefore the Yerushalmi says "not all from this law": not for everything does it forgive, but from this law itself, from this sin itself, it does not atone, for this is a great sin, as if a person says to the king, you are not king. So too, for this sin itself, that he does not believe in Yom Kippur, it does not atone. This is well settled; examine carefully. The Korban ha-Edah explained that the intent is that one who kicks against its atonement is nevertheless not comparable to one who does not kick against it; see there. Besides that this is forced, the sugya in the first chapter of Shevuos escaped him, that Rabbi agrees regarding kareis of the day. Rather, the intent is as above: certainly it is Rabbi, but when one kicks against Yom Kippur itself, it does not atone, for it is like kareis of the day, where Rabbi agrees that Yom Kippur does not atone, as above. What the Talmud in Yoma says, that one who says "I will sin and repent" twice is like Rav Huna, that once he transgressed and repeated, etc.; see its explanation in my work on the Torah at the beginning of the year 611, in parashas Bereishis; see there and examine. Regarding what they asked against the Rambam, that he copied only one time "I will sin and repent," which is against the Talmudic sugya, and regarding what the Talmud there in Yoma says, "shall we say the Mishnah is not like Rabbi," and what requires examination in the entire sugya, see with the help of Heaven my work on the Torah from the year 599, in the haftarah of parashas Tazria, on the verse "and I bowed in the house of Rimmon." See there correct matters and several laws that branch from this; see there and examine.
