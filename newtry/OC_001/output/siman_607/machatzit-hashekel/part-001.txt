**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And it seems to me that specifically, etc.</b> Meaning not as appears from the words of the Beis Yosef, that even with a sin that is not public, one may specify aloud, for he wrote generally in the Shulchan Aruch and did not distinguish between public and not public. This follows his approach in his Beis Yosef, and I need to set out the matter briefly. It is stated in Yoma 86b: one must specify the sin, as it is said, "Please, this people has sinned a great sin, and they made themselves gods of gold," the words of Rabbi Yehudah ben Bava. Rabbi Akiva says: "fortunate is he whose transgression is forgiven, whose sin is covered." Rather, what Moshe said, "and they made themselves gods of gold," is like Rabbi Yannai, etc.: the silver and gold You increased for them, etc., caused them to make gods of gold. This is our reading. But the Rif and Rosh read: Rabbi Akiva says, he need not; rather, how do I fulfill "and they made themselves gods of gold"? Like Rabbi Yannai, etc. The Tur wrote that the law is that one need not specify, like Rabbi Akiva, for the law follows Rabbi Akiva against his colleague; and the Rambam ruled like Rabbi Yehudah ben Bava. The Beis Yosef wrote that the Rambam's reason is that Rav necessarily holds like Rabbi Yehudah ben Bava, for it is stated there: Rav Yehudah said in Rav's name, he raised a contradiction: it is written "fortunate is he whose transgression is forgiven, whose sin is covered," and it is written "one who covers his transgressions will not succeed." It is not difficult: here with a public sin, here with a sin that is not public. When it is not public, "fortunate, etc., whose sin is covered." Rav Nachman said: here with transgressions between man and his fellow, "one who covers, etc., will not succeed," for by revealing it many will plead before the one he sinned against so that he forgive him; and here with transgressions between man and the Omnipresent, about which it says "fortunate is he whose transgression is forgiven, etc." Rav necessarily does not hold like Rabbi Akiva, for the sin of the calf was public and even so Rabbi Akiva needed to answer the verse "and they made themselves gods of gold" like Rabbi Yannai, etc. One must therefore say that he holds that even a public sin should not be specified; necessarily Rav holds like Rabbi Yehudah ben Bava, and therefore the Rambam ruled like him. The Beis Yosef's conclusion is: regarding practical law, since the Rif and Rosh agreed to rule like Rabbi Akiva, so we adopt. Nevertheless, it seems that if he wants to specify the sin, he has permission, for Rabbi Akiva did not forbid specifying; he only came to say that one who does not specify his sins is more fortunate. Perhaps Rabbi Akiva said this only where he specifies aloud, but quietly it is proper to specify so that he be more ashamed, and it is still "whose sin is covered" since it is not heard by people, end quote of the Beis Yosef. Based on this he ruled here in the Shulchan Aruch that one need not specify, like Rabbi Akiva, and nevertheless if he wants to specify aloud, he has permission; quietly it is more preferable to specify, as his conclusion in the Beis Yosef. But regarding this one must say that he retracted from what he wrote in his book, that aloud one is more fortunate when he does not specify. Rather, nevertheless, if he wants, he may specify aloud, though he is not so fortunate. In the Shulchan Aruch he wrote generally, "and if he wants to specify," meaning aloud, as the Magen Avraham wrote, "he has permission," and he did not conclude that if he does not specify he is more fortunate. This implies that he holds that in truth one who does not specify is not more fortunate than one who specifies. So too wrote the Taz. Perhaps in the Shulchan Aruch he relied on the reading of the Rif and Rosh, who copied Rabbi Akiva's language as "he need not specify," as above, implying that whether he specifies or does not specify is equal, and neither is more fortunate than the other. So too wrote the Chemdas Moshe. The Magen Avraham disagrees with this, and says that with a sin that is not public, according to everyone it is forbidden to say it aloud, as will be explained.

<b>Rather, Rabbi Yehudah ben Bava holds that before the Omnipresent, etc.</b> And Rabbi Akiva holds even before the Omnipresent, etc.; and in this there is no distinction. That is, unlike the Beis Yosef, who holds that the dispute of Rabbi Yehudah ben Bava and Rabbi Akiva and the dispute of Rav and Rav Nachman are of one kind, but that Rav and Rav Nachman stated their matter according to everyone. Rav and Rav Nachman speak of one who confesses aloud, where people hear. In this, Rav holds there is a distinction: with a public sin, "one who covers his transgressions will not succeed," so that he should be ashamed and atoned; but with a sin that is not public he should not specify, for it is disgraceful, as they said, "one who specifies his sins is brazen." Rav Nachman distinguishes between transgressions between man and his fellow or between him and the Omnipresent. But Rabbi Yehudah ben Bava and Rabbi Akiva disagree when one confesses quietly before the Omnipresent and no person hears. In that case there is no distinction between public and not public, for everything is revealed and known before Him, blessed be He. Nor is it relevant there that he should be ashamed, for what difference is there whether he specifies or not? In both cases the shame is the same before Him, blessed be He, since everything is revealed before Him. Rabbi Yehudah ben Bava holds that one should specify because so we find with Moshe Rabbeinu. But aloud, the tanna'im did not speak, and one should rule like Rav, that with a sin that is not public it is forbidden to specify. So ruled the Raavad in chapter 2 of the Laws of Repentance, and according to the Kesef Mishneh this is also the Rambam's view, except that the Rif and Rosh ruled like Rav Nachman. The Magen Avraham's wording, "and Rabbi Akiva holds even before the Omnipresent, etc.," is not precise in the word "even," and this is easy to understand.

<b>And there is a difficulty from what Rashi wrote in Chullin, etc.</b> For we learned there: one who slaughters outside, and says he slaughters for the sake of something brought as a vow or gift, such as for the sake of a burnt-offering, his slaughter is invalid because of appearance. One who sees will think that now he consecrates it, and he is found slaughtering consecrated animals outside, and they will err that it is permitted to slaughter consecrated animals outside. Also, when they see that people eat from this slaughter, they will err that if consecrated animals were slaughtered outside, they are permitted to eat. Therefore his slaughter is invalid. But if he slaughtered for the sake of something that does not come as a vow or gift, such as for the sake of a sin-offering, it is valid, for there is no reason to say he now consecrates it as a voluntary offering, since it does not come as a voluntary offering, and those who hear know there is nothing in his words. Rashi answered that there is no concern for this, for if he had sinned and become liable to bring a sin-offering, it would have a voice, etc.

<b>דצ"ל על הקל תחלה.</b> וכמ"ש הטור בסימן תקפ"ב כדמצינו בדוד. תחלה אמר שגיאות מי יבין ואח"כ ביקש על הזדונות דכתיב גם מזדים חשוך עבדך. ואח"כ אל ימשלו בי אז איתם. פי' עבירות החזקו' ומביא משל לזה א"ר אבא כותים הללו יודעים לבקש על הפתחים תחלה אומרים תן לי מעט מים ונותנים לו תנו לי בצל א' נותנים לו. אומר בצל בלא פתא נסיב לבא. כך הצדיקים יודעים לרצות בוראם שנאמר שפתי צדיק ידעו רצון עכ"ל הטור. לא יהיה כסדר א"ב דהוידוי מסודר דרך א"ב והטעם מבואר בספרי מוסר ובגלוי ובסתר הוא באות ג'. וזדון ושגגה הוא באות זיין:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>For one should say the lighter matter first.</b> As the Tur wrote in siman 582, as we find with David: first he said, "Who can discern errors," and afterward he asked about intentional sins, as it is written, "also from intentional sinners restrain Your servant." Then, "let them not rule over me; then I will be whole," meaning strong transgressions. He brings a parable for this: Rabbi Abba said, these Kutim know how to beg at doorways. First they say, give me a little water, and he gives him. Give me one onion, and he gives him. He says, an onion without bread harms the heart. So the righteous know how to appease their Creator, as it is said, "the lips of the righteous know favor," end quote of the Tur. It will not be in alphabetic order, for the confession is arranged alphabetically, and the reason is explained in mussar books. "Openly and secretly" is under the letter gimel, and "intentional and inadvertent" is under the letter zayin.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>As stated in Temurah chapter 5: one who says, this is the substitute of a burnt-offering and a peace-offering.</b> Rabbi Meir says the first expression takes effect; Rabbi Yosi says that if from the beginning he intended to say both, since a person cannot utter two things at once, his words stand. **** ENGLISH ****
<b>The order of confession, etc.</b> This is the language of Piskei Mahara Isserlin, siman 118: burnt-offering, sin-offering, variable sin-offering, definite guilt-offering and doubtful guilt-offering.
