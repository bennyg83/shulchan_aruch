<b>(Seif 3)</b> If he discovered on Shabbos, etc., because human dignity is great. Note: The source of this ruling is the Gemara in the third chapter of Berachos. I find the Gemara difficult. Why does it not raise the same question from what is stated in the chapter Kol Kisvei, Shabbos 118b, where Rabbi Yose said: “I never transgressed the words of my colleagues. If they had told me, ‘Ascend to the duchan,’ I would have ascended”? In truth, there is a positive prohibition in doing so, as is explicit in the second chapter of Kesubos. Why, then, was he permitted to transgress a positive commandment out of respect for his colleagues? It must be because human dignity is so great that it overrides a prohibition of the Torah.

Tosafos there in Kol Kisvei asks the opposite question: what prohibition would there be in his ascending? See Magen Avraham, siman 128, regarding this. Yet if there were a prohibition, Tosafos would have found the statement understandable. On the contrary, however, if there were a prohibition, how could he have been permitted to act because of human dignity, especially where the act is performed actively? One must say that a positive commandment may indeed be overridden by human dignity, while a negative commandment is not overridden.

This supports the view that a negative commandment is more stringent than a positive commandment, and that the rule that a positive commandment overrides a negative commandment is a special Torah decree. According to those who maintain that a positive commandment is more stringent, as discussed in the sugya of feeding a dangerously ill person the less severe prohibition first, where the later authorities cite the dispute, this passage is very difficult. If human dignity overrides a positive commandment, it should certainly override a negative commandment.

There is an additional difficulty. Tosafos writes there that the prohibition would be a blessing recited in vain. This involves the negative commandment, “You shall not take Hashem’s Name in vain.” How, then, could it be permitted because of human dignity? According to those who maintain that uttering Hashem’s Name in the form of a blessing is prohibited only D’Rabbanan, the matter is understandable. According to those who maintain that it is prohibited according to the Torah even in the form of a blessing, however, it is difficult why the Gemara did not raise a question from this source. This presently requires study.

I was also uncertain regarding the rule that human dignity is so great that it overrides a prohibition of the Torah: if the danger of humiliation arose through the person’s own negligence, does human dignity still override the prohibition or not? At first glance, one might derive an a fortiori argument from the rule that a positive commandment overrides a negative commandment. Nevertheless, when the positive commandment arose through negligence, it does not override the negative commandment, as Tosafos writes. This should all the more apply to human dignity, which does not override a Torah prohibition but only a D’Rabbanan prohibition. If the situation arose through negligence, it should certainly not override the prohibition.

According to this, however, the Gemara’s question in Berachos 20a is difficult. Rav says that one who discovers kilayim in his garment must remove it even in the marketplace. The Gemara challenges this from the teaching that human dignity is so great that it overrides a Torah prohibition. Why is this a question? One could answer that the latter teaching concerns a situation that did not arise through negligence, whereas discovering kilayim in one’s garment resulted from negligence, since he could have inspected the garment beforehand. Tosafos considers contact between a lighter and more sacred offering, and likewise the case of mixed applications of blood, to involve a positive commandment arising through negligence. This case should certainly be viewed as one arising through negligence, and human dignity should therefore not override the prohibition. Why, then, does the Gemara raise its question?

There is an even greater difficulty from Eruvin 41b. Rav Nachman said in the name of Shmuel: one who intentionally left his Shabbos boundary has only four amos. The Gemara asks, “What does this teach us? It is obvious. If one who was taken out by non-Jews has only four amos, is there any question that one who intentionally left has only four amos?” Why is this a question? The Gemara later relates that they asked Rabbah: What is the law if he needs to relieve himself? He answered: human dignity is so great that it overrides a Torah prohibition. One could therefore say that Shmuel teaches that one who intentionally left has only four amos even if he needs to relieve himself, because the principle of human dignity would not apply where the situation arose through his negligence in leaving intentionally. The Gemara’s question thus necessarily proves that human dignity applies even where the situation arose through negligence. The reason for this, and the Gemara’s basis for assuming it and framing its question accordingly, require study.

Regarding what the Shulchan Aruch writes here, that if he discovered on Shabbos while in a karmelis that the tallis he is wearing is invalid, etc., this follows Mordechai, who proves it from the Gemara in Menachos, which establishes the case in a karmelis, where the prohibition is D’Rabbanan. At first glance, there is no sufficient reason for the ruling. Even though the Shabbos prohibition is only D’Rabbanan, there remains a Torah prohibition because he is wearing a four-cornered garment without tzitzis. Mordechai therefore proves that there is no inherent prohibition against wearing a garment without tzitzis. Rather, where one can attach tzitzis, he is obligated to do so; where he cannot, he may continue wearing it. Rabbi Eliezer of Metz disagrees.

To resolve Mordechai’s difficulty according to the dissenting view, it appears that Rabbi Eliezer of Metz follows Rambam, who maintains that a Torah-level doubt is treated leniently according to the Torah and stringently only D’Rabbanan. Some authorities distinguish between a doubt involving kares and other doubts, maintaining that Rambam concedes that a doubt involving kares is treated stringently according to the Torah.

Accordingly, the matter is understandable. In Berachos, the Gemara challenges the opinion that human dignity does not override a Torah prohibition from the baraisa concerning one who was going to slaughter his Korban Pesach, etc. The Gemara answers that the verse “and for his sister” explicitly reveals the exception. It then asks that we should derive the general rule from there, and answers that passive nonperformance is different. Rashi asks that involvement with a meis mitzvah is active conduct. Rashi answers that the prohibition was never stated regarding a meis mitzvah. See Chavos Yair and Pnei Yehoshua concerning Rashi’s intent.

In my work Sefer HaChayim I wrote that this may depend upon the dispute whether tumah is merely overridden for the community or entirely permitted for the community. According to the opinion that tumah is permitted for the community, one may say the same regarding a meis mitzvah. If, however, tumah is only overridden for the community, then even the verse “in its appointed time,” which permits the communal offering in tumah, does not make tumah fully permitted. Although an individual’s offering is postponed and the communal offering is not, tumah nevertheless remains merely overridden. The same would apply to a meis mitzvah. One could then derive from there that human dignity overrides a Torah prohibition even through active conduct.

In Sefer HaChayim, however, we wrote that the Gemara remains uncertain whether burial is required because of disgrace or because it provides atonement. Tosafos explains that the question is whether burial is required because of disgrace alone or because of disgrace together with atonement, for disgrace certainly exists. The Gemara therefore cannot simply derive a general rule from meis mitzvah, because perhaps it is the combination of human dignity and atonement that overrides the prohibition, not human dignity alone. We nevertheless wrote that because the Gemara leaves unresolved whether burial is because of disgrace or atonement, its question, “Let us derive from there,” may be intended to resolve that burial is because of atonement. It then rejects the proof by saying that passive nonperformance is different. Regarding a nazir and a meis mitzvah, one may indeed say that the passage follows the opinion that tumah is permitted for the community, and burial may still be because of atonement.

This applies there. In Menachos, however, the case concerns Mar, son of Ravina. Ravina explicitly maintains in Yoma 6b that tumah is merely overridden for the community. His son presumably follows his father, as the Gemara frequently assumes. Since he maintains that tumah is merely overridden, the Gemara’s question, that we should derive a general rule from meis mitzvah, is well founded. One may still answer that a meis mitzvah is different because atonement combines with disgrace, while human dignity alone does not override a Torah prohibition. Yet this remains an unresolved question in the Gemara and is therefore a doubt.

Accordingly, regarding Shabbos, which carries judicial execution, the doubt must be treated stringently according to the Torah. Perhaps burial in the case of a meis mitzvah is permitted because of atonement, while human dignity alone does not override a Torah prohibition. The Gemara therefore had to establish the case as a karmelis, where the prohibition is D’Rabbanan. Regarding the obligation of tzitzis, however, the issue is only a positive commandment. The doubt is treated leniently according to the Torah. We may therefore say that burial is required because of disgrace, and consequently that human dignity overrides a Torah prohibition even through active conduct. Only D’Rabbanan is the doubt treated stringently, and a D’Rabbanan prohibition is again overridden because human dignity is great.

The view of Rabbi Eliezer of Metz is thus understandable if he follows Rambam. Were it not for human dignity, however, one could certainly maintain that there is a Torah prohibition against walking in a garment without tzitzis, contrary to Mordechai. Mordechai, in turn, may follow the authorities who maintain that a Torah-level doubt is treated stringently according to the Torah, and he therefore proves his ruling as stated. This appears correct to me. See what I wrote concerning part of this matter in my work on Yoreh Deah, siman 372, regarding the tumah of kohanim and the above-cited words of Rashi, and you will understand it well.

See Shaar HaMelech, chapter 7 of Hilchos Lulav, who cites Pri Chadash and Ritva as explicitly stating the opposite, that even declaring it ownerless is forbidden on Shabbos.
