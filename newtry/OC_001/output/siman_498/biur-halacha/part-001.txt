<b> אם באו ביו"ט</b> -  כן הוא לשון הרמב"ם וכ"כ הרא"ה בחידושיו ומשמע מזה דאם באו בעיו"ט שוב נפקע מנייהו שם מדברי ומן הסתם הוי להו מוכנים ופשוט דדוקא בדידע בהו ביה"ש. וכן מצאתי בבה"ג הלכות יו"ט בסופו וז"ל שם בהמה דאתיא מאפר ועיילא בתוך התחום מעיו"ט ולא הוי ידע בה ומטאי לגבי ישראל ביו"ט א"נ אתאי בשביל גוים דלאיסור תחומין ליכא למיחש דאמרינן הבא בשביל ישראל זה מותר לישראל אחר וכש"כ הבא בשביל גוי ולאיסור מוקצה הוא דאיכא למיחש וכו' עי"ש באריכות הסוגיא דשם [והנה מש"כ א"נ אתאי בשביל גוים צ"ל דס"ל כמ"ד דשל נכרי צריך ג"כ הכנה ובאמת פלוגתא הוא בירושלמי כמו שהביא הרשב"א והרא"ה ועיין פר"ח סי' תצ"ז] והנה מבואר בבה"ג דאם באה בתוך התחום בעיו"ט לא משכחת שיהיה עליה שם מדברי אא"כ לא ידע בה ומוכח בהדיא דאם ידע בה אע"פ שלא הוא הביאה מ"מ מן הסתם היא כמוכנה כשאר בהמות שבביתו דדעתו עלייהו אם יצטרך להו ביו"ט אכן הרב המאירי כתב להדיא דאפילו באו מעיו"ט דמסתמא לאו דעתיה עלייהו אא"כ זימנם בפירוש מערב עי"ש וצ"ע לדינא ועיין בספר בית מאיר שמאריך בפרט זה:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>If they arrived on Yom Tov.</b> This is Rambam's wording, and Ra'ah writes likewise in his novellae. This implies that if they arrived on Erev Yom Tov, their classification as animals that graze in the wild is removed, and they are presumed prepared. It is obvious that this applies only if he knew of them at bein hashemashos. I likewise found in the conclusion of the Halachos of Yom Tov in Behag: “An animal that came from the grazing area and entered the techum on Erev Yom Tov, but he did not know of it, and it reached a Jew on Yom Tov, or alternatively, it came for non-Jews, there is no concern for the prohibition of techum, because we say that an item brought for one Jew is permitted to another Jew, and all the more so an item brought for a non-Jew. The only concern is the prohibition of muktzeh,” etc. See there for the lengthy discussion. Regarding what he writes, “or alternatively, it came for non-Jews,” he must hold like the opinion that an item belonging to a non-Jew also requires preparation. This is actually a dispute in the Yerushalmi, as Rashba and Ra'ah cite. See Pri Chadash, siman 497. It is explicit in Behag that if the animal entered the techum on Erev Yom Tov, it can retain the classification of an animal that grazes in the wild only if he was unaware of it. This clearly proves that if he knew of it, even though he did not bring it himself, it is ordinarily considered prepared like the other animals in his home, because he has it in mind should he need it on Yom Tov. Meiri, however, writes explicitly that even if they arrived on Erev Yom Tov, he presumably did not have them in mind unless he expressly designated them beforehand. See there. The practical halachah requires study. See Beis Meir, who discusses this detail at length.

<b> לפיכך וכו'</b> -  אם ידוע וכו'. הלשון מוקשה מאוד דמה שהצריך דוקא ידוע אינו שייך כלל למה שכתב מקודם ועוד דמאי קאמר אח"כ אפי' באו בשביל ישראל הלא גם מקודם מיירי בזה וכבר הקשה כן הפר"ח ובעבודת הקודש שממנו נובע דין זה הלשון מתוקן ביותר וז"ל שם המוקצה אינו אלא מד"ס והולכין בספיקו להקל ולפיכך גוי שהביא בהמה במקולין של ישראל אע"פ שהובאו לצורך ישראל אם ידוע שלנו וכו' וענין אחר הוא ואינו שייך לבייתיות ומדבריות וקרי לאיסור תחומין ג"כ מוקצה ונראה כמו שהגהתי בפנים הוא הנכון ובעבור שענין הקודם שכתוב בשו"ע אא"כ באו בשביל ישראל דיבר לענין בייתיות ומדבריות ומתיבת לפיכך הוא ענין אחר לכך חזר וביאר אפילו באו לצורך ישראל:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Therefore, etc.</b> “If it is known,” etc. The wording is very difficult. The requirement that it specifically be known has no connection to what was written previously. Moreover, what does it mean afterward by saying, “even if they came for a Jew,” when the earlier passage also addressed that case? Pri Chadash already raised this difficulty. In Avodas HaKodesh, the source of this ruling, the wording is much clearer: “Muktzeh is only from the words of the Sages, and in a case of doubt we rule leniently. Therefore, if a non-Jew brought an animal into a Jewish meat market, although it was brought for Jews, if it is known that it spent the night,” etc. This is a separate matter and has no connection to domestic and wild animals. He also refers to the prohibition of techum as muktzeh. The wording as I emended it in the main text appears correct. Since the preceding matter written in Shulchan Aruch, “unless they came for a Jew,” concerned domestic and wild animals, while the words “therefore” begin a different matter, it returned and clarified, “even if they came for a Jew.”

<b> כדי לאכול ממנה וכו'</b> -  עיין במ"ב דבהמה בריאה אין לשחטה אא"כ צריך לה ביו"ט גופא והיינו אפילו בצריך רק לכזית משום דא"א לכזית בשר בלא שחיטה ומותר ממילא ג"כ להפשיט כל העור מן הבהמה משום דאל"ה מימנע ולא שחיט [וכ"כ הפמ"ג בא"א] ואין להקשות הלא קי"ל דבשביל מימנע ולא שחיט אין להתיר איסור דאורייתא כמו שכתב המגן אברהם בתצ"ז סקי"ח והפשטה הלא מלאכה גמורה היא ולא היה לנו להתיר כ"א הפשט מקצת העור כדי ליטול הכזית שתחתיה ולא יותר דכל שיעור הפשט והפשט שהוא כדי לעשות קמיע יש עליה שם מלאכה בשבת וחייב עליה (כדאיתא ברמב"ם פי"א מהלכות שבת הלכה ה') י"ל דביו"ט אין בזה איסור דאורייתא מטעם הואיל ואי מיקלעי ליה אורחים הרבה שצריכים לאכול היה בודאי מותר להפשיט בשביל כולם כדי להאכילם השתא נמי ליכא איסור ואין בזה רק איסור דרבנן ומותר בשביל דאל"ה מימנע ולא שחיט ולפ"ז אין מותר להפשיט כל העור רק בשיש שהות כדי להאכיל לכ"א כזית צלי עכ"פ מבע"י אבל אם ירצה לשחוט סמוך לערב כדי לאכול ממנה כזית ואין שהות לצלות ולהאכיל לאורחים הרבה אין מותר להפשיט רק כדי צרכו להבשר שנוטל ממנה:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>In order to eat from it, etc.</b> See Mishnah Berurah, which states that a healthy animal may not be slaughtered unless it is needed on Yom Tov itself. This applies even if one needs only a kezayis, because obtaining a kezayis of meat is impossible without slaughtering. Consequently, one may also skin the animal completely, because otherwise he may refrain from slaughtering. Peri Megadim writes likewise in Eshel Avraham. One should not object that we rule that a Torah prohibition may not be permitted merely because otherwise he may refrain from slaughtering, as Magen Avraham writes in siman 497, seif katan 18, while skinning is a full melachah. Seemingly, only enough skinning to remove the kezayis beneath it should have been permitted, and no more, since skinning the amount needed to make an amulet is classified as a melachah on Shabbos and incurs liability, as Rambam states in chapter 11 of Hilchos Shabbos, halachah 5. The answer is that on Yom Tov this does not involve a Torah prohibition because of the principle of ho'il: if many guests arrived and needed to eat, it would certainly be permitted to skin the animal for all of them. Consequently, even now there is no Torah prohibition, but only a D'Rabbanan prohibition, which was permitted because otherwise he may refrain from slaughtering. According to this, completely skinning the animal is permitted only when sufficient time remains before nightfall to feed at least a kezayis of roasted meat to each potential guest. If he wishes to slaughter shortly before evening merely to eat a kezayis and there is insufficient time to roast and feed meat to many guests, he may skin only as much as he needs for the meat he will take.

<b> וי"א דאין להפשיטה כלל</b> -  משמע מלשון זה לכאורה דאפילו הפשט קצת כדי לבדוק הריאה ג"כ אינו רשאי וכן משמע ממגן אברהם סקי"ב ע"ש אכן נראה דזה דוקא כשאין פנאי ביום לבדוק הריאה לכן אין רשאי לפשוט כלל משא"כ כשיש פנאי ושוחט אותה שחושש שמא תמות עד שתחשך בודאי נכון יותר שיבדוק הריאה להכשיר הבשר כראוי אי מקלעי ליה אורחים ואין להחמיר אלא אהפשט דכולא בהמה ומשום דלא התירו חכמים בזה משום דהשתא עכ"פ לית ליה אורחים שיצטרכו לבשר והנה מדברי המגן אברהם שם בסקי"ג דתלה דין דאין צריך בדיקה משום דאסור להפשיטה ומשמע לכאורה מזה דאפילו יש פנאי ג"כ אין להפשיט ולבדוק אחרי שאין בדעתו לאכול בשר מ"מ לענ"ד נראה כמ"ש ובפרט דבלא"ה הרבה פוסקים חולקים על הג"ה זו וכמו שכתבתי בשה"צ וע"כ עכ"פ בודאי אין להחמיר באופן זה:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Some say that it may not be skinned at all.</b> This wording apparently implies that one may not even skin a small portion in order to inspect the lung. Magen Avraham, seif katan 12, also appears to say this. It seems, however, that this applies specifically when insufficient time remains in the day to inspect the lung, and therefore one may not skin it at all. If time remains, and he slaughters because he fears that the animal may die before nightfall, it is certainly more proper to inspect the lung so that the meat will be properly permitted should guests arrive. The stringency applies only to skinning the entire animal, because the Sages did not permit this when he presently has no guests who need the meat. Magen Avraham in seif katan 13 connects the rule that the animal need not be inspected with the fact that it may not be skinned. This could imply that even where time remains, it may not be skinned and inspected if he does not presently intend to eat the meat. Nevertheless, in my humble opinion the ruling is as I have written. In addition, many authorities disagree with this gloss, as I wrote in Shaar HaTziyun. At minimum, there is certainly no reason to be stringent in this case.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>But if it was born with its blemish.</b> If it was born unblemished and acquired a blemish that same day, Pri Chadash writes that it is also permitted, according to our ruling in siman 308 that there is no muktzeh for half of Shabbos. Here too, it was fit at bein hashemashos by virtue of its mother. Although it became prohibited upon birth, it became fit again when it acquired a blemish. See there. The language of the Gemara, however, does not imply this. Peri Megadim and Chemed Moshe likewise express hesitation based on Rashi's wording. See there. I found that Meiri addressed why the Gemara had to speak of an animal born with a blemish, since it could have spoken of one that acquired a blemish that day. He answers that perhaps once it was born unblemished, it was completely set aside, because its becoming fit again is not a development one would reasonably anticipate, as it is uncommon. This is unlike sunrise upon dried fruit that became wet from rain, which is common. In such a case we say that since it was fit at bein hashemashos, when it is later set aside in a manner that a person could reasonably anticipate would be reversed, it is permitted. The same applies to an animal born with its blemish, because even at the time it was set aside there was a circumstance that made its becoming fit again reasonably foreseeable, since one sees the blemish and it merely remains to show it to a sage to determine whether it is temporary or permanent. This appears clear to me. These are his words. He thus rules explicitly contrary to Pri Chadash.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>He can investigate it.</b> See Taz, who gives several reasons for the rule that blemishes may not be examined. He concludes that if a prohibited liquid became mixed with permitted liquid and there was not sixty times its amount, or dry prohibited food became mixed with permitted dry food and there was no majority, and afterward more permitted food was added until there was sixty or a majority, a sage may not rule on the mixture on Yom Tov, just as in the case of a firstborn animal. See there. It appears that he wrote this only according to Maggid Mishneh's reason, that the concern is moving muktzeh without need. Maamar Mordechai and the GraZ in Kuntres Acharon write likewise. It is unclear, however, what muktzeh exists in prohibited food mixed with permitted food. At bein hashemashos it was at least fit for dogs, and something prepared for dogs is considered prepared for people, as explained above in seif 5. If the mixture occurred on Shabbos and more permitted food was then added, it was fit for people at bein hashemashos, and there is no muktzeh for half of Shabbos. I found that Nehar Shalom raises part of this point. See there, where he also questions the comparison itself: how do we know to derive this case from a firstborn animal? Machatzis HaShekel likewise writes that according to Terumas HaDeshen's reason, the Taz's ruling has no basis. I also found that Bigdei Yesha straightforwardly rejects the Taz's ruling from practical halachah, giving several reasons. See there.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>He should not pluck.</b> See Mishnah Berurah. Plucking a bird's feathers is more severe than plucking wool. Plucking wool is considered an unusual manner, whereas feathers are normally removed this way, and it is a derivative of shearing. I saw in Pischei Teshuvah, Yoreh Deah 308, citing Shaar HaMelech, that he proves from Chullin 137a that goat hair has the same status as bird feathers because it is normally removed by plucking and is not considered an unusual manner. He therefore questions why the authorities did not distinguish between goat hair and sheep's wool regarding a firstborn animal. See Pischei Teshuvah. However, Tosafos on Shabbos 27a, s.v. “Noga,” write that hair and the fine fleece are distinct. The fine fleece is the thin material upon goats from which garments are made. It is concerning this that the Gemara says its normal method is plucking, like a bird's feathers. The goat's hair is the coarse material from which sacks are made. Accordingly, goat hair has the same status as animal wool. At minimum, the Gemara proves regarding the thin fleece that its normal method of removal is plucking, like a bird's feathers. Thus, even according to Rambam, who is lenient regarding animal wool and permits intentional plucking because it is only an unusual manner, as Beit Yosef explains, one who slaughters a goat must be very careful not to pluck the thin fleece from its neck, for according to Rambam this entails a Torah prohibition. Ramban's first reason for permitting plucking from a bird, namely, that this stage of the melachah will ultimately be displaced on Yom Tov when the feathers are removed after slaughter, does not apply here, because the goat's entire hide will ultimately be skinned.

<b>Even the blood of a domesticated animal.</b> See Mishnah Berurah. Magen Avraham questions this from the Gemara on page 8. The Gemara asks, “If so, the same should apply on a weekday,” and answers that on a weekday people will say that he needs the earth to clean his courtyard. Since the act is prohibited on Yom Tov, this shows that on Yom Tov we do not attribute it to cleaning his courtyard, and it proves that cleaning one's courtyard is forbidden on Yom Tov. Magen Avraham writes that it is difficult to say that the Gemara concerns one who slaughtered in a corner, for if so the same should apply on a weekday. Eliyah Rabbah, Maamar Mordechai, and Nehar Shalom agree with Magen Avraham's first answer, that the Gemara concerns a corner. They answer his objection by explaining that on a weekday a person certainly normally cleans every corner of his courtyard, whereas on Yom Tov, if the filth is not in a place where people walk, one may not clean it. Their answer is not entirely simple, as I shall explain below.

In any event, the unqualified discussion of the Gemara proves that one may not initially slaughter a koy on Yom Tov under any circumstances, even in the middle of the courtyard where all household members walk. Although in such a place covering the blood does not create concern, because observers will say that he is cleaning his courtyard, it is still forbidden. This is also implied by the gloss, which says, “If he slaughtered it,” etc. The reason must be that the Sages did not wish to distinguish among cases regarding initial slaughter.

The intent of the gloss is also unclear. If he slaughtered in the middle of the courtyard, may he cover the blood even solely to fulfill the mitzvah of kisui hadam, because there is no concern regarding observers? Or perhaps covering for the purpose of the mitzvah is forbidden in every case, because the Sages did not distinguish in their decree based on where the slaughter took place, just as they forbade initially slaughtering in every case. The gloss may have come to permit covering only when he fears becoming soiled while walking, in which case he may cover to prevent his clothing from becoming dirty. Magen Avraham inclines to this in his concluding words. Yet the gloss's phrase, “even the blood of a domesticated animal,” implies that all the more so the blood of a koy may be covered, since two factors are present, the mitzvah of kisui hadam and cleaning the courtyard.

This can nevertheless be rejected. When his intention is to clean the courtyard because he is concerned that his utensils or clothing will become soiled, he may cover the blood and also intend to fulfill the mitzvah of kisui hadam. If he would not have been concerned about the filth and his principal intention is only the mitzvah, it is forbidden, even though observers will say that he is cleaning his courtyard, because he himself knows that his sole intention is covering the blood. This appears from the Gemara's statement, “If he comes to consult,” etc. See Rashi there. I also saw that Machatzis HaShekel derives proof for this from that discussion.

Know further that in my humble opinion he should not cover the blood of a koy even when he wishes to clean the courtyard unless he also intends to fulfill the mitzvah of kisui hadam. Otherwise, he actively nullifies the mitzvah according to the principle that mitzvos require intent. If he did not cover it at all, the mark might remain visible until evening, when he could cover it. Magen Avraham's statement that the case is one in which he intends to clean his courtyard also means that he should additionally intend to fulfill the mitzvah of kisui hadam.

Know also that Gra explains the gloss as follows: Riaz understands that the Gemara concerns earth that is not fully prepared, such as where a spade was inserted into it, or the like. See there. Ra'ah in Bedek HaBayis, bayis 1, shaar 5, also holds this view. This implies that according to our accepted ruling following the authorities who understand the Gemara to concern even fully prepared earth, we do not accept this gloss.
