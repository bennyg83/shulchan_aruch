<b>And after midnight, etc. </b> In the Tur it is written: if one transgressed and prayed after four hours until midnight, although he does not have reward as prayer in its time, he nevertheless has reward of prayer. Beit Yosef wrote on this two paths. One is that the Tur learned from what the Gemara challenges on Rabanan who say until midnight and no more — and we say: one who erred and did not pray Shacharit prays two Minchah, and it answers: all day he prays and goes until midnight — from here they give him reward of prayer in its time, from here they do not give him reward of prayer, from here they give him reward of prayer in its time they do not give him — from this we learn for R' Yehuda that after four hours it is so: what difference to me after Shacharit for R' Yehuda and what difference after midnight for Rabanan. And he wrote that according to his explanation it is not precise that after Shacharit for R' Yehuda only; rather also until night it would be so, since once the time has passed it would be for R' Yehuda after his time as for Rabanan after their time. And that the Tur only took until midnight is not precise. And he challenged this: since we learn after Shacharit from “one who did not pray Shacharit prays two at Mincha,” let us also learn this matter — that just as when he did not pray Shacharit he has compensation at Mincha, this is specifically when he did not cancel intentionally, but when he cancelled intentionally he has no compensation at all; if so, also for the morning prayer after Shacharit the law would be thus. And why did the Tur write that even if he transgressed intentionally and did not pray before Shacharit, he prays afterward? Therefore he wrote a second path: the Tur learns after Shacharit for R' Yehuda from reasoning — since for Rabanan even lechatchilah he prays until midnight and has reward of prayer in its time, so too for R' Yehuda at least regarding reward of prayer not in its time. And therefore immediately after midnight it is forbidden to pray and it is a blessing in vain. And some say: after Mincha when he prays two — for there he prays in the time of the Mincha prayer. And he brought proof from the words of the Rashba that we will mention shortly; and accordingly Rama wrote his words here that after midnight it is forbidden, etc. And it is difficult on the second path, for we say in the Gemara’s explanation “all day he prays and goes” — implying until night he prays, only he does not have reward like in its time; and this would also be for R' Yehuda, for the challenge is to R' Yehuda like to Rabanan from “one who did not pray Shacharit,” etc.; automatically the answer would also be for him like for Rabanan. And one could ask: why does the Gemara challenge “and all agree until midnight and no more” from “one who did not pray Shacharit” — perhaps it is so that until midnight and no more, but at Mincha it is better since it is the time of prayer? And it seems to me that the first path is true, only that the source of this law is not from “one who did not pray Shacharit,” etc., as Beit Yosef thought; and from this it followed for him to say that if one cancelled intentionally before Shacharit, afterward he has no remedy — and a difficulty emerged for him on the Tur, as stated. Rather the source is from what is stated in the Gemara “all day he prays,” etc.; and this would also be for R' Yehuda. And in the Gemara they said to Rabanan: until midnight he has reward of its time, and afterward all day, even after Mincha, he has reward not in its time. From this we also learn for R' Yehuda that all day he has compensation — only that if it is after Shacharit when the hour of Mincha has not yet arrived, he certainly may pray even lechatchilah, even if he cancelled intentionally until that hour, as stated in the Gemara “all day is time for prayer,” only he does not have reward of prayer in its time. And after the time of Mincha has arrived, which is six and a half hours, then it is forbidden for him to pray at that hour Shacharit, since the time of Mincha has arrived; therefore he must give precedence to the Mincha prayer and afterward pray to complete Shacharit, and he has reward of prayer not in its time. And since in this there is a prohibition to pray before he prays Mincha, therefore they said in this that if he cancelled intentionally until that hour he has no compensation — for after midnight R' Yehuda and Rabanan are equal, that he prays until six and a half, which is half an hour after midnight. And therefore Rif and Rosh did not mention the measure here but stated simply: if he transgressed and prayed after four hours. But Rambam and Tur wrote after four hours until midnight; and they too did not come to exclude that half hour but to exclude after the time of great Mincha. And that they did not write until six hours — because I would have said “until” includes it and he could pray even if six and a half hours had arrived; and in truth it is forbidden until he prays Mincha first — therefore they wrote until midnight, so that even if you say “until” includes it there is no error here, for there remains another half hour. Rather between after Shacharit and until midnight they disagree, and R' Yehuda holds that also in that hour they do not give him reward of prayer in its time, even though he prays immediately at that hour without giving precedence to the Mincha prayer before it. And what Beit Yosef brought as proof for the second path from the responsum of the Rashba — with apology to our master Beit Yosef — it seems to me there is no proof from there, for thus he wrote on that which the conclusion is “one who erred and did not pray Mincha prays two Maariv,” etc.; however specifically in the time of prayer, while he is engaged in his prayer, he returns and completes what he erred in his prayer — but not outside the time of prayer. For if not, what is “prays two Maariv” and “prays two Mincha” that it says? Let it say: one who erred and did not pray Shacharit before midnight returns and prays after midnight; one who erred and did not pray Mincha returns and completes all night, end quote. Our master Beit Yosef thought the Rashba comes to exclude even before the time of Mincha, which is six and a half, that he should not pray. And it does not seem so to me, for the Rashba took the parallel of “one who did not pray Mincha” in his proof — he should have taken “one who erred and did not pray Mincha returns and completes all night,” and there he does not exclude except after the time of the evening prayer, for there is no time interrupting between the end of the time of Mincha and the beginning of Maariv. Rather it is simple that also regarding the morning prayer the Rashba did not come to exclude except that immediately after he prays Mincha he prays to complete Shacharit — and this is explicit in his wording that he wrote “while he is engaged in his prayer,” etc.; but if he waits afterward, compensation does not apply to what he will pray a second time afterward, for otherwise the author of the Talmud would not have tied compensation of Shacharit to the Mincha prayer but would have said that after midnight Rabanan may complete the morning prayer, and similarly for the Mincha prayer all night. Or he intended that compensation be at the time he is engaged in prayer in any case and not afterward. This is what seems clear and correct in the explanation of the Tur’s words; and how much more so Rama’s words here also accord with the halacha that after midnight it is forbidden to pray — that is, after the time of great Mincha has arrived, which is six and a half hours, it is forbidden for him to pray unless he prays Mincha first and afterward completes Shacharit. But before that — that is, after Shacharit until the hour of great Mincha — he prays Shacharit, only he does not have reward of prayer in its time, as seems to me for halacha and practice:

<b>And if he began reciting the blessings, etc. </b>This is difficult for me. The Gemara challenges the prohibition against extending a greeting before prayer from the Mishnah that states that between the sections of Krias Shema one may initiate and return a greeting. Rashi explains that Krias Shema precedes prayer, yet the Mishnah teaches that one may initiate a greeting. According to this, what is the Gemara’s question? In the Mishnah he has already begun the blessings of Krias Shema. On the strength of this difficulty, Terumas HaDeshen ruled regarding attending to one’s affairs that reciting some of the blessings before Baruch She’amar does not help, as Rama writes nearby. All the more so, then, should it not help here. Moreover, Shulchan Aruch itself writes nearby, regarding attending to one’s affairs, that it is forbidden specifically until after the Amidah. Why, then, did it rule leniently here regarding extending a greeting? One cannot answer that Shulchan Aruch distinguishes between extending a greeting and attending to one’s affairs, because the principal proof that reciting some blessings does not help regarding one’s affairs is the Gemara’s question from the law between the sections, as explained, and that question concerns extending a greeting. It therefore appears to me that reciting some blessings certainly does not help regarding extending a greeting either. Here, however, the discussion concerns bowing alone, without extending a greeting, as Beit Yosef explicitly writes in the name of Orchos Chaim. He concludes that if one recited some blessings, there is not as much concern. This means that bowing alone need not be forbidden, but extending a greeting is certainly not permitted by reciting some blessings, just as is written afterward regarding attending to one’s affairs.

<b>Or to set out on a journey. </b>Beit Yosef, in the name of Mahari Abuhav, writes that this applies specifically according to the view of the Rosh cited by Tur at the end of the siman, that praying at home is preferable. Beit Yosef distinguishes that here the case is one in which he is able to delay but wishes to hurry, etc. It appears that none of this is necessary. Here the case is even one in which he will take a short journey and much of the day will remain, so that upon arriving he will still be able to pray standing. Nevertheless, it is forbidden because he is attending to his own affairs before prayer. The later case, by contrast, is one in which he would have to pray while traveling and not standing. The prohibition against attending to one’s affairs before prayer does not apply there, because he sets out early, before the principal time for prayer has arrived.
