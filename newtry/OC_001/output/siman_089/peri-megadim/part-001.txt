<b>And after.</b> See Taz. There are five approaches here, as I shall explain with Hashem's help. The first is Beit Yosef's first approach. The questioner asks why the time extends only until midday and no further, since Rabbi Yochanan states that one may pray Minchah twice. Certainly, during the half hour before another prayer has arrived, one should still be able to pray Shacharis. The answer is that he receives reward for prayer, but not reward for prayer at its proper time. See Kesef Mishneh, chapter 3 of Hilchos Tefillah, halachah 1. According to this, there is no distinction between Rabbi Yehudah and the Sages. Outside its proper time one may pray even during that half hour. The same would apply, according to Rabbi Yehudah, from plag haMinchah until nightfall, for one may pray Shacharis throughout the entire day, as we shall explain below with Hashem's help. This applies only where one erred, but not where he acted intentionally. The second approach is that one may not pray during the half hour after midday, and “the entire day” refers to the Sages, who hold that Minchah may be prayed until evening. According to Rabbi Yehudah it means only until plag haMinchah. We do not multiply the dispute between Rabbi Yehudah and the Sages more than necessary. Each approach therefore produces both a leniency and a stringency. If we combine the two approaches, we may say that we certainly do not multiply the dispute: according to Rabbi Yehudah, one who intentionally delayed may pray until midday, while during the half hour after midday one who erred may pray, all the more so since another prayer has not yet arrived. See Kesef Mishneh there. By doing so we resolve three questions. First, the question of Lechem Mishneh and Taz here, why the Gemara does not answer that “until midday” excludes the following half hour. Second, Maharsha's question, why the Gemara does not answer that the case was intentional. According to this approach, Rabbi Yehudah permits even an intentional delay until midday, while the answer concerning both the Sages and Rabbi Yehudah is that afterward one receives reward for prayer but not for prayer at its proper time. Rambam's wording is also precise: “If he intentionally passed the time or erred, until midday,” implying that after midday intentional delay is not included. This also explains why the Mechaber omitted an explicit ruling that after midday one may not pray, for perhaps both approaches are true. Rama, however, refers to siman 108, seif 3, where the Mechaber cites Rashba according to his own understanding that one may not pray during the half hour; see there. Rambam's wording, “if he intentionally passed the time or erred,” may be a progression from the lesser case to the greater, teaching that after midday one may not pray. More plausibly, however, even then he does not receive reward for prayer at its proper time. The Mechaber says “if he erred or intentionally passed the time,” yet he still receives reward for prayer, and this fits well. Taz was satisfied with saying that “the entire day” excludes the half hour, but the phrase “the entire day” implies until nightfall. According to Rabbi Yehudah, the time for Minchah ends at plag haMinchah, and although it is no longer the time for Minchah and much of the day remains, he may nevertheless pray Shacharis. In truth, this requires practical study, because siman 108 implies that once the time of the following prayer has passed, one can no longer make up Shacharis. With Hashem's help, the wording of Levush will be explained there. Taz also raises Lechem Mishneh's question and therefore explains, following Bach, that both leniencies are true: even during the half hour after midday, one who intentionally delayed may still pray. This differs from the combined approach, which at least excludes an intentional delay. It appears to me that since the Gemara did not answer that the time extends until midday but that an intentional delay is excluded during the following half hour, we learn that even an intentional offender may pray then, since the time for Minchah has not arrived. Once Minchah has arrived, however, makeup is available only for an inadvertent omission, not an intentional one. This is the reverse of the first approach from one extreme to the other. It follows that according to Rabbi Yehudah one may likewise pray throughout the day just as according to the Sages. Their only dispute concerns the reward for prayer at its proper time. According to the Sages this reward extends until midday; during the half hour after midday one receives only reward for prayer outside its time; once Minchah arrives, only one who erred may make it up. There are thus three periods. The same applies according to Rabbi Yehudah: until four hours is the proper time; from four hours until six and one half hours, even one who intentionally delayed may pray, though outside its proper time; once Minchah arrives, only one who erred may pray. Taz's wording may be reconciled this way. When he writes that according to the Sages one may pray even after Minchah, he means after the time for Minchah has arrived; according to Rabbi Yehudah, he means after the time for Minchah has ended. His explanation of Lechem Chamudos means that one should ideally pray the makeup immediately after praying Minchah and not interrupt between them. Certainly, however, even if one delayed a long time he may still make up Shacharis, as is proved by the phrase “the entire day” regarding Rabbi Yehudah, which includes after plag haMinchah. His statement that there is no intervening time between Minchah and Maariv implies that twilight is also the time for Minchah according to the Sages. See siman 233 and what we wrote in our novellae. Accordingly, he is speaking according to Rabbi Yehudah. His reference to “after the time for Maariv” means only that one should not interrupt between the prayers by delaying. Nevertheless, his statement that this is Rama's intention is difficult, for Rama's reference to siman 108, seif 3 points to the Mechaber's understanding of Rashba there, and since Rama does not disagree, he apparently accepts it. You now have four approaches, as explained.

<b>אמנם</b> השיטה החמישית המעיין בפרישה היא כך דכולי יומא מחצות עד הערב אפילו מזיד שכר שלא בזמנה ובטעה מחצי שבע אם משלימה שכר בזמנה יהבי ליה ולהכי קרי משלים. ולפ״ז בחצי שעה מזיד ושוגג שווין שלא בזמנה יע״ש. והעד מדלא תי׳ דמ״ש לא ש״מ מזיד נמי מתפלל ואהא אמר כולי יומא אף מזיד שלא בזמנה יהבי ליה. ומ״מ לדינא אין לסמוך ע״ז כ״א כפסק המחבר והר״ב כאן דלאחר חצות לא ולענין הזיד בזמן מנחה בק״ח ס״ז נדבה דוקא לא סתם ואי״ה שם יבואר עוד ואלו עד חצות יראה דצריך להתפלל ושכ״מ לשון הר״מ ז״ל יצא ידי חובת תפלה (המחבר שינה קצת והבן) ובטור ב׳ גירסאות ואם עבר ולא התפלל יתפלל עד חצות חובה וי״ג ואם עבר והתפלל עד חצות י״ל רשות והנך רואה שי״ל שחובה להתפלל עד חצות ושלא בזמנה יש לו ובזמן תפלה אם רצה נדבה הוי לא חובה. וחצי שעה אסור לו להתפלל אף בטעה ונדבה דל״ש כיון דאלו שגג עט״ז בק״ח ה׳ ואי״ה שם יבואר עוד והפרישה כתב שאין חילוק בין הגירסאות וכמ״ש י״ל בזה. אמור מעתה דג׳ זמנים וד׳ דינים יש מד״ש עד חצות צריך להתפלל ויצא י״ח תפלה כמ״ש הר״מ ז״ל ושכר שלא בזמנה יש לו והכין מסתבר דלא מפשינן מחלוקת ולרבנן צריך ודאי ה״ה לר״י צריך וא״י למה שינה המחבר מלשון הר״מ ז״ל (ואף אם נרכיב קשה זה) בחצי שעה אפילו שוגג אסור להתפלל ובזמן מנחה בשוגג ישלים ולהמחבר שכר שלא בזמנה ולהפרישה שכר בזמנה (ואפשר יש נ״מ אם אירע לו מצוה דרבנן בזמן השלמה איזו ידחה והבן) ובמזיד אם רצה יתפלל נדבה ואין חובה עליו ואי״ה בק״ח יבואר עוד. וראיתי להפר״ח סימן ק״ח ס״ג פי׳ בתה״ד כט״ז כאן ומשמע שם הפסיק והלך לעסקיו אין מתפלל והנה מר״י ע״פ המנחה וכולי יומא קשה ואם נאמר כפרישה בהלך לעסקיו אין תשלומין כתפלה בזמנה גם בחידושינו הערנו מתו׳ אבעיא עד ועד בכלל ועתה ראיתי שהפר״ח העיר בזה:

<b>However.</b> The fifth approach, as one who studies Derishah will see, is that throughout the day from midday until evening, even one who intentionally delayed receives reward for prayer outside its proper time. If one erred from six and one half hours onward and makes it up, he receives reward for prayer at its proper time, and for this reason it is called a makeup prayer. According to this, during the half hour after midday one who acted intentionally and one who erred are alike, and both receive reward for prayer outside its proper time. See there. The proof is that the Gemara did not answer that the statement applies only to one who erred. This shows that even one who acted intentionally may pray, and of him it states that throughout the entire day even an intentional offender receives reward for prayer outside its proper time. In practice, however, one should not rely on this, but should follow the Mechaber and Rama here, that after midday one may no longer pray Shacharis. Regarding an intentional omission once the time for Minchah has arrived, siman 108, seif 7 permits only a voluntary prayer, not an ordinary obligatory prayer. With Hashem's help this will be explained further there. Until midday, however, it appears that one is obligated to pray, as is also implied by Rambam's wording, “he has fulfilled the obligation of prayer.” The Mechaber altered the wording somewhat; understand this. Tur has two versions. According to one, “if he passed the time and did not pray, he should pray until midday,” which indicates an obligation. According to the other, “if he passed the time and prayed until midday,” it may indicate mere permission. You see that one may say there is an obligation to pray until midday, and he receives reward for prayer outside its proper time; once the time of another prayer has arrived, if he wishes he may pray voluntarily, but there is no obligation. During the half hour after midday he may not pray even if he erred, and a voluntary prayer is also inapplicable, since had he erred the law would still prohibit it. See Taz in siman 108, seif katan 5; with Hashem's help this will be explained further there. Derishah writes that there is no difference between the two textual versions, and the matter may be explained as stated. We may now conclude that there are three periods and four laws. From after four hours until midday he must pray, fulfills the obligation of prayer as Rambam writes, and receives reward for prayer outside its proper time. This is also reasonable, because we do not multiply the dispute. If the Sages certainly require him to pray, Rabbi Yehudah should likewise require it. I do not know why the Mechaber departed from Rambam's wording. Even if we combine the approaches, this remains difficult. During the half hour after midday, even one who erred may not pray. During the time for Minchah, one who erred makes up the prayer. According to the Mechaber he receives reward for prayer outside its proper time, while according to Derishah he receives reward for prayer at its proper time. A practical difference may arise if a Rabbinic mitzvah presents itself at the time for the makeup prayer, regarding which one takes precedence. Understand this. If the omission was intentional, he may pray voluntarily if he wishes, but he has no obligation. With Hashem's help this will be explained further in siman 108. I saw that Peri Chadash in siman 108, seif 3, explains Terumas HaDeshen in accordance with Taz here. It appears there that if one interrupted and went about his affairs, he may no longer pray. Rabbi Yerucham's statement according to Rabbi Yehudah, that after plag haMinchah one may pray throughout the entire day, is therefore difficult. If we say like Derishah that once he went about his affairs there is no makeup in the manner of a prayer in its proper time, the difficulty remains. In our novellae we also noted Tosafos' question whether “until” is inclusive. I have now seen that Peri Chadash raises this point.

<b>והנה</b> הפר״ח יש לו שיטה ששית וממש כדרך הא׳ של הב״י כרי״ף ורא״ש דוקא היכא דטעי וצלי הא מזיד לא ומיהו שוגג כולי יומא אף בח״ש והנה בח״ש יש לנו פסק המחבר והרב וליחוש לפסק הפר״ח באחר ד״ש עד חצות בהזיד יש תקנה שיתנה ויאמר אם הלכה כפר״ח יהיה נדבה ושפיר דמי ומהא אמינא לה מק״ח ס״ז וט״ז אות ה׳ כל ששגג מתפלל מזיד בתורת נדבה שפיר דמי וא״צ חידוש אף לדידן:

<b>Now.</b> Peri Chadash has a sixth approach, exactly like Beit Yosef's first approach and in accordance with Rif and Rosh. The permission applies only where one erred and prayed; an intentional omission is excluded. An inadvertent omission, however, may be remedied throughout the entire day, even during the half hour after midday. Regarding that half hour we already have the ruling of the Mechaber and Rama. To accommodate Peri Chadash's ruling in the case of an intentional delay after four hours but before midday, there is a remedy: one may stipulate that if the halachah follows Peri Chadash, the prayer should be voluntary. This is valid. I derive this from siman 108, seif 7, and Taz, seif katan 5: whenever one who erred may pray, one who acted intentionally may properly pray it as a voluntary prayer, and according to our practice no novel addition is required.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And if.</b> See Taz and Bach, who note that the Shulchan Aruch appears to contradict itself. Here it states that after reciting some of the blessings one may proceed, implying that one may even greet a person at his doorway with shalom. Levush writes this explicitly. If so, the Gemara's statement in Berachos 14a that one may not greet another with shalom before praying must mean that reciting the blessings is sufficient. Certainly, then, regarding attending to one's affairs, the ruling there should likewise be that the blessings suffice. Terumas HaDeshen, responsum 18, proves that “before he prays” means before the Amidah; otherwise, what is the Gemara's question from greeting between the sections of Krias Shema? If so, since in seif 2 the Mechaber did not accept this difficulty, why in seif 3 does he specify the Amidah? The answer is that bowing alone, without greeting with shalom, is permitted after one has recited the blessings, even at the person's doorway. Certainly, a greeting with shalom alone, which is more lenient than bowing, is permitted. It is difficult to say that after the blessings, shalom is more stringent than bowing alone, as Levush writes. A greeting with shalom accompanied by bowing, however, as was customary in their practice, remains forbidden even after the blessings until one has prayed the Amidah. The same applies to attending to one's affairs, which is comparable to greeting with shalom and bowing. Levush's words are therefore well explained. Eliyah Rabbah, seif katan 7, appears to explain that according to these authorities, the primary meaning of “shalom” is bowing alone. On the road this is forbidden before the blessings, and at the person's doorway bowing alone is forbidden even after the blessings. A greeting with shalom alone, however, is permitted after the blessings even at the doorway, because the Gemara's primary reference to shalom means bowing. According to this, the first opinion, which forbids a greeting with shalom alone when one arises early to go to the doorway, would forbid it even after the blessings, as may be inferred from the law between the sections of Krias Shema. The first opinion should therefore have been presented as the stringent view and the second as introducing a leniency. From Nachalas Tzvi, however, it appears that the permission after the blessings applies only on the road and refers only to bowing alone, also only on the road. At the person's doorway it remains forbidden after the blessings, and even a greeting with shalom alone is forbidden there after the blessings. According to this reading, the second opinion introduces no leniency either. See Magen Avraham, seif katan 5. The second opinion, which forbids a greeting with shalom on the road, is seemingly only a custom enacted so that one will direct his attention to prayer, rather than a strict law. See Peri Chadash and Levush. It may, however, be a strict law. These authorities maintain that greeting on the road is permitted after one has recited the blessings, for the passage concerning greeting between the sections is discussing such a case. The Gemara does not answer that greeting is forbidden only before the blessings because the phrase “before he prays” specifically means before the Amidah, as Terumas HaDeshen writes and as the wording “until he prays” implies. It therefore answers that the prohibition concerns going to the person's doorway. See Darkei Moshe in the second edition, and review the matter here.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Or.</b> See Taz. One who studies Beit Yosef will see that he too maintains that setting out on a journey is forbidden because it is included in attending to one's affairs, for there is no greater instance of attending to one's affairs than this. His difficulty is how, in seif 8, Rashba permits one to pray while traveling; see Tur. He answers that there the person is compelled, because the caravan will not wait for him, and for this reason he was permitted to leave beforehand. Taz, however, answers that from dawn the principal time for prayer has not yet arrived, since it begins at sunrise. Perhaps even after the eastern horizon becomes illuminated it is still not the principal time. He may therefore leave before praying, and this is not considered attending to his affairs. Such activity is forbidden only once the principal time has arrived, just as seif 2 rules concerning greeting. Magen Avraham, seif katan 6, disagrees. Since in a compelled situation one may pray from dawn, dawn is properly called the time for prayer in such a case. Rashba permitted the compelled traveler to leave and pray on the road. According to this, when one is not compelled, greeting and attending to one's affairs are forbidden from dawn. According to Taz, it appears that greeting is also permitted before sunrise. Yet what is the Gemara's question from the law that one may greet between the sections of Krias Shema, since the exceptionally scrupulous also recite Krias Shema before sunrise? One must say that before the eastern horizon becomes illuminated it is not called the time for prayer, but once it becomes illuminated, since after the fact one has fulfilled his obligation, it is properly called the time for prayer. This would necessarily mean that the time when one can recognize his fellow at a distance of four cubits is at least equivalent to illumination of the eastern horizon. See siman 58, seif 1, regarding this.
