**** ENGLISH ****
<b>As stated in Yoma at the beginning of the third chapter, although the wording is not entirely exact.</b> This is what is stated there: The officer said to them, “Go out and see whether the time for slaughtering the morning daily offering has arrived.” This accords with Rashi’s explanation that the observer ascended the wall or the roof. If the time had arrived, the observer would say, “The light has flashed,” meaning the beginning of dawn. Mattisyah ben Shmuel says, “The entire eastern horizon has become illuminated,” and the halachah follows Mattisyah ben Shmuel. Afterward the Mishnah teaches: Why was this necessary? Rashi explains, why was it necessary to ascend the roof and look? Because on one occasion the light of the moon rose, they mistook it for illumination of the east, slaughtered the daily offering, and had to take it to the place of burning. Magen Avraham understands that just as they had to ascend and inspect because of the concern for error, for that same reason Mattisyah ben Shmuel holds that the entire eastern horizon must be illuminated and that the mere rising of dawn is insufficient.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>(Seif katan 5) And afterward, etc.</b> “For in truth the time for Minchah, etc.” Meaning, the time for Minchah is from six and one half hours. Accordingly, if one transgressed and did not pray Shacharis before four hours, it should have been permissible to pray until six and one half hours. Bach indeed holds this way, and writes that the Shulchan Aruch’s statement “until midday” is not precise. Magen Avraham, however, holds that it is precise, because in truth the time for Minchah begins at midday. They merely added half an hour because of possible error and ruled not to pray Minchah until six and one half hours. In any event, from midday onward the time is no longer suited for Shacharis. Although Bach disputes Beit Yosef’s reasoning for prohibiting Shacharis immediately after midday, it is still prohibited after midday for another reason, namely that the time for Minchah begins immediately after midday. See Taz.

In their days the normal custom was to greet with “shalom,” so when one said “Good morning, master,” it constituted an alteration and thereby reminded him. Today, however, everyone normally says “Good morning, master” rather than greeting with “shalom,” so there is no alteration. It is therefore possible that according to Raavad, nowadays one should not say “Good morning, master.”
