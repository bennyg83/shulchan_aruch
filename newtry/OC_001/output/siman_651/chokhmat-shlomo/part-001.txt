<b>(There, se'if 5)</b> He should recite the blessing "on taking the lulav" and Shehecheyanu before he takes the esrog, etc. Note: see Magen Avraham subparagraph 12, who wrote that nevertheless one should not do this initially, for there are those who say mitzvos do not require intent, etc. From this it implies that he holds that lulav is comparable in this respect to all mitzvos, and according to those authorities who hold mitzvos do not require intent, lulav also does not require intent. In my opinion, at first glance one can distinguish between lulav and ordinary cases, based on what we read in chapter 5 of Yoma, 47b: Rav Pappa asked regarding what is between the fingers in a handful, etc., or perhaps the Merciful One said "and he shall take" and "and he shall bring," and this is not present. Rashi explained: perhaps it did not enter between the fingers, and he did not intend it, end quote. If so, it implies that the language "and he shall take" teaches that intent is required. If so, regarding lulav too it is written "and you shall take," and it is like there, "and he shall take," so without intent it does not help. If so, although the Gemara there remains with the question unresolved, this would again become a double doubt: perhaps with all mitzvos intent is required; and if you say that with all of them intent is not required, perhaps with lulav we require intent, since it is written "and you shall take." With a double doubt one is obligated to recite the blessing again, since a double doubt is like certainty. I reconsidered and saw that this is not so. For if so, with shechitah, where it is written "and you shall slaughter," we should say similarly that intent is required, yet we maintain that shechitah does not require intent. Perforce the intent of the Gemara in Yoma is as follows: with shechitah too we maintain that although we do not require intent for the sake of shechitah, intent for the act is required. But if he did not intend any act, it does not help, because the language "and you shall slaughter" concedes that at least intent for an act is required. This is also the case in that passage in Yoma. It does not say there in Yoma that intent for its own sake is required, for there is no proof for that from the word "and he shall take." Rather, there, regarding what is between the fingers, his intent is not even for taking, as Rashi wrote, that they entered by themselves. Therefore it properly says: or perhaps the Merciful One said "and he shall take" and "and he shall bring," and at least intent for taking is required. If so, with lulav too we require intent for taking, but intent for the mitzvah depends on the law whether mitzvos require intent. It emerges from this that if one took a lulav entirely without intent, for example he intended to take another item and took the lulav, or in another manner where he did not intend even an act of taking at all with the lulav and esrog, this depends on that question in Yoma. For us it is a double doubt and he is obligated to recite the blessing. But there is no proof from the Gemara for the words of the Magen Avraham, that lulav is comparable to other mitzvos and does not require intent, from the fact that it asks, "once he lifted it he fulfilled his obligation." For one may say that the question was only that, if so, let us resolve the question of Rav Pappa mentioned above in Yoma, that we require "and he shall take" and "and he shall bring." We find similar reasoning in several places. Therefore the Gemara rejects this, saying there is no proof from there, for one may say it is speaking of a case where he turned it upside down. But in truth, the law of lulav is like there, and it is doubtful like there, and for us it is a double doubt. However, we already wrote that there is no proof from Yoma, for there the intent is only that intent for taking is required, not intent for the mitzvah. Now at first glance this was difficult to me: according to this, why in Yoma does it remain unresolved whether intent for taking is required, while with shechitah it is plainly proven in the Gemara that if he did not intend for an act it is invalid? Why was it not doubtful there whether if it fell by itself it is invalid, while in Yoma he is uncertain? Therefore it appeared that in Yoma too it is obvious to him that "and he shall take" and "and he shall bring" implies that intent for taking and bringing is required. Only regarding what is between the fingers he is uncertain for another reason: since this is a common and usual thing, that it enters between the fingers, then even though he did not intend this, it is like he intended it, since it will certainly be so and he knows at the time of the act that it cannot be avoided. It resembles what we maintain regarding a psik reisha that will not die, where one is liable even though one who does not intend is exempt; perforce, in a psik reisha, one who does not intend is considered as one who intends. If so, here too, regarding what is between the fingers, the Gemara asks whether this is considered intent, whether the intent for the body of the taking also extends to what is between the fingers, or perhaps the intent for the taking does not extend to what is between the fingers, and since it is written "and he shall take" and "and he shall bring," it is invalid because he did not intend it. If so, according to this, with lulav, where "and you shall take" is also written, we also require intent for taking. Therefore, if he took it without any intent, where he did not intend for taking at all, he certainly has not fulfilled his obligation and must certainly recite the blessing, in a case where he did not intend for taking at all. Specifically intent for the mitzvah is not required, but intent for taking is required. However, if so, I have difficulty with the Gemara in chapter 3 of Rosh Hashanah, which says there that if Persians compelled someone and he ate matzah, he fulfilled his obligation, and it derives from there: learn from this that mitzvos do not require intent. This is difficult: what of it? Granted that intent for the mitzvah is not required, at least intent for eating should be required, since it is written "you shall eat matzos," and intent for eating should be required. How is this different from shechitah, where it is written "and you shall slaughter" and we require intent for an act in general, and likewise the Yoma case above, where it is written "and he shall take" and "and he shall bring," and we require intent for taking and bringing? If so, here too it is written "you shall eat," and if so intent for eating should at least be required. If they compelled him to eat, then under compulsion his intent is not considered intent, as is proven in the Gemara there. If so, there was not even intent for eating there, and why did he fulfill his obligation even according to the one who says intent is not required? At first glance this requires analysis. From this, however, there appeared to be proof for what I wrote in my book Sefer HaChayim, siman 589, that regarding matzah, even though it is under compulsion, the act is considered an act, since because of his compulsion and his enjoyment he resolves and consents. If so, it is fine: regarding the body of the act, he consented because of his enjoyment and compulsion, only to the mitzvah he did not consent; nevertheless, regarding this it helps that mitzvos do not require intent. This is correct; examine it. See what I wrote on this in my novellae to Orach Chayim, beginning Wednesday, parashas Eikev, year 5586, page 41a, in siman 639; see there and examine it. And regarding the wonder about the Rambam, that he did not copy the Gemara's answer that it is a case where he turned it upside down, see there in siman 639 what I settled about this with Hashem's help; examine it. Now regarding what is explained in the Shulchan Aruch, that he should recite the blessing "on taking the lulav" and Shehecheyanu before taking the esrog: see the language of Tosafos, which the Turei Zahav also brought. One must examine this, for their words contradict one another. From what they wrote, that it is not reasonable to recite the blessing while the lulav is still lying in the vessel, it is proven that if it were not in a vessel, even though it is not in his hand, it would still be relevant to recite the blessing over it. If so, why did they afterward write, and so too the Shulchan Aruch, that he should first take the lulav in his hand? Why is this necessary? Let it lie before him without a vessel, and that would also suffice. And if the fact that it lies before him does not help, and this too is too far removed, and therefore we require that he take it in his hand, why did they first write "and the lulav is lying in a vessel"? They should have written plainly that the lulav is not in his hand, or that the lulav is lying there. Why did they need to mention the vessel? Therefore their words require analysis; examine it. While I am on this topic I will mention what was reported to me regarding what the Magen Avraham wrote above in the name of the Mateh Moshe, that it is proper to take the esrog first and afterward the lulav. I was told that the Dagul Merevavah, brought in Shaar Ephraim on Orach Chayim, disagrees with this and wonders at the Magen Avraham from the words of Tosafos and the Shulchan Aruch here, and from the Magen Avraham himself, who wrote that one should take the lulav first. When I heard this I wondered, for certainly the words of the Magen Avraham and the Mateh Moshe are correct. Certainly, according to the practice of the Talmud and the early authorities, where they did not practice turning the esrog upside down but took it in its order, it is proper to do as Tosafos and the Shulchan Aruch wrote. Otherwise, either way there is a problem: if he also takes the lulav, he has already fulfilled his obligation and the blessing is not before the performance; and if he does not take the lulav, it is a great advance preparation and too far removed, since it is not yet in his hand. Therefore an arrangement is needed, to take the lulav first without the esrog. But the Mateh Moshe and the Magen Avraham are speaking according to what we now practice, to take the esrog upside down, with its stem upward. If so, even though the lulav is also in his hand, he still has not fulfilled his obligation, since the esrog is upside down. Therefore it is proper to take the esrog first, as a sign corresponding to the tefillin of the hand and of the head, and afterward the lulav. Nevertheless, it is also like the words of Tosafos and the Shulchan Aruch, since he holds the lulav in its proper manner and the esrog upside down, and after the blessing he turns the esrog to its law. This is exactly like taking the lulav first and afterward the esrog, for he does not hold it in its proper manner until after the blessing, while the lulav is already held in its proper manner beforehand. This is precisely like the words of Tosafos and the Shulchan Aruch, and the words of the Magen Avraham do not contradict one another. Therefore, certainly the custom of Yisrael is Torah, and one who fears Heaven satisfies both the words of Tosafos and the Shulchan Aruch and the words of the Mateh Moshe and the Magen Avraham. Leave Yisrael to their custom, and this is fine with Hashem's help; examine it. Regarding the reason why we recite the blessing specifically over the lulav more than all the species, see what I wrote about its reason in the derashah for Sukkos of this year, 5613, in my commentary on the Torah. Now it appears further that the shaking of the lulav resembles accepting the kingship of Heaven in Kerias Shema. In Kerias Shema, one coronates Him, may He be blessed, above and below and to the four directions of the world; and so too the shaking of the lulav is to the four directions and upward and downward, to accept His kingship as in Kerias Shema. The difference is only that in Kerias Shema we coronate Him above first and afterward below, and therefore the letter ches in Echad precedes the dalet, as the authorities wrote, for the ches indicates that He is One over seven heavens and the earth, and the dalet over the four directions. But with lulav it is the reverse, for first we shake to the four directions and afterward upward and downward, meaning that we begin with an awakening from below first and afterward above, for this is the essence of the matter. It is known that the lulav hints that Yisrael prevailed in judgment on Rosh Hashanah. And how did they prevail? As our Sages of blessed memory said in tractate Rosh Hashanah: coronate Me over you so that your remembrance should rise before Me for good. If so, through coronating Him, may He be blessed, below, and saying "King over all the earth," making Him King below first, therefore they merited in judgment. Since the lulav hints that they prevailed in judgment, we shake it first to the four directions and afterward upward and downward, to hint that through this, through accepting the kingship of Heaven, we merited in judgment. Since the main shakings are with the lulav, and through it it is recognizable that we merited in judgment and by what we merited, therefore we recite the blessing over it. With this our custom is supported, to shake first to the four directions and afterward upward and downward, unlike the custom of the Chassidim. This is clear in my humble opinion with Hashem's help; examine it.

<b>(There, se'if 7)</b> If he made a handle and placed the lulav in it, this is proper, etc. Note: see the Magen Avraham, who wrote that this means if the entire lulav is outside his hand, etc.; but if the lulav is in his hand it is invalid because of interposition. However, if he fixed it with nails, it is not an interposition. See Tosafos, Yoma 44b, s.v. "every day," who wrote in the name of the Yerushalmi that if he fixed nails into it, it is not an interposition; see there. If so, the same applies here with lulav. According to this, there is no need to press the explanation as the Magen Avraham wrote, that it is speaking where the entire lulav was outside his hand. Rather, it is speaking where the lulav was in his hand, only the handle was fixed with nails and was not an interposition; nevertheless, it still needs to be in a respectful manner. This is fine; examine it. Now regarding what is explained in the Shulchan Aruch, "and if he wrapped a cloth around it and took it, or wrapped it around his hand and took it, there are those who say that he has not fulfilled his obligation," etc., see the Magen Avraham, who wrote that he fulfilled his obligation because it is nullified to the hand. See what I wrote to challenge this in my work on Orach Chayim, year 5612, page 15, in the laws of Nesias Kapayim, siman 128; see there and examine it. <br><b>(There in that same se'if, in the gloss)</b> But by law there is no concern, since the entire hand is not covered by them, etc. Note: see the Magen Avraham, who wrote to settle the difficulty of the Bach, that what is wrapped on the hand is different because it is nullified to the hand. It appears that there is proof to his words from what the Rambam wrote in chapter 10, halachah 9, of Hilchos Klei HaMikdash: a kohen who was wounded on his finger may wrap reed-grass over it on Shabbos, or a cloth that is not three by three; and if he intended to draw blood it is forbidden, provided that the reed-grass or cloth should not interpose between his flesh and the vessel at the time of the service. The Raavad challenged him and wrote: his words contradict one another; he said that not in the place of garments, less than three does not interpose between the hand and the vessel, etc. The Kesef Mishneh wrote that the Rambam explains like Rashi, that "they interpose and they do not interpose" is speaking not at the time of the service, but only because of extra garments, etc. The Kapos Temarim brought this in this sugya and rejected his explanation. I will prove further, with Hashem's help, from the language of the Rambam himself that he does not hold like Rashi. For it is difficult: after he wrote that he wraps reed-grass over it on Shabbos, he should have said "even on Shabbos." Especially since the law of Shabbos has no connection here, and perforce what he mentioned here is because of what is relevant to the law of the Temple service; only incidentally he mentioned it as a novelty, that it is permitted even on Shabbos. If so, he should have written "even on Shabbos." Furthermore, in truth, this law is unnecessary here, for he already explained above that less than three does not interpose. If so, this law is unnecessary; why do we need both? And if it is because of Shabbos, he should have written it in Hilchos Shabbos, not here. And if his intent is to say that even initially it is permitted, and if his intent is to teach us that in any event it must not interpose between his flesh and the vessel, he should have written this above briefly and made it one law: that if it does not contain three, it is permitted even initially, provided that it not interpose between his flesh and the vessel. There is another difficulty: in the previous law the Rambam changed his wording three times. First he wrote: if he wrapped on his flesh a garment not in the place of the priestly garments, etc., if it was three by three, it interposes and invalidates. There he used both terms, "interposes" and "invalidates." Afterward he wrote: less than this does not interpose; he used only the language of interposition. Afterward he wrote: and if it was a small belt, since it is significant in itself, it invalidates, even though it is not three by three. Here he used only "invalidates" and did not use "interposes." Perforce, he does not explain like Rashi, but like the Raavad, that "interposes and does not interpose" means actual interposition between the hand and the vessel. Therefore he mentioned the novelty in each case: where it is three by three, it interposes between the hand and the vessel, and not only does it interpose, but it also invalidates because of extra garments. But where it does not contain three, he teaches that it is not even an interposition between the hand and the vessel, and we learn all the more so that it does not invalidate because of extra garments, for otherwise let him state it because of extra garments. Therefore he teaches this, that it does not interpose. But if it was a small belt, which is something significant, then he teaches the novelty that it even invalidates because of extra garments, and all the more so because of interposition. In particular, since once it is invalid because of extra garments there is no need for the issue of interposition. If so, the words of the Rambam explicitly prove like the Raavad. But if so, the difficulty of the Raavad remains: why did he write the opposite in the latter clause? From this there is proof to the words of the Magen Avraham, that where the whole hand is not covered, it is nullified to the hand. According to this, this applies specifically to something that is not needed by the hand; then it is considered as nonexistent, and is nullified as though it were not on the hand. But if the hand needs it for healing and he cannot do without it, and without it he would be unable to perform the service, then one cannot say that it is as though it is not there; rather, it is significant, and therefore it is not nullified to the hand. Thus, although this significance does not make it a garment so that there should be a disqualification of extra garments, since it is not significant in itself but only because of the hand, nevertheless one also cannot say that it is nonexistent and nullified. Since the hand needs it, it is not comparable to straps, for there the place of the mitzvah of tefillin is without the wrappings on the hand, and he can also take the lulav without the straps on his hand, so it is as though it is not there. But when he has a wound on his finger, he needs the reed-grass, and without it he could not perform the service; therefore it is not nullified to it, and therefore it is an interposition. Therefore the Rambam wrote it here. And therefore he wrote "he wraps reed-grass over it on Shabbos" and did not write "even on Shabbos," for, on the contrary, he did not mention Shabbos as a novelty that it is permitted to wrap reed-grass over it, since that has no relevance here. Rather, he mentioned Shabbos in the opposite direction, as a novelty of prohibition: that even on Shabbos we require that it not interpose between him and the vessel. One might have thought that on Shabbos, either way, since on Shabbos it is forbidden to intend for healing and it is only for guarding, one might have thought that then it is nullified to the hand and not considered needed by it. It would be like two contradictory sides: if it is needed by the hand it should be forbidden on Shabbos, and if it is not needed by the hand it should be permitted even if it interposes. Therefore he teaches that although it is permitted on Shabbos, nevertheless it interposes. Therefore, if he had written "even on Shabbos," it would have implied that he mentioned Shabbos as a novelty of permissibility. Therefore he teaches that this is not so; he mentioned Shabbos only because of the latter clause, as a novelty of prohibition, that even then it interposes between the hand and the vessel. Therefore the Rambam properly stated this law by itself. In my humble opinion, this is the truth in the words of the Rambam, with Hashem's help. There is proof for the Agudah and the Magen Avraham, unlike the Kapos Temarim, who wrote from here a proof to the opposite; examine it.

<b>(There, se'if 10)</b> He should go around toward the right when shaking: east, south, west, north. Note: I have a great wonder here. From all the authorities it seems that the rule "all turns that you turn should be only toward the right" applies even to what is not done by foot but by hand; and in the laws of Chanukah it also seems so. At first glance, with all due respect, an explicit sugya in Yoma 59a was hidden from all of them. It says there that if you say they hold that encircling means by foot, everyone agrees that we derive the inner altar from the outer altar, and here they disagree about this: one master holds that encircling is by hand, and one master holds that encircling is by foot, etc.; see there. From this it is explicitly clear that when it is by hand and not by foot, this law of going around toward the right does not apply. It is already known that the Rambam always rules like the "if you wish, say" answer. If so, it is proven that this reasoning is according to everyone, that where it is by hand we do not require going toward the right. The Talmud concludes there that the correct explanation is as we answered initially, that one master holds encircling is by hand and one master holds encircling is by foot. What it says afterward, "if you wish, say, they disagree about this," is only to say about what reasoning they disagree, whether one encircles by hand or by foot. But this remains there in the conclusion, that they disagree whether it is by hand or by foot; so it is proven to one who examines the sugya there. If so, it is proven in the conclusion that by hand we do not require going toward the right. How, then, do all of them hold regarding lulav and Chanukah, which are not done by foot but only by hand, that going toward the right is required? This requires much analysis now. At first glance, it occurred to me to answer this difficulty against the authorities, so that they not be so astonishing, and not to say, Heaven forbid, that an explicit sugya was hidden from them. One can say as follows: one who examines the Talmud there sees that it first wanted to say that the reason of the one who says he places first on the northeast corner is that since it is the corner he encountered, he should have done it first because one does not pass over mitzvos. Only because it is written, "and he shall go out to the altar," etc., therefore when he returns, he returns to that corner first. If so, it is proven from the Talmud that something for which one already became obligated, and which was proper to do there, is preferable to the advantage of turning to the right. The practical difference for lulav is that if one forgot to extend it to the east or to the south and extended it to the west, and immediately remembered, then he should return to the east or the south, even though he goes from west to south and east and it is not toward the right, since that is where he should have begun, only it was not possible because of some matter; if afterward it is possible, he returns to it. Likewise with Chanukah lights: if he forgot to light the rightmost candle, or it was not possible then to light it, he should return to it and not be concerned that it is not toward the right. If so, although the Talmud retracts and wants to say another reasoning regarding what they disagree about, whether encircling is by hand or by foot, the intent is also this: certainly, where there is no other advantage opposing it, even by hand one should incline to the right. What difference does it make that it is by hand? Whether he turns right or left, all will be done by hand; and since the right has an advantage to turn toward it, even by hand it is proper to do so. Where there is no opposing advantage, the right is preferable even by hand. But where there is an opposing advantage, as here, where he should have placed on the northwest corner first, only it was not possible, if the encircling is by foot, since the actual essence of right is in walking, that is preferable to the advantage of what should have been placed first, and he should turn to the right. But if it is by hand, since he is not truly uprooting the rightward turn, because he stands in one place, in this case it is preferable to do what should have been done there first, and therefore he turns to it. This is what the Talmud says: if you wish, say that one master holds encircling is by hand. That is, according to the first wording, the reason was only because what should have been placed there first is preferable, even if encircling is by foot. The practical difference for the altar would be that if it happened that he forgot and did not place on the corner where he should have placed first, he would turn to it first and not take account of turning right, but would return behind him. Afterward it says that if you wish, say: actually, turning to the right is preferable to the advantage of what should have been placed initially. Therefore, if encircling is by foot, the right is preferable. Thus, on the outer altar, if he forgot and did not place on the corner where he should have placed first, he should not turn back behind him, but only turn to the right. Here, however, they disagree whether it is encircling by hand, for since he stands in one place, his right is not so strong, and in this case we return to the reasoning that he should turn back to the place to which he should have placed first. But in general, where there is no opposing advantage, the right is preferable even by hand. So it appears to explain this sugya. What emerges from what I wrote as practical law is that if there is no other advantage opposing it, the right is always preferable, whether by foot or by hand. But where there is another advantage, for example where he should have done this first and forgot or was prevented and did the second first, then if the act is only by hand, he goes back to what should have been preceded, even though it is by way of the left; but if the circling is by walking with the feet, he turns to the right in his ordinary manner and does not turn back behind him. So it appears in my humble opinion now, and there is no more time. Perhaps Hashem will grant me merit another time to explain this further; examine it well.
