<b>Seif 1. </b>If he erred or was prevented, etc. Note: The authorities dispute whether forgetfulness is considered an unavoidable circumstance. See my Chochmas HaTorah, in the work Mishnas 5610 on Parashas Haazinu, where I brought support from the Yalkut that forgetfulness is considered unavoidable. See there carefully. See also the questions of the Taz and Magen Avraham, and my responsum on Yoreh Deah in Tuv Taam VaDaas, third edition, laws of treifos, siman 33, and in my work, responsum 78, where I incidentally resolved their difficulty. Regarding the ruling that one who erred or was prevented from praying Shacharis prays Minchah twice, it appears to me that the rule that compensation is ineffective if not made at the immediately following prayer applies only when he was obligated during the intervening prayer times. Since he could have compensated at the proper opportunity and did not do so, later compensation is ineffective. This may be because the obligation was fit to be fulfilled and was then rejected, and something that became fit and was rejected does not again become fit. The initial missed prayer, by contrast, was rejected from the outset because of forgetfulness or unavoidable circumstances, and such initial rejection is not considered permanent rejection. One may also explain that although there is generally no permanent rejection concerning mitzvos, there are instances of rejection, as I wrote in Sefer HaChaim, siman 167, in the laws of Bircas HaMazon, in the name of the Rif. More precisely, prayer stands in place of offerings, and with an offering, something that became fit and was then rejected remains rejected. Although we also rule that the Patriarchs instituted the prayers, that institution concerns prayer in its proper time. The possibility of compensation after its time derives from offerings, which have a period for compensation. Therefore, once the compensation itself became due and was not offered at the immediately following prayer, the law of rejection that applies to offerings prevents later compensation. In any event, the reason later compensation is ineffective is that he could have compensated and failed to do so. If he could not compensate, however, he may do so later. For example, if he forgot Minchah and then became an onen and was therefore forbidden to pray Maariv, Shacharis, or even Minchah until after burial, he was wholly exempt during the intervening period. This was initial rejection, not something that became fit and was then rejected, and he may compensate afterward. Similarly, if a relative died during the day and the authorities did not permit burial until the next day, and he omitted Minchah because he mistakenly thought he already had the law of an onen, he is considered to have erred in a matter he thought permitted and therefore was inadvertent. When night arrives, and burial will take place the following day, he is then an onen and may not pray Maariv or Shacharis. After burial, when he prays Minchah, he should pray twice, the second prayer compensating for the previous day's Minchah that he omitted by mistake. This is then considered the immediately following prayer, and in my view this is clear and correct in practice. I further propose a new ruling. When one did not pray Shacharis and must pray Minchah twice, this applies only after midday, when the time for Shacharis has passed. Before midday he should not wait for Minchah, but should pray Shacharis immediately. However, that applies on a day without Mussaf. On a day with Mussaf, if he already prayed Mussaf and then remembered that he had not prayed Shacharis, or that he omitted something requiring repetition, his Shacharis now has only the status of compensation, because Shacharis properly precedes Mussaf. Compensation is made only with the immediately following prayer, so he should wait until Minchah. The ruling cited by Beit Yosef from Rashba in siman 286, and by the Rama there, that one who prayed Mussaf before Shacharis fulfilled his Mussaf obligation, does not disprove this. It may concern only after-the-fact validity. Indeed, initially the Gemara requires Mussaf after Shacharis. It also addresses only whether he fulfilled Mussaf, teaching that Mussaf is valid even though Shacharis was not yet prayed. If he later prayed Shacharis, it too is valid after the fact. Initially, however, once he has prayed Mussaf, the primary time of Shacharis has effectively passed for him, and the prayer has the status of compensation, which should wait until Minchah. I am uncertain whether one who nevertheless offered compensation not together with the immediately following prayer fulfilled his obligation after the fact. Perhaps the enactment requiring the adjacent prayer was only initial, or perhaps even after the fact he did not fulfill it. There is no proof from the law that one who placed the compensatory prayer before the current obligation did not fulfill it, because that may be worse. There he prayed during the time of the current prayer and improperly placed compensation first. Where he prays at a time when no present obligation is being discharged, he may perhaps fulfill it after the fact. This requires study. Regarding the earlier ruling, however, he should initially wait until Minchah, though if he prayed Shacharis afterward he certainly fulfilled it. I was later shown that Eliyah Rabbah rules oppositely, but in my view he did not consider the rationale stated above, and he permitted it only during the time of Mussaf, not afterward. See there carefully. In Sefer HaChaim I ruled that if one prays Mussaf on the first day of Pesach and fails to cease saying Mashiv HaRuach, he must repeat the prayer even if he has already heard the chazzan omit the mention of rain. I have now seen that Peri Megadim, siman 126, writes that in such a case he need not repeat, because the duty to omit the phrase depends only upon having heard the chazzan. In my view this is incorrect. Once the Sages established the required text, a prayer not recited according to their enactment must be repeated. Proof may be drawn from Shemini Atzeres. One who says Mashiv HaRuach before hearing the chazzan announce it must certainly repeat. Shulchan Aruch, siman 114, rules that even one who is ill or prevented should not pray before the congregation, and Magen Avraham explains that one who is not ill may not precede the congregation in any event. A pressing circumstance is treated like an after-the-fact case. If one who prayed alone and mentioned rain before hearing the chazzan had fulfilled his obligation after the fact, an ill or otherwise prevented person should be permitted initially to do so. Prayer with the congregation is of great importance and, according to several authorities, even takes precedence over joining redemption to prayer. Yet an ill or prevented person may pray before the congregation only where doing so is valid after the fact. Since they did not permit him to mention rain before the chazzan, it follows that even after the fact he must repeat. Therefore, whether he mentioned rain on Shemini Atzeres before hearing the announcement, or failed to stop mentioning it at Mussaf on the first day of Pesach before hearing the chazzan omit it, he must repeat because he did not follow the enactment of the Sages. This is clear and correct, unlike Peri Megadim. The source for compensating Shacharis at Minchah appears in Berachos 26. The Gemara concludes with a difficulty from the baraisa concerning one who erred and omitted a required mention. The authorities write in the name of Rav Hai Gaon that because the Gemara did not conclude with a formal refutation, the ruling remains that where his conduct reveals that the first prayer was intended as compensation, he did not fulfill it. I find support for this from Berachos 4b, where the Gemara says that since the Sages instituted Hashkiveinu, it is considered an extension of the blessing of redemption. Otherwise, how could one join redemption to prayer at Shacharis when Rabbi Yochanan says that at the beginning he says, 'Hashem, open my lips,' and at the end, 'May the words of my mouth be acceptable'? On Berachos 9b the discussion appears reversed. There Rabbi Elazar says that Rabbi Yochanan's statement may refer only to Minchah, while another view says it applies to all prayers. The Gemara then proves from Hashkiveinu that the inserted verse is considered part of the extended prayer. The two passages appear inconsistent. On page 4, Hashkiveinu is proved from the rule of opening the Amidah; on page 9, the rule of opening the Amidah is proved from Hashkiveinu. Moreover, what proof is there on page 4, since Rabbi Yochanan's statement might concern only Minchah, as Rabbi Elazar explains on page 9? The solution lies in the fact that the Gemara quotes Rabbi Yochanan's conclusion, 'and at the end he says, May the words of my mouth be acceptable.' For the objection, the opening verse alone should have sufficed. The Gemara included the conclusion because the conclusion applies to every prayer, indicating that the opening likewise applies to every prayer. Rabbi Elazar, however, could answer that even the concluding verse applies specifically to Minchah, because the Gemara says one must be especially careful with Minchah, when Eliyahu was answered. At that prayer one is assured that his words will be accepted. Yet Rabbi Yochanan himself says that one is answered even at Maariv. This can be interpreted as referring not to the ordinary Maariv prayer, but to compensation for Minchah recited at Maariv. The concluding verse would then apply to Minchah and its compensation, but not to ordinary Maariv. This raises the rule that the subsidiary should not be more stringent than the principal. How can compensation for Minchah at night be more favored than the ordinary Maariv prayer? The answer depends on the issue in Berachos 26. If one who recites Havdalah only in the second prayer nevertheless fulfills both, we see that it does not matter that he treated the compensatory prayer as primary and the current prayer as secondary. It could then be said that Rabbi Yochanan's reference to Maariv means compensation for Minchah at Maariv. But if, as the accepted ruling following Rav Hai Gaon, Havdalah in the second but not the first reveals improper reversal and the first prayer does not count, then one may not make the subsidiary primary. Rabbi Yochanan must consequently refer to ordinary Maariv as well. Thus Rabbi Elazar, who limits the verse to Minchah, follows the rejected premise that reversing primary and subsidiary does not invalidate. The passage on page 4 follows the accepted ruling of Rav Hai Gaon, and therefore understands Rabbi Yochanan to address all prayers. It can then use the opening verse as proof concerning Hashkiveinu. This supports Rav Hai Gaon and the authorities who rule accordingly.

<b>Seif 8. </b>One who did not pray, etc. Note: This ruling demonstrates that forgetfulness is considered unavoidable. See the Magen Avraham's discussion. I find proof as follows. Bava Metzia 51b says that Shmuel can agree even with Rabbi Meir, because Rabbi Meir invalidates a condition against Torah law only where the condition certainly uproots the law, whereas in the case of onaah it is uncertain whether the law will be uprooted. This is difficult in light of the explicit Mishnah at the end of Peah, chapter 6: one who says, 'I am harvesting on condition that whatever I forget I may take,' is nevertheless subject to the law of shichechah. Rambam, Rash, and Bartenura explain that the condition is void because it contradicts Torah law. Yet it is uncertain whether he will forget anything at all, just as it is uncertain whether onaah will occur. One might answer that monetary law is lighter than prohibition. Rabbi Yehudah holds that a condition concerning money is valid. Rabbi Meir may agree that an uncertain uprooting is valid in monetary matters, while in nonmonetary law even uncertain uprooting invalidates the condition. But Gittin 84b discusses a condition that a woman eat meat and likewise concludes that a condition against Torah law is invalid only where it certainly uproots the law, such as food, clothing, and conjugal rights, whereas if she might not eat and therefore might not be divorced, the uprooting is uncertain. Shulchan Aruch, Even HaEzer, simanim 38 and 144, rules accordingly. Thus even in prohibitory law an uncertain uprooting does not invalidate. Why, then, is the condition in Peah invalid? We must distinguish that in the case of divorce it is within her power not to eat and thereby not be divorced, whereas forgetting is not within a person's control. If forgetfulness is truly unavoidable, the condition in Peah contradicts Torah law because he cannot ensure that he will not forget. But if forgetting is negligence, it must be within his ability to guard against it, for otherwise it could not be called negligence. It would then resemble the divorce condition and should be valid because the uprooting is uncertain. The Mishnah therefore proves that forgetting is not within his control and is considered a genuine unavoidable circumstance. This is, in my view, a clear proof.

<b>Seif 12. </b>One who mistakenly mentions an occasion from another day, etc. Note: During Chanukah I was asked about one who said Al HaNissim in the blessing of Avodah, before Vesechezena, and had already stepped back. I answered that he must repeat the Amidah. Both according to Shulchan Aruch and according to the Taz, their dispute concerns a mention whose substance belongs in that location, so that it is related to the blessing and is not inherently an interruption, but is inappropriate only because today is not the proper time. Shulchan Aruch holds that this is not an interruption, while the Taz disagrees. Where the inserted matter does not belong in that blessing, however, even if it is appropriate to the day, it interrupts the actual text of the prayer. Certainly, if he already stepped back, even ordinary speech would require repetition, as the Taz writes here and above in siman 104. It appears that even if he has not stepped back he must repeat. Indeed, if the mention were wholly irrelevant to the day, it would be no more than ordinary speech, and he could return to Retzei. Here, however, because it is appropriate to the day but was inserted in the wrong blessing, it is worse. This resembles the principle in Zevachim 2a that something of the same category can invalidate more severely than something of a different category. Therefore, mentioning it in the wrong place requires returning to the beginning in all cases. Since there is no conclusive proof, if he has not stepped back he should intend the repeated prayer as voluntary because of the doubt. If he has stepped back, he must certainly repeat, even if this occurred at Maariv, just as with any complete error, such as omitting Yaaleh Veyavo on Yom Tov or Chol HaMoed. See the Taz's disagreement with the Mechaber, and my novellae to the laws of Chanukah, siman 677, in my Orach Chaim work of 5604, where I brought strong proof for the Taz from the Gemara and explained how he rejects Rabbeinu Yonah's proof.
