**** ENGLISH ****
<b>(Seif katan 4) He should say, etc.</b> For otherwise, why would he need to interrupt with Ashrei? The reason for saying Ashrei is as written in siman 105, that when one prays two prayers one after the other, he must wait between them; and since he must wait, it is better that he say Ashrei during the time that he waits, so that he will stand in prayer from words of Torah. One cannot say that if he says Tachanun immediately after the first prayer he no longer needs to say Ashrei, since in any event he interrupted with Tachanun. Lechem Chamudos wrote that after Tachanun, etc., for Tachanun should be joined to the prayer to which it belongs, and one should not interrupt between the prayer and Tachanun, as written at the beginning of siman 131. The reason he wrote that one should interrupt with Ashrei and that an interruption of Tachanun does not suffice is that it is not a fixed matter, since there are many days when Tachanun is not said. According to this, it implies that on days when Tachanun is said, it suffices to interrupt with Tachanun, and he should say Ashrei, Lamnatzeiach, and Uva Letziyon after the second prayer, unlike the implication of the Eliyah Rabbah.

<b>(Seif katan 5) He should say, etc.</b> But the kabbalist, etc., held that one should not say Ashrei after Mincha. If so, according to this, the same applies when one erred and did not pray Shacharis and then prays Mincha twice: he should not say Ashrei after the Mincha prayer. However, the Bach wrote that the words of Rabbi Menachem Recanati refer to one who prays Maariv twice, because he holds that when one prays Mincha twice, even without the words of Rabbi Menachem, he need not say Ashrei between one prayer and the next. For he holds that the main reason for saying Ashrei before prayer is to stand in prayer from words of Torah and joy, but for the reason explained in siman 105, it suffices to wait the time it takes to walk four cubits. Therefore, when one prays Mincha twice, since he already said Ashrei before the first prayer, his recitation counts also for the second prayer; see there. This is unlike the Kol Bo, who holds that even when one prays Mincha twice, he should say Ashrei between one prayer and the next. See the Taz.

The proof of the Agudah, which is the opinion cited in Yoreh Deah, siman 232, that it is not called unavoidable, was brought by the Beit Yosef in Choshen Mishpat, siman 207. It is from what we say in Gittin 75: according to the law, one who sells a house among the walled cities has time to redeem it for one year from its sale; if the year passes and he did not redeem it, it remains permanently with the buyer and does not go out at Yovel. The Mishnah teaches that at first, on the last day of the year, the buyer would hide so that the seller could not return his money to him and redeem it, in order that it remain in his possession forever. Hillel the Elder enacted that the seller may deposit his money in the chamber and enter his house. Now, if such a case is called unavoidable, why was Hillel's enactment needed? Let the law itself be so, since on the last day he was prevented because the buyer hid himself; and since he came on the last day to beis din and wished to redeem it, and beis din recognized his unavoidable circumstance, even if the last day passed, beis din should be able to compel the buyer to accept his money and return his house. Rather, it must be that such a case is not called unavoidable, for he should have been concerned that perhaps an unavoidable circumstance would occur on the last day, and he should have redeemed it earlier. He stipulated to redeem it within three years, in a manner that is not an asmachta, such as by saying that if he does not redeem it within three years, it shall be acquired to him from now, or similar terms that remove asmachta, as explained there in Choshen Mishpat. If an unavoidable circumstance occurred, it is absolutely his; this is because through an unavoidable circumstance he is exempt, for the Merciful One exempts one who is prevented by circumstances beyond his control. This is like that case in siman 21 in Choshen Mishpat, where one obligates himself that if he does not swear within a certain time, his fellow will be believed without an oath, and he will have to pay what he would not otherwise have been obligated to pay. Since an unavoidable circumstance occurred, the Merciful One exempts him, and he should not be obligated. But to obligate his fellow in something that his fellow was not obligated to do, to obligate him now because of this unavoidable circumstance, we have not heard. If so, in this case where he lent him money on his field and stipulated that if he does not redeem it within three years it will belong to the lender from now, then when the time arrived and he did not redeem it, it belongs to the lender retroactively. Because an unavoidable circumstance occurred, you wish to obligate the lender to return the field that he acquired by law; this we do not say. The same applies in such a case with a house among walled cities: were it not for Hillel's enactment, according to the law the buyer would not need to return it. Even though the seller was prevented, one cannot obligate the buyer, who acquired the house according to the law, because of the seller's unavoidable circumstance. The Shach wrote similarly in Choshen Mishpat, siman 21; see there, for he further distinguished several legal distinctions. See Yoreh Deah, siman 232, where the Taz also rejected the proof of the Agudah.
