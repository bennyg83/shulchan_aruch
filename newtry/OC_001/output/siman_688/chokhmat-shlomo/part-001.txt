<b>Section 1.</b> "Walled cities surrounded by a wall from the days of Yehoshua bin Nun, etc." Note: The source of this law is in the Gemara of Megillah, in the passage "walled cities surrounded by a wall from the days of Yehoshua, etc.; from where are these words derived, etc.," until the end of the sugya. "And according to our Tanna, is it satisfactory? Since he has 'unwalled, unwalled,' why do I need 'province and province'?" See there. When I was involved in this, the Gemara was difficult for me: why does it ask, since he has "unwalled, unwalled," why do I need "province and province"? According to what is explicit in the Gemara below, and so we rule, that a doubtful walled city reads on both the fourteenth and the fifteenth, in Sotah Rabban Gamliel raised a contradiction: it is written "every pure person may eat meat," implying that a definitely pure person may eat, but one of doubtful status may not eat; and it is written "and every impure person, etc.," implying that one who is definitely impure may not eat, but one of doubtful status may eat. It answers: here it speaks of impurity of meat, etc. If so, it implies that wherever the verse specifies the matter, it speaks only of certainty and not of doubt. Therefore one can say that if only "unwalled" were written, I would say specifically one that is certainly unwalled reads on the fourteenth, but one that is doubtfully unwalled reads on the fifteenth. Especially since we derive by verbal analogy from "the unwalled cities, very many," and there the matter was not doubtful, for in the days of Moshe and Yehoshua themselves they saw whether it was surrounded by a wall or unwalled. In the days of Yehoshua himself no doubt applied, only certainly walled or certainly unwalled. Therefore, if we derived only "unwalled, unwalled," I would have thought specifically one that is unwalled reads on the fourteenth, but one that is doubtfully unwalled reads like a walled city on the fifteenth. Therefore "province and province" is written, to teach that a province that is certainly unwalled reads on the fourteenth, and a province that is certainly walled reads on the fifteenth, but one of doubtful status reads on both days. One cannot say that a doubtful one should not read at all, for are they not Jews? Furthermore, it is written "from Hodu to Kush," as the Gemara asks above. Therefore, perforce, we expound this way: one that is certainly unwalled reads only on the fourteenth, and one that is certainly walled reads only on the fifteenth, but one of doubtful status reads on both. This is unlike the case where "province and province" was not written and only "unwalled" was written, where I would say specifically one certainly unwalled reads on the fourteenth, but one doubtfully unwalled is judged like one certainly walled and reads only on the fifteenth. This requires analysis, and analyze carefully. See Taz, seif katan 1, regarding what he used to settle the words of the Ran in explaining the Gemara's challenge, "but Shushan, according to whom did it act?" See there. This is astonishing, for in the Gemara it is explicitly stated: not like unwalled towns and not like walled cities. I later saw in Peri Megadim in the name of Chemed Moshe that he sensed this. I am even more astonished than this: if the Taz's explanation is correct, the same question should be difficult according to the one who says from the days of Achashverosh, and in the Gemara it is explicit that it asks only according to the one who says walled from the days of Yehoshua bin Nun; see there and analyze carefully. See my work on Orach Chaim from the year 5601, in my responsum to the holy community of Rimlov, laws of Niddah, siman 131, where I discussed this with Hashem's help in explaining the sugya in the first chapter of Yevamos, where it asks from "the megillah is read on the fourteenth," read here "do not form factions," etc., and in explaining the sugya in the first chapter of Megillah; see there, with Hashem's help, and analyze carefully. In this siman I elaborated in my novellae in two places, in the sugya of "there is no difference between the first Adar and the second" and in the sugya of when it falls on Friday, "and who is it? It is Rebbe"; see there, and you will find satisfaction with Hashem's help. There, in that section: "provided that it was surrounded and afterward settled, etc." Note: This is the opinion of Maharam of Rottenburg that the Tur brought, but the early and later poskim require analysis: why did they not bring here the opinion of Tosafos, who explicitly disagree with this, in their comment under "a walled city that was settled and afterward surrounded"? They asked: why do I need the verse "that has a wall" to exclude Shur Iger, etc.? Let it follow because in such a case it was settled and afterward surrounded, etc. If the words of Maharam are correct, what is the difficulty? In any case, this was settled with intent to surround it, and this is considered surrounded and afterward settled. Perforce, Tosafos hold that it must be actually surrounded first, and not merely settled with intent to surround it. If so, why did they not bring the opinion of Tosafos, who disagree with this? This requires analysis. While I was involved in this, I saw that Pnei Yehoshua in his novellae asked: what practical difference is there in this statement, since we require surrounded by a wall from the days of Yehoshua bin Nun? If it was surrounded after Yehoshua bin Nun, in any case it does not help; and if his intent is that it was surrounded from the days of Yehoshua bin Nun, how can one find a case where it was first settled, since the Ran holds that the dwelling of gentiles is not called dwelling? See there. In my humble opinion it appears that the Ran's view, that gentile dwelling is not considered dwelling, applies only after Israel came to the Land and acquired it; then gentile dwelling is not dwelling. But before Israel came to the Land, when the land was theirs, gentile dwelling is considered dwelling. It is an explicit verse in the Torah several times that it calls them inhabitants of the land, as written in Parashas Mishpatim and Parashas Masei. Therefore, before Israel acquired it, gentile dwelling was dwelling; but once Israel acquired it, gentile dwelling was not dwelling. Therefore the Ran speaks regarding the Second Temple era, and one can properly say that gentile dwelling is not dwelling. But Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi speaks of the first sanctification from the days of Yehoshua bin Nun, and therefore he properly teaches that specifically surrounded and afterward settled; but if it was settled and afterward surrounded, even gentile dwelling is considered dwelling, and it is not considered surrounded by a wall from the days of Yehoshua bin Nun. This is simple and correct; analyze carefully.

<b>There, section 4.</b> "A walled city about which there is doubt whether it was surrounded in the days of Yehoshua or not, they read on the fourteenth and fifteenth, etc." Note: Mordechai wrote in the first chapter of Megillah: why do we not practice reading the megillah on the fourteenth and fifteenth because of doubt nowadays, etc., until: alternatively, there is here a violation of adding, for it is its time, or it may occur for those walled by a wall and he could discharge their obligation, etc. I was asked by one scholar: it is explicit in the Yerushalmi, brought in the poskim, that a resident of a walled city cannot discharge a resident of an unwalled town, and a resident of an unwalled town cannot discharge a resident of a walled city, because he is not obligated in the matter. How then did Mordechai write that he can discharge a resident of a walled city? I answered that this is not difficult at all. Specifically where the resident of an unwalled town is completely exempt, and likewise the resident of a walled city, there he cannot discharge another who is obligated in the matter. But where the resident of the unwalled town is also involved and obligated in the matter at least because of doubt, he is considered obligated in the matter and can discharge even the one who is certainly obligated. This is similar to what Kesef Mishneh wrote, brought in Even HaEzer, siman 44, regarding one who betroths a yevamah to the marketplace after the yavam will perform chalitzah for her: since kiddushin take effect in her now because of doubt, it is not considered something that has not yet come into the world; see there. So too here: since he is obligated because of doubt, he can discharge his fellow who is certainly obligated. Therefore Mordechai properly wrote that if we enacted concern for this doubt, he would automatically be obligated in the matter because of doubt, and then could discharge his fellow who is certainly obligated, and consequently he would violate "it shall not pass" or the prohibition of adding. This seems correct and simple in my humble opinion; analyze carefully. The Ran wrote in the name of the Geonim that they read only on the fourteenth, and he wrote two reasons. One is that we follow the majority of the world. The other is that in truth it would be fitting to say that a rabbinic doubt is treated leniently, except that if so they would be exempt entirely, and this is impossible; therefore they should read on the fourteenth, because it comes first, and a mitzvah that comes to your hand, do not let it become delayed, etc. What he wrote, that we follow the majority of the world, is difficult, for anything fixed is considered half and half, and here there are several known fixed places that are walled, and the fixed place is recognizable. Therefore the main reason is the second reason. Mishneh LaMelech in chapter 1 of Hilchos Megillah asked that the Ran's words contradict each other: here he wrote that the fourteenth alone is enough, while in the chapter Arvei Pesachim regarding reclining for the four cups he wrote that reclining is required for both. He answered that with the four cups, if we say that both do not require reclining, the Sages' enactment is entirely uprooted; therefore both need reclining. But with megillah, since the Sages' enactment is not entirely uprooted, the fourteenth is enough; see there. With this I saw how to settle Pesachim 54b, where it says that what was taught, "this one's doubt is forbidden and this one's doubt is permitted," means fixing the month. Rashi explained that this means making two days, and Tosafos asked that we say at the end of Rosh Hashanah that Yom Kippur is made only one day. What Tosafos answered, that the intent is one who travels in the desert, does not appear correct, for the language "fixing the month" does not imply this. Therefore it appears that the intent is as follows. If Rashi's statement, to make two days, is certainly astonishing from the Gemara mentioned above, nevertheless the Gemara's intent appears this way, with Hashem's help: one can say that specifically regarding megillah or reclining, since it is possible to be stringent and perform on two days, therefore, since the two sides contradict one another and it would have been possible to be stringent and perform on two days, although we do not make two days, they must perform at least one day. But where it is impossible to make two days in any manner, we again say that a rabbinic doubt is lenient, and it is entirely permitted. This is what the Gemara means, that the intent is fixing the month, or Yom Kippur: since it is a Torah doubt, one is obligated to observe at least one day, and this is called its doubt being forbidden at least for one day. With Tishah BeAv, its doubt is permitted on both days, since it is impossible to make two days, as with Yom Kippur, because of danger to life, as written at the end of the first chapter of Rosh Hashanah. Therefore it is a rabbinic doubt treated leniently, and it is entirely permitted. This is the meaning of "its doubt is permitted"; analyze carefully, and it works well with Hashem's help. See Tosafos there under "is it not that its twilight is permitted, etc.," who asked that Yom Kippur also has addition, for the addition of Yom Kippur is Torah-level, and their answer is forced; see there. In my humble opinion this will be a proof for the Rambam that a Torah doubt is lenient by Torah law. The later authorities wrote that where a prohibition was established, as with one piece from two pieces, the Rambam agrees that it is Torah-level. The law of twilight, which is a doubt whether it belongs to this day or that, is like one piece from two pieces. From this it will be proven that a rabbinic doubt where prohibition was established is permitted, for otherwise why do we say that on Tishah BeAv, its twilight is permitted because of rabbinic doubt, since prohibition was established? Perforce, even in this case a rabbinic doubt is lenient. In truth, according to this it is difficult for me: why does the Gemara challenge Shmuel and Rabbi Yochanan from that baraisa? Regarding whether a rabbinic doubt where a prohibition was established is stringent or lenient, Rabbi Meir and Rabbi Yose disagree in Eruvin, and see the later authorities in Yoreh Deah, siman 110, section 1. Perhaps that baraisa, which says its doubt is permitted, follows Rabbi Meir, who holds that in rabbinic matters, even where prohibition was established, it is lenient, while Shmuel and Rabbi Yochanan hold like Rabbi Yose, that a doubt is stringent where prohibition was established. This requires analysis. In any case, twilight is a doubt where prohibition was established. Therefore it works well: if Yom Kippur's doubt were permitted because even where prohibition was established the doubt is lenient, then although the addition of Yom Kippur is forbidden, nevertheless it would be permitted to make the twilight into the addition, since its doubt is lenient; if so, the twilight could be the addition. Therefore it teaches us that Yom Kippur's doubt is forbidden by law because a Torah doubt is stringent, since prohibition was established, and therefore one cannot make this period the addition, and the addition too is forbidden then. This works well with Hashem's help; analyze carefully.
