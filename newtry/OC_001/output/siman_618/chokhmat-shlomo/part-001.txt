<b>(שם סעיף ה') </b> אם החולה או' א"צ והרופא מסופק מאכילין אותו וכו'. נ"ב הנה דין זה צ"ע דמ"ש בש"ע לעיל באם החולה אומך א"צ ורופא או' צריך אין מאכילין אותו א"כ הרי לולא אמירת החולה הוי הדבר ספק לנו אם צריך לאכול או לא דהרי הוי חד לגבי חד והוי ספק השקול לנו אם צריך או לא ומכריע הכרעת החולה להך צד שאו' א"צ ומ"ש באם ברופא עצמו הוי ספק שהוא עצמו שקול בדעתו אם צריך או לא מ"ט לא יכריע דעת החולה להך צד שא"צ. ולפי הנראה גרע באם הרופא עצמו מסופק ושרופא אחד או' בבירור צריך ואין לו' דשאני ברישא שיש ע"א שאו' בבירור א"צ. ז"א דהרי כנגד זה יש ע"א שאו' בבירור צריך וא"כ מה לי בחד לגבי חד דהוי הדבר שקול ומה לי אם הרופא עצמו מסופק שאין לנו בירור לא להקל ולא להחמיר והוי כמו הדבר שקול ולמה בזה לא יועיל הכרעת החולה מ"ש מרישא וצ"ע גדול לדעתי. ובפרט דהמעיין בירושלמי שהביא הב"י שממנו מקור דין זה סיים שם בטעמו וז"ל חולה או' יכולני לצום ורופא אומר איני יודע רבי אבוה בשם ר' יוחנן נעשה ספק נפשות וספק נפשות להקל ע"ש. א"כ עיקר טעמו מכח דנעשה ספק נפשות והוי להקל והרי ברופא אומר צריך ורופא אומר א"צ נמי נעשה ספק נפשות אם לא היה החולה או' כלום ולמה בזה אין הולכין להקל. ולכך לבי או' לי שהירושלמי הוי חולק על הש"ס דילן וס"ל אף ברופא אומר צריך ורופא אומר א"צ מאכילין אותו וקמ"ל הירושלמי דאפילו אם ליכא שום ברי להקל רק הרופא עצמו מסופק נמי מאכילין אותו מכ"ש בחד לגבי חד והש"ס דילן דס"ל ברופא כנגד רופא מהני הכרעת החולה קמ"ל אפי' בכה"ג מהני הכרעת החולה להחמיר מכ"ש באם הרופא עצמו מסופק ונקט כל חד לרבותא. אבל הפוסקים שפסקו כתרווייהו צ"ע טובא. ולדינא הי' נראה כיון דהלכה בש"ס דידן נגד הירושלמי אין מאכילין אותו כה"ג. אבל מה כוחי לחלוק על הפוסקים ובפרט להחמיר בפקוח נפש ולכך הדבר צ"ע בעיני ודוק:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>(There, se'if 5)</b> If the sick person says he does not need to eat and the physician is uncertain, we feed him, etc. Note: This law requires analysis. What is different from what the Shulchan Aruch wrote above, that if the sick person says he does not need and a physician says he does need, we do not feed him? If so, were it not for the sick person's statement, the matter would be uncertain to us whether he needs to eat or not, since it is one against one, and it is an evenly balanced doubt whether he needs or not; the sick person's decision tips it to the side that he does not need. Why then, when the physician himself is uncertain, being equally balanced in his own mind whether the person needs or not, should the sick person's view not tip the matter to the side that he does not need? It seems that if the physician himself is uncertain this is worse than where one physician says clearly that he needs. One cannot say that the first case is different because there is one witness who clearly says he does not need, for opposite him there is one witness who clearly says he does need; if so, what difference is there between one against one, where the matter is balanced, and the physician himself being uncertain, where we have no clarity either to be lenient or stringent and it is like a balanced matter? Why in this case does the sick person's decision not help, unlike the first case? This is a great difficulty in my opinion. Especially because one who examines the Yerushalmi brought by the Beis Yosef, from which this law originates, sees that it concludes with the reason: a sick person says, 'I can fast,' and the physician says, 'I do not know'; Rabbi Abbahu in the name of Rabbi Yochanan says it becomes a doubt involving life, and in a doubt involving life we rule leniently. See there. If so, the main reason is that it becomes a doubt involving life and we are lenient. But where one physician says he needs and one says he does not need, it also becomes a doubt involving life if the sick person said nothing. Why there do we not follow leniency? Therefore my heart tells me that the Yerushalmi disagrees with our Talmud and holds that even where one physician says he needs and one says he does not need, we feed him. The Yerushalmi teaches that even if there is no definite statement to be lenient, only the physician himself is uncertain, we still feed him, and all the more so in one against one. Our Talmud, which holds that when one physician is against another the sick person's decision helps, teaches that even in such a case the sick person's decision helps to be stringent, all the more so where the physician himself is uncertain. Each source stated its own novelty. But the authorities who ruled like both require much analysis. In practice it would have seemed that since the halachah follows our Talmud against the Yerushalmi, in such a case we would not feed him. But what strength do I have to disagree with the authorities, especially to be stringent in saving life? Therefore the matter requires analysis in my eyes. Examine it carefully.

<b>(There, se'if 9)</b> One who was seized by bulmus, an illness that comes from hunger, etc.; if there is no permitted food there, we feed him forbidden food, etc. Note: If the sick person has neveilah meat and a living animal before him, whether we feed him the neveilah or slaughter the animal for him is disputed by the authorities; see the Ran and the Rosh, and I have written about this. It now appears to bring a proof for those who hold that we feed him neveilah and do not slaughter for him, from Yoma 64, where Rava explains a case as one who had a sick person in his house and they slaughtered, etc.; see my novellae to Yoreh De'ah, siman 14, where in the copied omissions I rejected this proof, and see Rashi there. It is difficult: what forced Rava to explain that they had already slaughtered? According to that, it would follow that the Torah's teaching that being underage in time invalidates even the scapegoat applies even after the fact, even where he already performed the lottery; if so, he would need to take two others and perform the lottery again. Without this, we would not find a case of being underage in time for the verse to reveal, since otherwise it is not fit for Hashem. One could have said that the verse reveals only an initial prohibition, similar to Temurah 6, where 'it shall not be accepted' was needed for a blemished animal because one might have thought he only violates a commandment but the meat is valid; see there. Regarding ordinary offerings that are underage in time, it is clear that they are invalid even if already sanctified, from 'from the eighth day onward it shall be accepted as a fire-offering,' which implies that before then it is not accepted. But regarding the scapegoat, where by reasoning being underage in time would not invalidate it and it is included only from the word 'to Hashem,' one could say it is included only initially, not to sanctify it, but if it was already sanctified it is valid. The practical case would be that after the lottery he had a sick person in his house, and he had the mother of the goat to slaughter, and prepared neveilah. If being underage in time would not invalidate the scapegoat, it would be better to slaughter for the sick person than to feed him neveilah, according to those authorities. But if the verse revealed that being underage in time invalidates the scapegoat initially, it is better to feed him neveilah, for then the scapegoat will be offered properly; if he slaughters, the scapegoat becomes underage in time and initially forbidden. Moreover, the main reason to slaughter for him is that if we feed him neveilah he violates many negative prohibitions for each kezayis. Here, even if he slaughters, he violates the prohibition of slaughtering an animal and its offspring, and the one who sends the scapegoat will violate the same prohibition; thus there will also be two prohibitions, so it is better here to give him neveilah and not slaughter. If so, one could say that the revelation is only initially, but after the fact, if they already slaughtered the mother, the goat is not invalidated. Also, according to this, one could find the case even before the lottery: he has a sick person in his house and needs either to slaughter the mother or give him neveilah. If not for the verse including the scapegoat, that its pushing from the cliff is its slaughter, one would think that pushing from the cliff is not slaughter. If so, it would be fit for Hashem, for if the lot for Hashem fell on it, he would indeed not slaughter the mother and thereby make the goat for Hashem invalid as underage in time; therefore they would feed the sick person neveilah. But if the lot for Azazel fell on it, one might have thought it would then be better to slaughter for him so he not violate neveilah on every kezayis, and the goat would not be invalid, for there would be no prohibition of slaughtering an animal and its offspring. Therefore the verse 'to Hashem' reveals that its pushing from the cliff is also its slaughter; if he slaughters the mother, the scapegoat would initially be invalid by the prohibition of mother and offspring, and therefore we feed him neveilos. Since Rava did not answer this way, it proves that in every case, if there is neveilah, we give him neveilah and do not slaughter for him. This proof applies only according to those who explain that we slaughter for him because neveilah is more severe, since he violates with each kezayis. In such a case, where the scapegoat will be invalidated and there will also be a prohibition of mother and offspring, one can say it is better to give him neveilah. But according to the Rambam, who wrote that the reason is that a person's soul recoils from neveilah, this proof does not apply; since saving life overrides everything, what does it matter that the goat will be invalidated? In the end we slaughter for him because of saving life, since his soul recoils from neveilah. So there is no proof from here; examine carefully. Perhaps even according to the Ran's reason there is no proof, for although it cannot be rejected that neveilah has many prohibitions while slaughter has only two, there could be a practical difference where the sick person needed only two kezaysim. With neveilah he would violate two prohibitions, and with slaughter, were it not for the goat, there would be one prohibition; but if the goat is involved, two prohibitions too would be set aside, so they give him neveilah. This may be rejected, because being underage in time itself is proven invalid even after the fact from 'from the eighth day onward it shall be accepted,' which proves that before then it is not accepted. Since in that same verse the word 'to Hashem' is adjacent to include the scapegoat, it follows that even regarding it, it is not accepted after the fact. If so, why lengthen the discussion with a case where he also has neveilah? Therefore Rava stated briefly that they had already slaughtered the mother on Yom Kippur. Examine carefully. Do not ask, according to those who hold that we feed him neveilah, as I proved above like them, why they slaughtered and did not call a non-Jew to kill it without shechitah. One can say the case is where there is no non-Jew; and even if there is a non-Jew, since he is not immediately present, one need not search for him in a case of saving life. They said their rule only where the non-Jew is immediately available and there is no more delay in this than in that. But our question stands well, for we could say the practical difference is where the non-Jew is immediately present. Examine carefully. Regarding the law of one seized by bulmus, see what I answered to the holy community of Kolkov, to the sharp scholar Rabbi Tzvi Hirsch Pineles, in the sugya of feeding the lighter prohibition first, and see my omissions to tractate Niddah, page 46, in the sugya of an exceptionally wise minor near adulthood, where I wrote in passing what pertains to these matters, and there I answered incidentally this matter as well. I was asked to explain the language of the Tosefta, Yoma chapter 8: One seized by bulmus is fed the lighter prohibition first. How? If tevel and shevi'is are before him, we feed him shevi'is; tevel and neveilah, we feed him neveilah; neveilah and terumah, we feed him terumah; terumah and shevi'is, we feed him shevi'is. End quote. This is astonishing: why with tevel and neveilah is neveilah lighter, while with terumah and neveilah, terumah is lighter? I have written about this. Now it appears as follows, and from this there will be a proof to what is written in Sefer HaChayim, siman 328, in the name of Tosafos Yom Tov, that retroactive separation is effective. According to this, one may say that terumah is lighter than neveilah on one side, because terumah can be permitted later through annulment of the designation; although it will then become tevel, he can later separate from another place and it will take effect also on what he already ate retroactively. But neveilah has no permit, and therefore neveilah is more severe than terumah. Thus the Tosefta teaches a novelty in each case. In the first case, where there is no other tevel besides this, not only terumah but even tevel is more severe than neveilah, and we feed him neveilah. Afterward it teaches that if one can separate from elsewhere besides what he needs to eat, then not only tevel but even terumah we feed him rather than neveilah, since this can be repaired and that cannot. Each case teaches a novelty: sometimes even tevel is more severe than neveilah, and certainly terumah, so we feed neveilah and not tevel; and sometimes even neveilah is more severe than terumah, and certainly than tevel, if he can separate from elsewhere. This is sound; examine carefully. See my Orach Chayim novellae, year 5607, page 112b, which further relates to explaining this Tosefta. The Mishneh LaMelech asked in the sugya of Yoma chapter 8 regarding tevel and terumah, and the above rabbi asked why the terumah should not be nullified in a majority, etc. In my humble opinion, there is a new point: since we say in Bechoros regarding impurity by carrying that it is considered present when together, necessarily the reason is that with eating, where each item is eaten separately, we can say that what he ate was the permitted one; but with carrying, where it is all together, nullification does not apply. The same appears regarding eating: specifically for a healthy person, where one can say he will eat this and no more, nullification applies, for we can say each one he ate was the permitted item. But with a sick person, if they assessed that he must eat all this and otherwise he will die, from the beginning it is known that he must eat all of it; it is as though he ate it all together, and nullification does not apply. Although Tosafos there wrote that it is only rabbinic, one can say that specifically there, since for eating it is nullified, for carrying too it is nullified from the Torah through a miggo, because it took effect for eating and is considered absent; therefore it helps for carrying as well, and only rabbinically it is not nullified. But here, where for eating it is considered together and nullification does not apply at all, certainly from the Torah it is not nullified. Therefore even if he does not eat it all together, since it is known that he must eat all of it, it is as though he ate it together and nullification does not apply. With this one can resolve what the Chok Yaakov brought in Hilchos Pesach, the Beis Yosef's question that the authorities contradict one another: we rule that prohibitions nullify one another, yet we practice like Hillel to wrap matzah and maror together, for he holds that mitzvos do not nullify one another; see there, and see what the Chok Yaakov wrote. According to the above it is also sound. The Gemara in Menachos says they disagree with Rabbi Eliezer because it follows the view that impurity by carrying is also nullified; therefore although according to Hillel one must eat them together, they should have nullified one another, and we must say mitzvos do not nullify one another. For the same reason it would be proper to say that prohibitions also do not nullify one another. But for us, who rule that impurity by carrying is considered present, although prohibitions nullify one another and mitzvos nullify one another, Hillel's law is sound, because since one must eat them together, nullification does not apply from the Torah. This is correct, with Hashem's help; examine carefully. Regarding feeding the lighter prohibition first, if he has neveilah and shevi'is before him, which do we feed him? See Kapos Temarim, where this is a dispute among the early authorities. See my responsa in the laws of esrog, siman 648, and in siman 11 in Hilchos Tzitzis, and in my work on Orach Chayim from the year 5614, where I wrote to resolve the view of the Rambam and Ramban, who hold that a positive commandment is more severe than a negative commandment, and I brought several proofs for their words from the Talmud; see there. Now I found in the introduction to Tikkunei HaZohar language relevant to this matter, saying that the sheva, which is fear in the Name Havayah, precedes love, which is kamatz; sheva is from the side of gevurah, while kamatz is on the right and rises in holiness and does not descend. Therefore the 365 negative commandments, which are fear, precede the 248 positive commandments, which are mercy: 'this is My Name forever' with yud-hei equals 365, and 'My remembrance' with vav-hei equals 248. This explicitly says that a positive commandment is more severe than a negative commandment, for negative commandments are from fear and positive commandments from love; therefore they are mentioned last, because one rises in holiness. It proves that a positive commandment is more severe than a negative commandment. Finally, regarding the law of feeding the lighter prohibition first, there is room to doubt if he has before him a kezayis of neveilah, which is neveilah by Torah law, and a kezayis of chelev that is only rabbinic chelev: which is considered more severe? Is neveilah more severe because it is Torah-level, or is chelev more severe because the name chelev is upon it and its ordinary punishment is kares? It seems to resolve this from Sukkah 16b, where the Gemara says: This is not so; Rabbi Yehudah does not hold like Rabbi Yosei, and Rabbi Yosei does not hold like Rabbi Yehudah, etc. He said only there regarding sukkah, which is a positive commandment, but not Shabbos, which involves stoning, etc.; see Rashi there. This explicitly proves that a rabbinic prohibition rooted in a Torah matter of stoning is more severe than an actual Torah positive commandment. If so, the same applies that it is more severe than an ordinary negative prohibition where there is no stoning. All the more so according to those who hold that a positive commandment is more severe than a negative commandment: if a positive commandment is lighter than a rabbinic prohibition rooted in stoning, certainly a negative commandment is. And even according to those who hold that a negative commandment is more severe, it is at least proven that where there is a positive prohibition before us and a rabbinic prohibition rooted in stoning, the rabbinic prohibition is more severe than the positive commandment. Since the anonymous Gemara there says 'This is not so,' it implies this is primary. This is a new law in my humble opinion.
