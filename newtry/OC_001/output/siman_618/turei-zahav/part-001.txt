<b>וה"ה אם החולה ורופא א' כו'. </b> כ"כ הטור ורבים מקשים ל"ל האי א' שעמו הא קי"ל אפי' הוא עצמו שאומ' צריך מהני אפי' כנגד מאה ותי' ב"י בשם מהר"י דה"א דגרע טפי אם אחר עמו וכמ"ש כאן רמ"א דלא חיישינן דהחול' אומר צריך משום דמאמין לרופא וק"ל ממה דפרכינן בגמר' תנן חולה מאכילין אותו ע"פ בקיאין ע"פ בקיאין אין ע"פ עצמו לא ע"פ בקיאין אין ע"פ בקי א' לא ומזה פרכינן על מ"ש שם דמאכילין ע"פ עצמו או ע"פ רופא שנים ע"כ צריך כאן א' עמו דאז נחשב כאלו היו כאן שני רופאים ונאמן אפי' נגד ק' כנ"ל בישוב דברי הטור:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And the same applies if the sick person and one physician, etc.</b> So wrote the Tur. Many ask: why do we need this one who is with him, since we rule that even the sick person himself, when he says he needs, is effective even against one hundred? The Beis Yosef answered in the name of Mahari Abohav that one might have thought it is worse if another is with him, as the Rema wrote here, that we are not concerned that the sick person says he needs because he believes the physician. This is difficult for me from the Gemara's question. We learned: A sick person is fed on the basis of experts. On the basis of experts, yes, but on his own basis, no? On the basis of experts, yes, but on the basis of one expert, no? From this it challenges what was said there, that we feed on his own basis or on the basis of one physician. It answers: Here what are we dealing with? Where he says, 'I do not need.' Then let them feed him on the basis of one expert? He does not need, for there is another with him who says he does not need. It asks: This is obvious, for where there are experts who say to feed him, it is danger to life and in a doubt involving life we rule leniently. It answers: It is needed where there are two others with him who say he does not need. Although Rav Safra said that two are like one hundred and one hundred are like two, that applies to testimony; but regarding assessment we follow the majority of opinions. This applies to assessment in monetary matters; here, however, it is danger to life, and we do not follow the majority of opinions. Since there are two who say he needs, even if one hundred say he does not need, it is a doubt involving life. It then asks: But from the fact that the latter clause teaches, 'If there are no experts there, they feed him on his own basis,' the first clause must refer to where they say he needs; the text is deficient and should read: In what case is this said? Where he says, 'I do not need,' then we require two experts. But if he says, 'I need,' then if there are not two experts who say he does not need, but only one, they feed him on his own basis. This is difficult: why does the Talmud force itself to establish the Mishnah as deficient? Why not answer that we follow the majority of opinions, and both in the first clause and the latter clause the sick person says, 'I need'; but in the first clause, where there are experts with him, he is not counted at all because he believes the words of the two experts, and it is as though he is absent, and the permission is based on the experts. The practical difference would be that if the majority of opinions prohibit, we follow the majority and do not join the sick person's opinion with those who permit. Then the latter clause would properly conclude that if there are no experts, but he himself says he needs, he is believed. If there were room for this reasoning, that he believes the experts, the baraisa would be well resolved. Even if you say that this reasoning is not enough to establish it as certainly so, in any case we could say that regarding one other with him we do not apply this reasoning, but regarding two experts we certainly could say he believes two physicians and is drawn after them. It seems to me to answer the words of the Tur as follows: the sick person's statement 'I need' is effective only after he asks to eat, as the Tur wrote nearby, for then it is certainly clear to him regarding his illness, and he is believed even against one hundred. But if he asks nothing, and only when they ask him he says, 'I need,' this is not so effective; it is as though another physician says this, meaning he says it by his own assessment. Then he is not believed more than one physician and is not believed against two. Therefore here one person with him is required, so that it is considered as though there are two physicians here, and he is believed even against one hundred. So it seems to me in resolving the words of the Tur.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And the sick person says, 'I do not know,' etc.</b> For the sick person's 'I do not know' is nothing, since how would he know? Most sick people do not know and are not expert in their illness. So wrote the Beis Yosef in the name of Ramban. It appears from this that if the sick person himself understands and is a physician, his 'I do not know' is automatically like another physician's statement, as it seems to me.

<b>מאכילין אותם מעט כו'. </b> במהרי"ל בשם מהר"ש מאיסטרייך וז"א שאין נותנין לו השיעור בבת א' אבל לא נהגי' הכי ואמרי' ספק נפשות להקל ונותנים לפניו וא"ל י"כ היום ואם יאכל הרשות בידו ע"כ ונ"ל דצ"ל לו אם יודע אתה שיש לך סכנה אם לא תאכל כשיעור ואם לאו תאכל תחלה פחות מכשיעור דאם לא יאמר לו אלא יום כיפור היום יסבור שמן הדין אסור לו לאכול ויביא עצמו לידי סכנה:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>We feed them little, etc.</b> In Maharil in the name of Maharshal of Austria, it is said that they do not give him the full measure at once, but we do not practice this way; rather, we say that in a doubt involving life we rule leniently, place food before him, and say to him, 'Today is Yom Kippur; if he eats, permission is in his hand.' It seems to me that one must say to him: if you know that there is danger to you if you do not eat the measure, then eat it; and if not, first eat less than the measure. For if one says to him only, 'Today is Yom Kippur,' he will think that according to the law it is forbidden for him to eat, and he will bring himself into danger.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>That he says Ya'aleh VeYavo, etc.</b> So wrote the Rosh, for since he ate permissibly, Yom Kippur is for him like any other Yom Tov for us. End quote in the Beis Yosef. This is astonishing to me from what is stated in Bameh Madlikin, page 24: the halachah is that when Yom Kippur falls on Shabbos, one who prays Ne'ilah must mention Shabbos, because it is a day that obligated four prayers. The Gemara challenges from Rava's statement that when Yom Tov falls on Shabbos, the shaliach tzibbur who descends before the ark at Ma'ariv need not mention Yom Tov, because were it not Shabbos, the shaliach tzibbur would not descend before the ark at Ma'ariv on Yom Tov. It answers: there, according to the law, even on Shabbos he would not be required, and the Sages instituted it because of danger; but here, it is the day that obligated four prayers. Thus we see that only something fixed for this day is mentioned as belonging to the day, like Ne'ilah, where the day causes the obligation to say that prayer, just like the other prayers. Therefore all the prayers are equal. This is not so with the Ma'ariv repetition, where the day does not cause the obligation but only the rabbinic enactment because of danger; it is not comparable to the other prayers that are obligations of this day. If so, all the more so here he should not mention the day in Birkas HaMazon, for there at least there is an obligation from the day to say Ma'ariv by enactment, yet he does not mention it because it is not a full obligation of this day like the other prayers. All the more so with this person's Birkas HaMazon, where the day does not cause it. On the contrary, the order of the day is not to recite Birkas HaMazon, because there is no eating; only the incident of compulsion that happened to him brings him to Birkas HaMazon. How can we say here that the day causes it? If you say that the day causes it if he comes to such a case, then regarding Ma'ariv too let us say so, even though there is no full obligation upon him. Rather, certainly this is not so. This is unlike other Yamim Tovim, where he has an obligation from the day to eat and it is forbidden to be without eating. Here it is the opposite; the compulsion causes the eating, and what place does the sanctity of Yom Kippur have here? Another proof is from siman 284, regarding Rosh Chodesh that falls on Shabbos: we do not mention Rosh Chodesh in the haftarah, since there is no reading from the Prophets on Rosh Chodesh. All the more so here, where there is no Birkas HaMazon on Yom Kippur. It seems to me that sitting and refraining from action is preferable, not to mention Ya'aleh VeYavo in this case. Birkas HaMazon itself has a dispute whether he recites it over prohibited food in a case of danger, as mentioned in siman 196; see our words there. Let us not add to it by saying Ya'aleh VeYavo and mentioning Yom Kippur in it. Regarding Tishah B'Av too it appears so.
