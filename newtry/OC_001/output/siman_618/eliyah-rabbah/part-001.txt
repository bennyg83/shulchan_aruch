[ז] <b>[לבוש] והשנים אומרים צריך וכו'.</b> משמע דאם רק אחד אומר צריך, סמכינן על הבקי דאין צריך והוא הדין בשנים נגד שנים או שלוש נגד שלוש, וכן מבואר מדהשמיט הגה"ה רמ"א סעיף ב' עיין שם ודו"ק, וכן הב"ח חולק על רמ"א שפסק כטור דכל הפוסקים חולקים עליו. וגדולה מזו אני אומר דרמ"א וב"ח שהבינו דמה שכתב הטור אלא כדפירשתי קאי על מה שכתב בתחילה הדינים בסתם ורצה לומר שאין לחלק כלל בין מומחה. ולעניות דעתי לא דקדקו דהטור קאי על מה שכתב ואינו נראה לרמב"ן וכו' ומכל מקום במופלג וכו', ואם כן דעת הטור ככל הפוסקים ודו"ק. גם נראה לי ראיה לזה מאגור סימן תתק"מ ושלטי גיבורים דף שכ"ד שלא הביאו תחילת דברי הטור כלל אלא איכא מאן דאמר וכו' ואינו נראה לרמב"ן וכו' אלא כדפירשתי, עד כאן. ואי קאי הטור על ריש דבריו ודאי היה להם להביאו אלא כדאמרן גם הרא"ש אפשר דמודה לזה, וראיה מרבינו ירוחם שמיקל מאד כל שמצא דעת המיקל משום סכנת נפשות ובדין זה פסק בסתם כרמב"ן, ולא הביא דעת הרא"ש רבו. לכן נראה לי להלכה למעשה לסמוך על הבקי כשהן שוין אף שאומר אין צריך, מיהו אם שלושה אומרים שצריך אף ששנים אומרים שאינו צריך יש להקל ולילך אחר רוב דיעות. ובזה יש לי תימא גדולה על שיירי כנסת הגדולה שכתב על הרמב"ן באחד נגד שנים שכן כתב רמ"א אבל הב"ח כתב וכו', עד כאן, הא גם הב"ח סבירא ליה בזה כרמב"ן, אלא דרמ"א חולק על רמב"ן כשהם שוין והב"ח מסכים לרמב"ן, וקיצרתי כיון שכבר העליתי לדינא:
**** ENGLISH ****
[7] <b>[Levush:] And the two say he needs, etc.</b> This implies that if only one says he needs, we rely on the expert who says he does not need; and the same applies with two against two or three against three. This is also clear from the fact that the Rema's gloss in se'if 2 omitted this; see there and examine carefully. The Bach likewise disagrees with the Rema, who ruled like the Tur, for all the authorities disagree with him. I say even more than this: the Rema and Bach understood that what the Tur wrote, 'rather, as I explained,' refers to what he wrote initially, the laws in general, meaning that there is no distinction at all regarding an expert. In my humble opinion, they were not precise, for the Tur refers to what he wrote, 'and it does not appear so to Ramban,' etc., 'nevertheless, with one who is outstanding,' etc. If so, the Tur's view is like all the authorities; examine carefully. I also see a proof for this from the Agur, siman 940, and Shiltei Gibborim, page 324, who did not bring the beginning of the Tur's words at all, but only, 'there is one who says,' etc., 'and it does not appear so to Ramban,' etc., 'rather, as I explained.' End quote. If the Tur referred to the beginning of his words, they certainly should have brought it. Rather, it is as we said. It is also possible that the Rosh agrees to this. A proof is from Rabbeinu Yerucham, who is very lenient whenever he finds a lenient view because of danger to life, yet in this law he ruled plainly like Ramban and did not bring the view of the Rosh, his teacher. Therefore it seems to me in practical halachah that when they are equal, one may rely on the expert, even if he says the person does not need to eat. However, if three say he needs to eat, even though two say he does not need to eat, one should be lenient and follow the majority of opinions. In this I have great astonishment at the Sheyarei Kenesses HaGedolah, who wrote regarding the Ramban in the case of one against two that so wrote the Rema, but the Bach wrote, etc. End quote. But the Bach too holds like Ramban in this case; rather, the Rema disagrees with Ramban when they are equal, and the Bach agrees with Ramban. I have shortened, since I have already established the practical law.

**** ENGLISH ****
[8] <b>If the sick person and the physician, etc.</b> Although in se'if 1 we rely even on the sick person alone against one hundred physicians, nevertheless here, where one physician supports him, one might have thought this is worse, as he concludes, because he believes the physician. The Taz wrote at length to challenge from the Talmud in Yoma 83, which establishes the text as deficient, etc., and he answers that we follow the majority of opinions even to prohibit, and we do not join the opinion of the sick person who says he needs. These are his words in brief. This reasoning is astonishing, that his view should not be joined to be lenient against the majority of opinions. Furthermore, one can say that the Talmud holds that in danger to life we do not follow the majority of opinions. In any event there is no difficulty at all to one who examines it. What the Taz distinguishes, that our relying on the sick person against one hundred applies when he asks to eat, but if he does not ask at all and only when asked says, 'I need,' he is not believed more than one physician, is difficult. If so, what does the Gemara ask there, 'but experts, no?' Perhaps there it is speaking when they ask him.
