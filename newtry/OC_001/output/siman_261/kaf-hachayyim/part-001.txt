**** ENGLISH ****
<b>1) [Seif 1] When it is uncertain whether night has fallen, meaning during twilight, etc.</b> The early and later authorities struggled to determine the measure of twilight. Responsa Ginas Veradim in manuscript writes that the universal custom throughout Jewish communities follows the opinion of the Geonim, namely Rav Sherira Gaon, Rav Hai Gaon, and Rabbeinu Nissim Gaon, as Responsa Maharam Alashkar, siman 96, writes, while after Shabbos they kindle lights after the time required to walk two or three mil. Mahari ibn Chaviv, chapter 2 of Hilchos Kiddush HaChodesh, attributes this view to Rif, Rambam, and Rosh. The words of the Nagid, Rabbeinu Avraham son of Rambam, also accord with this opinion, as Maharam Alashkar writes in Responsa, siman 96. Birkei Yosef, paragraph 1, writes: This implies that we maintain the opinion of the Geonim. Even if you say the dispute is evenly balanced, we lack the authority to abolish the custom. These are his words. Get Mekushar, in his commentary to the additions of Re'em at the end of Hilchos Yom Kippur, folio 108b, similarly writes that common custom follows the opinion of the Geonim, that the period of twilight begins immediately after sunset and lasts three quarters of a mil. This view is reasonable, the custom of the Jewish people is Torah, and they have authorities upon whom to rely. One who wishes to be stringent like Rabbeinu Tam, meaning after Shabbos, is praiseworthy, but one should not protest against those who are lenient; see there. Machazik Berachah, paragraph 7, cites this. Responsa Batei Kehunah, volume 2, siman 4, writes the same, as cited by Machazik Berachah in Kuntres Acharon, paragraph 2; see there. Gra likewise discusses this at length and brings several proofs that twilight begins at the start of sunset, meaning immediately after the sun is no longer visible above the earth; see there. Our present custom follows the opinion of the Geonim specifically as a stringency, namely, for the onset of Shabbos, Yom Kippur, and the like. Thus, after sunset begins, when the sun is no longer visible above the earth, we regard the time as twilight, and after three quarters of a mil from that time we regard it as night. Regarding the conclusion of Shabbos and Yom Kippur, eating matzah on Pesach night, and anything else requiring definite night, however, we do not regard it as definitely night until three medium-sized stars emerge. This time varies by location. In these regions, such as Eretz Yisrael, Syria, and Babylonia, it occurs approximately two thirds of an hour after the sun is no longer visible above the earth. See further below, siman 293, paragraph 5, and siman 331.

<b>3) There: One may not tithe produce that is certainly untithed.</b> This constitutes a complete rectification. Although we maintain the opinion that rabbinic prohibitions were not imposed during twilight, as stated in siman 342, Beis Yosef writes that this applies only for the sake of a mitzvah or when one is preoccupied and hurried. He therefore writes that during twilight one may tell a non-Jew to perform any labor needed for a mitzvah or about which one is preoccupied and hurried; see there. Taz, paragraph 1, cites this and writes that accordingly, if one needs a utensil on Shabbos and has no other, he may immerse it during twilight. There is, however, another permitted course: giving it to a non-Jew as a gift, as stated in Yoreh De'ah, siman 120, seif 16. We should therefore not permit the rabbinic prohibition even during twilight. These are his words. Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 1, writes the same. He may likewise immerse it through a subterfuge, such as drawing water with it for drinking or another need, as stated below in siman 323, seif 7; see there. If it is a dirty knife, he may immerse it while washing the knife, which is also a permitted subterfuge. Eshel Avraham, paragraph 3. See below, paragraph 10.

<b>4) There: One may not tithe produce that is certainly untithed, even when the tithe obligation is rabbinic.</b> Eshel Avraham, paragraph 2. See below, siman 307, paragraph 149. One may, however, separate challah outside Eretz Yisrael during twilight, because this is a rabbinic institution without Scriptural support, enacted so that the law of challah would not be forgotten. Beis David, siman 103; Birkei Yosef, paragraph 3. Olas Shabbos, paragraph 1, however, writes that if one forgot to separate challah while it was still daytime, he should not separate it during twilight, because he can leave over a small portion. As we say regarding challah outside Eretz Yisrael, he may continue eating, leave a portion, and separate challah afterward. Magen Avraham, paragraph 2; Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 2; Tosefes Shabbos, paragraph 2, write the same. Accordingly, when Shabbos occurs on the day preceding Pesach and this option is unavailable, separation is permitted during twilight. Similarly, regarding challah in Eretz Yisrael, if he has nothing else to eat on Shabbos, separation is permitted during twilight, because a rabbinic prohibition for the sake of a mitzvah was not imposed during twilight. Eshel Avraham there. See below, siman 506, seif 3 in the gloss.
