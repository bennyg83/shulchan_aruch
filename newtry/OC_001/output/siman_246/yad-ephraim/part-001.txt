(ס"ק י') דאף בישראל לא מהני אא"כ זיכה לו ע"י אחר כו'. ולפענ"ד צ"ע דבישראל מהני כשמקנה לו שהוא ברשות הישראל וקיי"ל דזכין לאדם שלא בפניו וא"ל דכיון שאינו מכווין לקנות הוי כעודר בנכסי הגר וכסבור דשלו הן דלא קני דז"א דכל דאיכא דעת אחרת מקנה לו קנה כמ"ש הרשב"א בגיטין דף ך"ג גבי עובדא דזקן שאני דידע לאקנוי ע"ש וכ"כ הנ"י בב"ב דף מ"א מעובדא דרב כהנא הדר גודא ומצאתי במחנה אפרים הל' קנין חצר שכ' כן דבמתנה לא בעינן דעת המקבל אי איכא דעת אחרת מקנה והביא דברי הרשב"א והנ"י וכ' ראיה מדברי הש"ע אלו ודלא כספר מג"א כו' ומצאתי בקצה"ח ח"ב סימן רה"ע סק"ח שעמד ג"כ בזה על דברי הצ"צ ועבודת הגרשוני שכתבו לענין בכור דכל שלא כוון העכו"ם לקנות דלא קני והקשה מדברי הרשב"א והנ"י הנ"ל וכתב לחלק דבעכו"ם לא מהני דעת אחרת מקנה שאינו רק מטעם זכיה שהנותן עצמו זכה לו שלא בפניו ואין דין זכיה לעכו"ם. ומ"מ דברי המ"א צ"ע דמשמע דאף בישראל כה"ג לא מהני אם לא בזיכה ע"י אחר וז"א דדעת מקנה ודאי קונה ואפשר דס"ל להמג"א דבעינן דוקא שתהיה ברשות הזוכה בשעה שזה מזכה לו ואז מועיל אף שלא נתכוין לזכות וא"כ אם מקנה לו שלא בפניו יש לחוש שמא תהיה אז הבהמה באגם וכדומה ולא קנה ומה שימשכנה לרשותו אח"כ זה לא מהני כיון דאין מתכוין לקנות ואז אין דעת אחרת מקנה ולפי"ז אם הישראל רואה הבהמה עומדת בחצר העכו"ם שפיר הוא יכול להקנות שלא בפניו אפשר דמ"מ החצר אינו קונה לעכו"ם כמ"ש בתשובת אמונת שמואל והביאו בחק יעקב סימן תמ"ח ומה שהעכו"ם עובד בה אח"כ אינו קנין אעפ"י שהיא עוקרת יד ורגל כיון דבשעה שזה מקנה לו עשה העכו"ם קנין בה וגם בישראל כה"ג לא קנה כ"א כשהזוכה עושה מעשה בשעה שזה מקנה או שהיא בחצרו אז ואז אף שלא נתכוין לקנות דחצירו של אדם קונה לו כו' משא"כ לעובדי כוכבים ומזלות לא מהני אף בעומדה בחצר בשעת מעשה:

Regarding Magen Avraham, seif katan 10, who writes that even with an Israelite it is ineffective unless the owner transfers possession to him through another person: In my humble opinion, this requires study. With an Israelite, it is effective when the owner transfers the animal to him while it is in the owner's domain, because we rule that one may acquire an advantage for a person in his absence. One cannot argue that since the recipient does not intend to acquire, this resembles one who tills the property of a convert who died, believing it to be his own, and therefore does not acquire. That is incorrect, for whenever another person's intent effects the transfer, the recipient acquires, as Rashba writes in Gittin 23 regarding the statement that an elder is different because he knows how to transfer ownership; see there. Nimukei Yosef writes similarly in Bava Batra 41 from the incident in which Rav Kahana repaired a wall. I found that Machaneh Ephraim, Laws of Acquisition by Courtyard, rules likewise: regarding a gift, the recipient's awareness is unnecessary when another person's intent effects the transfer. He cites Rashba and Nimukei Yosef and brings proof from these words of the Shulchan Aruch, contrary to Magen Avraham. I also found that Ketzot HaChoshen, part II, siman 275, seif katan 8, raises this difficulty against Tzemach Tzedek and Avodat HaGershuni, who wrote regarding a firstborn animal that if the non-Jew did not intend to acquire, he did not acquire it. Ketzot challenges them from Rashba and Nimukei Yosef, and distinguishes that with a non-Jew, another person's intent to transfer cannot suffice. Its efficacy is based only on the law of acquisition on another's behalf, whereby the donor himself acquires for the recipient in his absence, and the law of such acquisition does not apply to a non-Jew. Nevertheless, Magen Avraham's words require study, because they imply that even for an Israelite such a transfer is ineffective unless made through another person, whereas the transferor's intent certainly causes acquisition. Perhaps Magen Avraham holds that the object must actually be in the recipient's domain at the moment the owner transfers it to him. It then takes effect even though the recipient did not intend to acquire. If the owner transfers it in the recipient's absence, however, there is concern that the animal may then be in a meadow or similar place and no acquisition occurs. The recipient's later act of drawing it into his domain does not help, because at that later moment he does not intend to acquire and the transferor's intent is no longer operating. Accordingly, if the Israelite sees the animal standing in the non-Jew's courtyard, perhaps he can transfer it to him in his absence. Yet even then, a courtyard may not acquire for a non-Jew, as Responsa Emunat Shmuel states and Chok Yaakov cites in siman 448. The non-Jew's later work with the animal is not an act of acquisition, although the animal moves hand and foot, because the non-Jew did not perform an acquisition at the moment the owner transferred it. Even for an Israelite, acquisition of this kind takes effect only when the recipient performs an act at the time of transfer, or when the object is then in his courtyard, in which case it is acquired despite his lack of intent because a person's courtyard acquires for him. For a non-Jew, by contrast, even standing in his courtyard at the time does not suffice.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Seif katan 12.</b> “Rather, it is prohibited rabbinically” means that an Israelite is prohibited to place a load upon the animal because this is a weekday activity. Consequently, when the non-Jew does so for his own purpose, there is no prohibition at all. Shibbolei HaLeket therefore writes that if the animal was transferred to the non-Jew before the festival, then even if he rides it and carries a load upon it, the Israelite need not protest. See the Beit Yosef, siman 305. There is no concern of resting one's animal, because the Israelite himself may carry on Yom Tov. He brings proof from Rashba's statement that within a courtyard there is no prohibition of resting one's animal. This shows that whenever the labor is not prohibited, its performance by one's animal is likewise permitted.

Magen Avraham's statement, “except when he drives the animal from behind,” at first appears irrelevant here. In the ruling of Shibbolei HaLeket, the prohibition of driving an animal does not apply. Only the command that one's animal rest, which is imposed upon the Israelite, requires discussion as to whether the non-Jew's performance of labor with it on Yom Tov is prohibited. Driving an animal, however, is a personal prohibition upon the person who drives it. Since here the non-Jew is the one driving, it is obvious that the Israelite is unaffected, whether according to Rashba or Ran. It appears that Magen Avraham's intent is as follows. At first glance, there is no proof that an animal may carry on Yom Tov merely because a person may carry. Had carrying been permitted on Yom Tov because it is not classified as labor, then there would indeed be no command to prevent one's animal from carrying, because carrying would not be included in the command of rest. In truth, however, carrying is certainly labor; it is permitted to a person only by the principle that once a category of labor was permitted for food preparation, it was also permitted for other festival needs. That principle may apply only to human labor. Regarding the separate command that one's animal rest and not perform labor, what source establishes that the same principle applies to the animal's labor? This also rebuts the proof from Rashba that there is no prohibition when an animal carries in a courtyard. There, carrying in a courtyard is not labor at all for a person; since it is outside the category of labor, one is obviously not commanded to prevent one's animal from doing it. One might therefore have distinguished the cases. In truth, however, this depends upon the dispute between Rashba and Ran. Rashba holds that the prohibition of driving an animal applies even in a courtyard. His reasoning is that although carrying a load in a courtyard is not deemed labor, the prohibition of driving applies to even the smallest act. Ran disagrees and holds that whatever a person would not be liable for doing himself, he is likewise not liable for doing through his animal, as explained by Ran in the first chapter of Avodah Zarah and in Responsa Rivash. According to Rashba, one can distinguish as stated: even though this labor was permitted to a person on Yom Tov by the principle of “once permitted,” his animal might remain prohibited. According to Ran, however, there is a general rule that whatever a person may do himself without liability, he likewise incurs no liability through his animal. Thus, regarding carrying on Yom Tov, since it is permitted to a person, it is also permitted through his animal and is not included in the command “so that it may rest.” This may be Magen Avraham's intent in stating that Bach did not descend to this distinction. No emendation of the text is needed. He means that Bach likewise follows Ran and does not distinguish between a person's carrying and carrying by an animal. Bach writes in siman 432 that there is no prohibition of driving an animal on Yom Tov, and cites Rambam and Semag, who state that one may not place a load upon an animal on Yom Tov because it is a weekday activity. This proves, he says, that according to Torah law neither resting one's animal nor driving it is prohibited. Bach thus holds that there is no distinction between the command that one's animal rest and the prohibition of driving it. Admittedly, the proof is not decisive. Bach may hold that carrying a load could have been prohibited because of resting one's animal, and in that context there is no difference between driving and resting one's animal; but where carrying is permitted, such as in a courtyard, he might still distinguish. It therefore appears preferable to read: “Bach did not descend to this distinction,” meaning that since he cites the law that one may not place a load upon an animal as proof regarding the prohibition of driving it, he evidently does not distinguish between carrying, which is labor permitted to a person, and other labors. Magen Avraham writes similarly in siman 495, seif katan 5, that one cannot exempt the animal merely because the person himself may carry. In my novellae I have explained at length that, independently, there is no proof from Rambam and Semag.
