(א) מותר להשאיל וכו' - הוא דעת הרי"ף והרמב"ם וס"ל דהא דתניא לא ישכיר כליו לא"י בע"ש ובד' וה' מותר הוא מטעם שביתת כלים דאסור ואתיא כב"ש דס"ל אדם מצווה על שביתת כליו כמו על שביתת בהמתו אבל ב"ה ס"ל שביתת כלים אין אדם מצווה עליו וממילא מותר להשאיל ולהשכיר כליו אפילו בע"ש ואפילו כלים שעושין בהם מלאכה ומ"מ גם הם ס"ל דכל הכלים אין היתר להשכיר רק בהבלעה אבל לא על שבת לחוד אפילו אם משכירם בתחלת השבוע דזה הוי שכר שבת וכמ"ש אח"כ לפי הי"א דע"ז לא מצינו מי שמקיל והי"א הוא דעת הרב רבינו יונה דהך ברייתא לא אסרה משום שביתת כלים אלא ככו"ע אתיא והטעם דכשמוסר בע"ש את הכלים שעושין מלאכה בהן והא"י יעשה בהן מיד בשבת נראה כמו שעושה מלאכתו בשליחותו של ישראל וזה אסור דאמירה לא"י שבות ואע"ג דבסימן רנ"ב ס"ב איתא דשרי ליתן עורות לעבדן עם חשיכה דא"י אדעתיה דנפשיה קעביד כיון דקצץ לו דמים שאני התם שאין ריוח לישראל במה שהא"י עושה בשבת אבל בשכירות כלים ידוע הוא שאם יתנה עם הא"י שישבות בהכלים בשבת לא יתן לו שכירות כ"כ הילכך כיון שיש ריוח לישראל במה שהכלים נשכרים אצל הא"י בסתמא ועושה בהן מלאכה בשבת מחזי כשלוחו של ישראל לזה משא"כ כשמוסר לו בד' וה' מותר דלא מיתחזי כולי האי כשלוחו:
**** ENGLISH ****
It is permitted to lend, etc. This is the view of the Rif and Rambam. They hold that the baraita stating that one may not rent utensils to a non-Jew on Friday, but may do so on Wednesday or Thursday, is based on the obligation that one's utensils rest on Shabbat, and follows Beit Shammai, who maintain that a person is commanded regarding the resting of his utensils just as regarding the resting of his animal. Beit Hillel, however, hold that a person is not commanded regarding the resting of his utensils. It is therefore permitted to lend or rent one's utensils even on Friday, including utensils used for labor. Nevertheless, they too hold that utensils may be rented only through inclusion in a broader rental period, but not for Shabbat alone, even if the rental begins earlier in the week, because that is payment for Shabbat, as explained below according to the alternative view; no authority is found to permit this. The alternative view is that of Rabbeinu Yonah. He explains that the baraita did not prohibit the rental because of the resting of utensils, but applies according to all opinions. When one gives utensils used for labor to a non-Jew on Friday and the non-Jew will immediately use them on Shabbat, it appears as though he performs the labor as the Israelite's agent, which is prohibited because instructing a non-Jew is a rabbinic prohibition. Although siman 252:2 permits giving hides to a non-Jewish tanner near nightfall where a fixed price was agreed, because the non-Jew works for his own benefit, that case differs: the Israelite gains nothing from the non-Jew's working on Shabbat. With rented utensils, it is known that if the Israelite stipulated that the non-Jew cease using them on Shabbat, the non-Jew would pay less rent. Since the Israelite profits from the ordinary rental under which the non-Jew uses the utensils on Shabbat, it appears as though the non-Jew is his agent. If he gives them on Wednesday or Thursday, however, this appearance is not as strong, and it is permitted.

Even according to our practice, where a full biblical public domain is generally absent, it is prohibited, because instructing a non-Jew is a rabbinic prohibition even regarding a rabbinic matter. If the city is walled and carrying throughout it is permitted, and the non-Jew also resides there, one may lend him utensils not used for labor. Regarding the resting of his animal. For it is written, 'So that your ox and your donkey may rest.' Although he rented the animal to a non-Jew, rental does not transfer ownership, and it therefore remains the Israelite's animal, regarding which he is commanded that it rest. If he bought an animal from a non-Jew, or sold one to a non-Jew, using only meshichah or only payment, the law is doubtful, and one rules stringently regarding the resting of the animal.

Even if he declares it ownerless before three people, not everyone knows of the declaration, and suspicion may arise that an Israelite's animal is working on Shabbat. The Bach permits initially renting it on condition that he declare it ownerless before three people, because the matter becomes public through them and suspicion will not arise. In pressing circumstances one may rely on his view, as will be explained at the end of the siman.

Although rabbinic law requires ownerless declaration before three people, biblically even a private declaration suffices. Here, since he already rented it, stipulated that it be returned, and the non-Jew retained it, this suffices to avoid a biblical prohibition. The Beit Yosef cites authorities who require three people even in this after-the-fact case. One should therefore do so where possible, and at minimum should declare it ownerless before one person, since many leading authorities hold that even biblically property is not ownerless until the declaration is made before at least one person. One member of his household suffices.

Nevertheless, one may rent them for Yom Tov only by inclusion, so that the payment not constitute Yom Tov wages. Maharshal disagrees and holds that Yom Tov and Shabbat are identical in this respect, as in other labors. The Magen Avraham discusses this at length and notes that the Beit Yosef in siman 495 cites the views of the Rishonim and inclines to prohibit. One should therefore be stringent. The Gra likewise concludes that Maharshal's view is primary, and in siman 495:3 explains that the prohibition against taking out a load on an animal on Yom Tov is because of the obligation that one's animal rest.
