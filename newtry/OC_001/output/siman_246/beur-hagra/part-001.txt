<b>כי אין כו'.</b> דאין העו"ג נאמן להתיר מהא דפ' גיד הנשה לא יאמר אדם לעו"ג ליקח לי בשר כו'. ולא לאסור מהא דסוף יבמות של עזקה הן כו' וסוף פ' החולץ אין עדות לעו"ג ובתוספתא פ"ה דדמאי גוי שהיה צווח כו' קחו לכם פירות עזקה הן כו' אבל אם א"ל מאיש פלוני גוי לקחתי כו' משלי הבאתים נאמן להחמיר דברי ר' רשב"ג אומר אינו נאמן שדברי הגוי לא מעלין ולא מורידין ואף ר' ל"ק אלא משום שהוא בידו וע' ר"ס רמ"ו:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>For there is no, etc.</b> A non-Jew is not believed to permit a matter, as is evident from the passage in Gid HaNasheh: 'A person may not say to a non-Jew: Buy meat for me,' etc. Nor is he believed to prohibit it, as is evident from the end of Yevamot regarding the statement, 'They are produce of Azekah,' and from the end of the chapter HaCholetz: 'There is no testimony by a non-Jew.' Likewise, the Tosefta, chapter 5 of Demai, states: If a non-Jew was calling out, 'Buy produce from me; it is produce of Azekah,' etc.; but if he said, 'I purchased it from such-and-such a non-Jew,' etc., or, 'I brought it from my own produce,' he is believed to impose a stringency. These are the words of Rabbi. Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel says he is not believed, because the words of a non-Jew neither establish nor remove a prohibition. Even Rabbi disagrees only because the matter is in his possession. See the beginning of siman 246.

<b>יפקירנה.</b> נדרים מ"ה א' ע"ש בפי' הרא"ש ד"ה ד"ת כו' שמחלק דבדיעבד אפילו בינו לבין עצמו וכ"ה בח"מ שם וז"ש כאן כדי שינצל דמדרבנן בעינן ג' ונלמד כ"ז דה"ה בינו לבין עצמו וסגי בכך מגיגית נר כו' לב"ש בפ"ק דשבת ועתוס' שם ד"ה דמפקרא כו' דהכא שאני כו' וכאן ג"כ א"א בענין אחר ודלא כמרדכי וש"פ שכ' דדוקא בפני ג' והם לשיטתן שחילקו בין גיגית כו' לבהמה דגיגית סתמא מפקר להו ור"ל משום שהוא דבר ההוה תמיד משא"כ בבהמה ולכן אפי' בדיעבד דוקא בפני ג' אבל לחילוק הרא"ש אפי' בינו לבין עצמו כנ"ל:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>He should declare it ownerless.</b> See Nedarim 45a and the Rosh's explanation there, s.v. 'By Torah law,' etc. He distinguishes that after the fact, even a private declaration is effective, and so it is ruled in Choshen Mishpat there. This is why the wording here is 'so that he may be saved,' because rabbinically three people are required. All of this, namely that the same rule applies even to a private declaration and that this suffices, is learned from the cases of the vat, lamp, etc., according to Beit Shammai in the first chapter of Shabbos. See Tosafot there, s.v. 'Since he declares it ownerless,' who write that this case is different, etc. Here too, no other course is possible. This is unlike the Mordechai and other authorities, who write that the declaration must specifically be before three people. They follow their own approach, distinguishing between a vat, etc., and an animal: a vat is ordinarily declared ownerless, meaning because this circumstance commonly recurs, unlike an animal. Therefore, according to them, even after the fact the declaration is effective only before three. According to the Rosh's distinction, however, even a private declaration is effective, as explained above.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And specifically on Shabbos, etc.</b> The Tosafot Yom Tov inferred at the end of the second chapter of Yom Tov from the Mishnah's statements, 'His cow may go out,' etc., and, 'One may cool an animal on Yom Tov.' Since the latter clause specifies Yom Tov, the first clause appears to apply specifically on Shabbos. The Yerushalmi, which states regarding riding an animal that the prohibition is due to the duty that one's animal rest, likewise discusses Shabbos, for it says: If one was riding an animal, they tell him, 'Dismount,' etc. Therefore the Gemara did not explain the Mishnah's prohibition against riding as based on the duty that one's animal rest. This is unlike the Rif and Rosh. The Yerushalmi in chapter 5 of Yom Tov states: 'Why did they say that if one was riding an animal they tell him, Dismount? Because he is commanded regarding his animal's rest as he is regarding his own, as it says, So that your ox and donkey may rest as you do.' That verse was stated only regarding Shabbos. See Magen Avraham in the name of Yam Shel Shlomo. It is possible that Yam Shel Shlomo holds as above, since the Gemara gives another rationale, as stated above. As a matter of practical halachah, however, his position does not appear correct; rather, the primary ruling is that on Yom Tov as well one is commanded that his animal rest, as stated in Yam Shel Shlomo.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And some say that since.</b> This follows Rav Yehudah there, whose view is accepted as halachah, for he says that even in such a case it is not considered his. A supporting baraita is cited there for Rav Yehudah: 'A woman who brings property into the marriage,' etc. Thus, anything one cannot sell is not considered his. The dispute between these two opinions depends upon the Rif's uncertainty there. This also resolves the difficulty in the Rambam, who rules there like Rav Yehudah, yet also rules in accordance with the baraita supporting Rabbi Ami concerning slaves of melog property, etc. The matter is as follows. The Rif was uncertain regarding the baraita about a woman who brings property into the marriage, writing, 'It is not clear to us,' etc. The Gemara there states: Rava said to Rav Nachman, 'But there is a supporting baraita for Rabbi Ami,' etc. At first glance this is difficult: is there not also a supporting baraita for Rav Yehudah? The passage may be resolved in two ways. First, the case is not comparable to Rav Yehudah's, because although he cannot sell the property, it is nevertheless his, as stated there: 'This case is not comparable, but rather,' etc. Second, that baraita represents an individual opinion, because Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel follows his own view in Gittin 41a, and the halachah there does not follow him. The Gemara answers that although a baraita supports Rabbi Ami and the law of the baraita concerning slaves of melog property is correct, nevertheless in the present case Rav Yehudah's reasoning is more persuasive. According to both explanations, the Rambam properly rules in accordance with the baraita concerning slaves of melog property. The practical difference between the explanations concerns whether one should rule in accordance with the baraita about property brought into marriage. According to the first explanation, the Gemara may hold that this baraita is not an individual opinion, as the Baal HaMaor explains at length at the beginning of the chapter Almanah, s.v. 'They sold,' that the Sages do not disagree with him on this point; see there. This is the Rif's statement, 'It is not clear to us,' etc. Here too, the dispute depends on these two explanations. According to the first, the Gemara does not accept the supporting baraita for Rav Yehudah, because even though he cannot sell it, it is nevertheless his. According to the second, that baraita is rejected only because it is an individual opinion, but its underlying rule is correct: anything one cannot sell is not considered his. The Geonim explain the baraita about a woman bringing property into marriage as referring to movable property and as connected to the dispute where she says, 'My utensil,' etc.; likewise the baraita about one who rents a cow, and the discussion there, etc. This resolves the Rambam's ruling, codified in Even HaEzer 90:14, that a husband may not, etc., but if he sold, etc. Since it is unclear whether that baraita should be followed in practice, as the Rif writes, he rules that initially the husband should not sell, but if he sold, what he did is effective.
