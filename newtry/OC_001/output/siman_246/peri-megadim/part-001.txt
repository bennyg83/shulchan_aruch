<b>מותר</b> עט״ז שבת י״ט א׳ ורא״ש ומ״מ פ״ו מה״ש הי״ו פירש״י כן לב״ש מלאכת ישראל בכלי ישראל הגיגית בע״ש ונעשה המלאכה בשבת אסור מ״ה לב״ש ומלאכת עכו״ם בכלי ישראל דרבנן ומש״ה בד׳ וה׳ הוה ספק דרבנן שמא לא יעשה בשבת ושרי ולדידן כב״ה שרי אף בע״ש דשביתת כלים שרי אף מדרבנן זה דעת המחבר כדיעה א׳ בסתם כר״מ ורי״ף שהשמיטן. ועב״ח ולא אבין דשביתת כלים לב״ש אף בקצץ ואף בשאלה ושלא מדעתו אסור ומחויב למחות ועיין בעל המאור בשבת י״ט וע״ז ט״ו י״א לב״ש שביתת בהמה נמי בד׳ וה׳ מותר ואין כן דעת הפוסקים ואי״ה בסעיף ג׳ יבואר בזה:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>It is permitted.</b> See Taz. Rashi explains the baraita according to Beit Shammai: labor of an Israelite performed in an Israelite's utensil, such as a vat set up on Friday in which the labor continues on Shabbat, is biblically prohibited according to Beit Shammai. Labor of a non-Jew in an Israelite's utensil is rabbinically prohibited; therefore on Wednesday or Thursday it is a rabbinic doubt whether the non-Jew will use it on Shabbat, and it is permitted. According to Beit Hillel, whom we follow, it is permitted even on Friday, because there is no obligation, even rabbinically, that utensils rest. This is the Mechaber's first, unattributed opinion, following Rambam and Rif, who omitted the prohibition. See Bach. I do not understand his words, for according to Beit Shammai the resting of utensils is prohibited even where a fixed price was set, even through lending, and even where the non-Jew uses them without the owner's knowledge; the Israelite must protest. See Baal HaMaor to Shabbat 19 and Avodah Zarah 15. Some say that even according to Beit Shammai an animal may be rented on Wednesday or Thursday, but this is not the view of the poskim. This will be explained in seif 3.

<b>ודע</b> למאן דמפרש בשבת י״ח א׳ לרב יוסף דשביתת כלים אף לב״ה אסור עב״ח כאן א״כ רב יוסף איך יפרנס ברייתא זו דאין משכירין כלים ע״ש לעכו״ם לפי׳ הרר״י באין עושין מלאכה שרי ובעושין דו״ה נמי אסור אף לב״ה וי״ל שעושים מלאכה ומינח נייחי כמו גיגית ולחי ותיקר׳ כו׳ דבל״ה שרי ודוקא כלי מחרישה וכדומה אסור לב״ה והתוס׳ שבת י״ט א׳ ד״ה לא תקנו זה בחלוק וטלית ועתו׳ י״ח א׳ ד״ה ולימא וע׳ נ״ץ. ומ״מ לפי הרר״י ל״ד מחרישה ה״ה גיגית רק חלוק וטלית שרי להרר״י. ומ״ש הט״ז בשכירות הואיל ויש ריוח לישראל נראה כשליח ומה״ט שאלה מותר. ומלבוש משמע שמב׳ חששות יאמרו שכר שבת הוא נוטל הואיל והוא ע״ש וגם חשש יאמר שלוחו הוא מש״ה אסור ומש״ה להשאיל שאין רק חששא דשלוחו לא גזרו בתלוש ואין מפורסם: ועסי׳ רמ״ד ורמ״ג כל דאיסורו מ״ע בדרבנן בחדרי חדרים מותר ה״ה זה בדר בישוב ומיהו חששא דבני ביתו יש כה״ג אם לא באין ב״ב אצלו עסי׳ רמ״ז בזה:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Know.</b> According to those who explain Rav Yosef in Shabbat 18 as holding that the resting of utensils is prohibited even according to Beit Hillel, one must explain how he understands this baraita. Under Rabbeinu Yonah's explanation, items not used for labor may be rented, while utensils used for labor would be prohibited even on Wednesday or Thursday. One may answer that the baraita concerns utensils that perform labor but periodically rest, such as a vat, beam, and press, whereas ordinary plowing implements would be prohibited. Tosafot on Shabbat 19 did not establish the case as a garment or cloak; see also Tosafot 18 and Netziv. Yet according to Rabbeinu Yonah, the rule is not limited to a plow; even a vat is prohibited, while only a garment or cloak is permitted. The Taz explains that rental, because it yields profit to the Israelite, makes the non-Jew appear as his agent, whereas lending is permitted. Levush implies that two concerns combine: people may say that he takes Shabbat wages, since he transfers it on Friday, and they may say that the non-Jew is his agent. Lending, where only the second concern exists, was not prohibited for detached objects whose use is not public. See simanim 243 and 244: where the prohibition is only rabbinic, an act done privately is permitted. The same may apply here in a sparsely populated place, though household members may still create suspicion unless none are present; see siman 247.

<b>מפני</b> עט״ז עמ״א אות וי״ו ומשמע מכאן מ״ע אסור אף בשבות דשבות עמ״ש בסי׳ רמ״ד אם לא במקום צורך קצת דאז שרי שבות דשבות פשיטא דמ״ע שרי עסי׳ שכ״ה בב״י: ומיהו אם ידוע וניכר שיוציא עכו״ם חוץ לתחום כמו מחרישה וכיוצא אסור במוקף חומה ועיין נ״ץ ולקמן תמ״ד בט״ז ה׳ נמי אם ידוע שיוציא חוץ לתחום אסור לומר לו כן בפירוש או חוץ לי״ב מיל למ״ד ד״ת אסור דלא הוה שבות דשבות ואי״ה שם יבואר: ועא״ר אות ח׳ בזה:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Because.</b> See Taz and Magen Avraham. This indicates that appearance is prohibited even regarding a double rabbinic prohibition. As written in siman 244, however, where there is some need a double rabbinic prohibition is permitted, and certainly appearance is then permitted; see Beit Yosef to siman 325. If it is evident that the non-Jew will take the item beyond the Shabbat boundary, as with a plow and the like, it remains prohibited even in a walled city. See Netziv and Taz to siman 444: if one knows he will take it beyond the boundary, he may not expressly instruct him to do so, and beyond twelve mil, according to the view that this is biblical, it is not a double rabbinic prohibition. This will be explained there. See Eliyah Rabbah.

<b>אסור</b> עט״ז מרתת אף באיסור תורה סמכינן עליה: (ול״ד יי״נ דרבנן) כמבואר בריש חולין בשחיטה וכדומה אבל להשאיל אסור אף שמתנה עמו ואם יודע נשקליה מיניה אפ״ה אסור עב״ח בתי׳ ראשון וא״ר כאן אות יו״ד: ועסי׳ ש״ה סכ״ג ומ״א יז״ח צ״ע דאף אין מוסיף שכירות בשביל שבת ואף שאלה אסור דמצווה על שביתת בהמתו ושכירות מ״ה ודאי לא קניא וליכא ס״ס ועמ״א א׳ דשכירות קניא לחומרא מדרבנן בעלמא ואי״ה שם יבואר. ועא״ר ש״ה אות כ״ה. ומיהו סוס לרכוב דלא הוה שביתת בהמתו דחי נושא עצמו רק ספק שמא יוציא אותה חוץ לי״ב מיל בש״ס בלבוש שם אפשר הוה ס״ס אם מתנה עמו שתנוח בשבת כי י״ב מיל מחלוקת הוא אבל כשעכו״ם עכ״פ מוסיף לו שכירות אח״כ אסור לקבל אף בהבלעה. וצ״ע ואי״ה לקמן וכאן במ״א אבאר עוד: ומ״ש מסימן נ״ב ס״ה אות ד׳ הקשה מכאן לשם: ואי״ה שם יבואר אי התם הוה ד״ת או לאו:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>It is prohibited.</b> See Taz. We rely on the non-Jew's fear of being caught even regarding a biblical prohibition, as is clear at the beginning of Chullin concerning slaughter and similar matters, and not only regarding ordinary non-Jewish wine, which is rabbinic. Yet lending is prohibited even when he stipulates with the non-Jew, and even if he knows he could take the animal back, as the Bach explains in his first answer and Eliyah Rabbah writes. See siman 305:23. The Magen Avraham requires study: even if no additional rent is paid for Shabbat, and even in a loan, it should be prohibited because the Israelite is commanded regarding the animal's rest, and rental certainly does not transfer ownership biblically. There is therefore no double doubt. The Magen Avraham's statement that rental transfers ownership stringently is only rabbinic. This will be explained there; see Eliyah Rabbah. With a riding horse, however, there may be no prohibition of resting the animal because a living person carries himself; the concern is only that the non-Jew may take it beyond twelve mil. Since whether twelve mil is biblical is disputed, there may be a double doubt if he stipulates that the animal rest on Shabbat. But if the non-Jew later adds rent for Shabbat, accepting it is prohibited even by inclusion. This requires study and will be explained below. The source cited from siman 52:5 raises a difficulty from here; it will be explained there whether that case is biblical.

שלא החזירה ורשות עכו״ם עליה ואין אחר יכול לזכות בה בשבת ולאחר השבת אה״נ דרשות בעליו עליה. אלא דא״כ לאחר שבת נמי חייב באונסין:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Even so.</b> See Taz. The Mechaber's words are from Rivash. On Shabbat the non-Jew has no lawful right to the animal, since the agreement ended before Shabbat and his retention is unauthorized. Biblically the Israelite can reacquire it from ownerlessness. The Beit Yosef similarly explains Rabbeinu Peretz in Semak, who permits renting by inclusion and declaring the animal ownerless, while another person cannot acquire it because the non-Jew has possession of it on Shabbat. After Shabbat, although the non-Jew's right has ended, the Israelite owner reacquires it immediately by declaration, since it was made ownerless only until the conclusion of Shabbat. The Beit Yosef therefore writes that the ownerlessness is not permanent. The Tur, however, appears to hold that the ownerlessness was made only to escape the Shabbat prohibition, and therefore another person cannot acquire it even where the non-Jew has no lawful possession on Shabbat. This is the Magen Avraham's intent, though Eliyah Rabbah questions him. Levush appears to understand it this way. The Mechaber and Rama here seem to reverse their positions from Choshen Mishpat 273. There the Mechaber holds that biblically ownerlessness must be declared before one person and rabbinically before three; accordingly here one should at least declare it before one. Rambam holds that biblically even a private declaration works and only rabbinically are witnesses needed, so for avoiding a Shabbat prohibition a private declaration suffices. Tosefet Shabbat notes this and suggests the present case is one where no other person is available; it is therefore better at least to satisfy the view that a private declaration works. The Mechaber lists private ownerlessness or transfer to the non-Jew as two emergency methods. Levush reverses the order and prefers ownerlessness before three. The reason initial reliance is prohibited may be appearance as Shabbat wages, or because the ownerlessness is not wholehearted but only intended to avoid the prohibition, which is inadequate initially. The Taz cites a certain rabbi, and Tzemach Tzedek likewise permits such a device to avoid a biblical Shabbat prohibition and, similarly, interest. Eliyah Rabbah objects that the Talmudic proof concerns only rabbinic interest; for a biblical prohibition a complete sale is required. With chametz, see siman 448 and Masat Binyamin. Interest may differ because where the transaction is not truly a loan and has the form of a sale, the Torah did not prohibit it. But sale of chametz and Shabbat are comparable and require a complete legal sale. The view cited by Olat Shabbat is sound: during the animal's labor no other Israelite will acquire it, because doing so would cause him to violate the resting of his animal; after the labor, it is no longer ownerless. This can explain Rivash's case, where the non-Jew retained it contrary to the agreement. Even if the non-Jew had no legal right, he remains liable for accidents until returning it, and thus has possession sufficient to prevent another from acquiring it on Shabbat; after Shabbat the owner's rights return.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>See.</b> Tevuot Shor, in Bechor Shor to Pesachim 21, considers that a sham sale of chametz may avoid a biblical prohibition because the owner also nullifies and declares it ownerless. According to this, after Pesach another Israelite cannot acquire it, because the ownerlessness was only for the days of Pesach, and the sale was sufficient at least to remove the rabbinic obligation of physical destruction. This may work for a rabbinic prohibition, as stated above in the name of Eliyah Rabbah. The matter will be explained in the laws of Pesach. In my view, however, nullification does not help unless it is wholehearted; with property of substantial value, one certainly does not renounce it wholeheartedly. A complete sale valid according to Torah law is therefore required. This is not the place to elaborate.
