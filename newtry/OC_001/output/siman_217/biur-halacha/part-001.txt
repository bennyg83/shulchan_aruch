<b>A fragrance became mixed</b> - This is Rambam's wording. The author of Shemen Rokeach writes that it refers only to oil and spices used for the dead, for the lavatory, and the like, which were not made for fragrance. Regarding these, Rambam writes that if their odor became mixed with a pleasant fragrance, we follow the majority. It does not refer, however, to the preceding law concerning a majority of non-Jews or a majority of Jews. In that case, even if only a minority of idolatrous fragrance became mixed in, one certainly may not recite a blessing, because idolatry is not nullified by a majority. See there. One should not challenge him from the ruling that when most of the city is Jewish one recites a blessing despite the minority of non-Jews. There, since the majority are Jewish, we follow the majority and assume that the fragrance came entirely from them and not at all from the non-Jews, who did not prepare incense at all. By contrast, where everyone prepared incense and the fragrances became mixed, he holds that following the majority does not apply, because idolatry is not nullified. This appears to be his view. From the Gra's words in the final seif katan, however, it appears that he does not agree, for he writes that this law is derived from seif 7, showing that he sees no distinction between seif 7 and seif 8. It further appears to me that Rambam's ruling derives from Rabbi Yose's statement in the Gemara, where he says that the majority was not prepared for fragrance. Rambam explains that this applies even though the odors became mixed together. The Gra, however, holds that Rambam's law can also be inferred from the opening clause of the Baraisa. See Machatzis HaShekel, who cites Chemed Moshe and concludes that his words require study. In truth, one who examines siman 216 concerning aromatic wood mixed with aromatic herbs will see that the poskim rule that one recites Borei Minei Besamim. See Kol Bo in the name of Raavad and Eliyah Rabbah. They make no inquiry at all as to which species constitutes the majority. This necessarily follows Chemed Moshe, that the majority is entirely disregarded, because in any event one smells even the minority.
