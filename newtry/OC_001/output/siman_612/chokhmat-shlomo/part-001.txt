<b>(שם סעיף ט בהגה) </b> שתה משקין שאינן ראויין לשתייה כגון ציר או מורייס וחומץ חי פטור נ"ב הנה מלשונו משמע הא בציר ומורייס אפילו מזוג ותמוה לי א"כ מי דחקו להש"ס ביומא דפ"א ובכריתות דח"י ע"ב לו' דהך מתניתין דשתה ציר ומורייס פטור דהא חומץ חייב דהא מני רבי היא ודלמא אתיא אף כרבנן ומיירי ע"י מזיגה וקמ"ל דציר ומורייס פטור אף במזוג הא חומץ חייב במזוג ולעולם רבנן היא ומזה מוכח דבמזוג אף ציר ומורייס חייב וזה דלא כדמשמע מדברי הרב בכאן וצ"ע ודוק:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>(There, se'if 9 in the gloss)</b> "If he drank liquids that are not fit for drinking, such as brine or muryas, or undiluted vinegar, he is exempt." Note: From his language it implies that with brine and muryas he is exempt even if diluted. This is astonishing to me. If so, what forced the Talmud in Yoma 81 and Kerisus 18b to say that this Mishnah, "if one drank brine or muryas he is exempt," since vinegar would make him liable, is according to Rabbi? Perhaps it follows even the Rabbis and speaks when diluted, and teaches that brine and muryas are exempt even when diluted, whereas vinegar is liable when diluted; and the Mishnah would still follow the Rabbis. From this it is proved that when diluted, even brine and muryas make one liable. This is not as implied by the Rav's words here. This requires study; examine carefully.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>(There, se'if 10)</b> "And some say that the measure for joining drinks is the time of eating a peras, like the joining of foods." Note: When I studied tractate Kerisus, chapter 3, I wondered about the first opinion, which holds that the measure for drinking is the time of a revi'is, for this is against the Talmud there. I then looked in the Beis Yosef, and this indeed is the Raavad's proof, and they wondered about the Rambam on this. After reconsidering, I changed my mind, for one can say that Yom Kippur is different. Granted, there, where the matter depends on drinking, drinking within the combined time of eating a peras is considered drinking. But on Yom Kippur, where the matter depends on settling the mind, the settling of the mind through this drinking occurs only if he drinks within the measure of a revi'is, but if he delayed longer, there is no settling of the mind. However, according to this, at least with other prohibitions the Rambam should not have ruled this way. One can say that with invalidating the body, where the matter depends on his mouth, even within the time of eating a peras it joins. But with other eating prohibitions, where the matter depends on the pleasure of his throat and innards, the pleasure of his throat and innards occurs only if it is within the measure of a revi'is, and this is fine; examine carefully. <b>(There in the gloss)</b> "It is permitted on Yom Kippur to touch foods and drinks and give them to children, etc." Note: See the Magen Avraham, who brought proof from Yoma that we do not decree, etc. His words are astonishing, for we rule that there is no rabbinic prohibition in the Temple. If so, even without this, it would not be appropriate to decree lest he eat. Rather, Rabbi Yehudah decrees because he holds that a rabbinic prohibition that is close to certain is decreed even in the Temple; specifically where it is not close to certain but only a concern, we do not decree in the Temple, but a rabbinic prohibition that is close to certain we do decree even in the Temple. If so, the Gemara answers well: on Yom Kippur, since everyone does not eat, it is not close to certain that he will come to eat, for one can say that he will remember, since everyone else also does not eat. Although there is still a doubt that perhaps he will eat, since it is only a mere concern, again there is no rabbinic prohibition in the Temple. If so, this works in the Temple, but for us one can say that since there is at least a concern lest he eat, we therefore decree that he should not touch either. Furthermore, even without this, it seems that the opposite is proved from there, that on Yom Kippur we are concerned lest he come to eat from it. The fact that the Talmud did not ask from every Yom Kippur, why are we not concerned lest he eat, is no proof at all. On the contrary, the words of the Magen Avraham are astonishing: how would he err and eat because of an ordinary Yom Kippur, and only because he is also an onen would he err and eat? Rather, the intent is as follows. Certainly, in general, if he occupies himself with foods, we are concerned that he will forget that it is Yom Kippur, since he is occupied with the service of the day. Therefore there is no difficulty from every Kohen Gadol. But according to Rabbi Yehudah it asks as follows: since he holds that we decree regarding an onen lest he eat, the reason must be that he will forget he is an onen. But oninus and bitterness of heart are not things one is likely to forget, for he is an onen because of that bitterness of heart. If so, there is no concern that he will forget; rather, the concern must be that he will err in the law and say: since he is an onen and it is permitted for him to offer, it is also permitted for him to eat, as the Magen Avraham wrote. If so, the Talmud asks this: since Rabbi Yehudah is concerned for error in law, and since in truth we rule that an onen is exempt from all commandments stated in the Torah, granted that we rule this is only regarding active commandments but regarding passive restraint he is forbidden like every Israelite, nevertheless we should be concerned that he will err and think that since he is an onen he is entirely exempt from all commandments. In particular on Yom Kippur, where there is no explicit prohibition regarding affliction, only a positive commandment, he may err and think that since an onen is exempt from all commandments, he is exempt even from the affliction of Yom Kippur. Then, since he is occupied with food, we should be concerned that he will mistakenly eat. Without Rabbi Yehudah's words, however, there would be no difficulty, for one can say that even if he errs in law that an onen is exempt from all commandments, nevertheless he recognizes that he is forbidden to eat consecrated foods because he is an onen, and oninus will not lead him to eat. But according to Rabbi Yehudah, who holds regarding an ordinary onen that we are concerned he will err and eat, meaning that he will think that since he offers as an onen, he is also permitted to eat, then how does he offer as an onen on Yom Kippur? Let us be concerned that he will err and say: since he is an onen he is also exempt from the mitzvos of Yom Kippur; and because he is an onen he is not forbidden, for he will think that since he offers as an onen he is also permitted to eat. Therefore he will come to eat since he is occupied with it. To this the Talmud answers that on Yom Kippur, since everyone does not eat, he will not err in both matters, for he will know there is a whichever-way-you-turn case here: if the law of oninus applies to him, then he is forbidden to eat because he is an onen; and if he thinks the law of oninus does not apply because he offers, then he will again know that it is forbidden to eat on Yom Kippur because it is Yom Kippur. Therefore, whichever way, there is no concern for error, for he knows there is a whichever-way-you-turn here. But with an ordinary onen one can well say that he will err: since he offers, the law of oninus does not apply and he will eat. This appears correct to my humble opinion as the explanation of the sugya. If so, it proves the opposite: there is concern on Yom Kippur lest he eat if he is occupied with eating in his case. The same applies in our case, where he is occupied with food: there is concern that he will forget that today is Yom Kippur and come to eat. In my humble opinion this is correct. I do not have time now to elaborate at such length. See what I wrote in my work on Orach Chayim in the year 599, a correct discussion of this with the help of Heaven; examine very carefully.
