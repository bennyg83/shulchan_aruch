<b>(Subparagraph 3) In addition to, etc.</b> And however, etc., until he loathes, etc. The Magen Avraham's intent is not to explain the Rema's words, for certainly according to the Rema, even if he loathes his eating, nevertheless with fragrant foods he is liable. The Rema is referring to the Mechaber's words, who wrote that until he loathes his food he is exempt, and about this the Rema wrote that if he ate fragrant foods he is liable. This implies that even if he had already eaten until he loathed his food, he is nevertheless liable. Rather, the Magen Avraham brought the words of the Bach, who disagrees with the Rema and holds that one must distinguish between not desiring to eat and loathing his food, in order to reconcile the view of Rashi and those with him with the view of Rabbenu Tam and the Rambam. Rashi and those who follow his approach hold that if one does not desire eating, even if he does not loathe his food, if he afterward ate, he is exempt. Rabbenu Tam and the Rambam hold that we require specifically that he loathe his food. About this the Bach wrote that Rashi is speaking when he eats foods that are not fragrant; in that case, if he had already eaten until he no longer desires food, that suffices to exempt him. Rabbenu Tam and the Rambam speak when he eats fragrant foods, and therefore he is not exempt until he loathes his food.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>(Subparagraph 5) Undiluted vinegar, he is exempt.</b> It seems to me this is specifically when it bubbles, etc., as written in Yoreh De'ah siman 123. It is explained there that if a non-Jew touched our vinegar, it is not forbidden. Rabbenu Tam wrote that it specifically requires that it bubble, etc.; if not, it is forbidden through a non-Jew's touch because of doubt, for we are not expert in what is vinegar. There are some who call it vinegar, while some people recite kiddush on that wine. But if it bubbles, etc., then it is certainly vinegar. According to this, the Magen Avraham's intent here in writing that he is certainly exempt is necessarily specifically when it bubbles, etc. But if it does not bubble, it is a doubt. This is forced, however, since in any event he would be exempt because of doubt. One cannot say there is no practical difference for us between liability and exemption, since in any event there is a prohibition, because there is a practical difference regarding a sick person, as the Taz wrote in subparagraph 1. According to this, here too there is a practical difference with a sick person who needs to drink vinegar: if possible, it is better to give him vinegar that bubbles, where there is certainly no liability, rather than vinegar that does not bubble, where in any event there is a possible liability. But what seems more correct to me is that the Magen Avraham holds that with vinegar that does not bubble, he is certainly liable, and not merely because of doubt. This is from his own reasoning, without proof: since on Yom Kippur one is liable for the pleasure of his throat, if it is not so strong that it bubbles, certainly he has pleasure and it is proper that he be liable. So too he wrote above in siman 204, subparagraph 13, in these words: when it does not bubble, he certainly has pleasure and needs to recite a blessing, end quote. He needed proof from Yoreh De'ah siman 123 only that when it bubbles there is no doubt, for it is certainly called vinegar and harms the one who drinks it, and he is certainly exempt if he drinks it on Yom Kippur.

<b>And so it implies in the Rosh, chapter 6 of Berachos, siman 23.</b> There we learned in a Mishnah: over vinegar one recites Shehakol. The Rosh asked: the Talmud compares the law of a blessing to Yom Kippur, and anything for which one is exempt on Yom Kippur if he ate it, one does not recite a blessing over it. But regarding vinegar we rule on Yom Kippur like the Rabbis against Rabbi, that if one drank vinegar he is exempt. Why then did we learn that one recites a blessing over it? And why did he not answer that the Mishnah, that one recites a blessing over it, speaks of vinegar that is not strong? See what the Magen Avraham wrote in siman 204, subparagraph 13. He answered, in one answer, that the Mishnah in Berachos speaks of one who has pain in his teeth and whose remedy is vinegar, and he put much vinegar into soup and drinks it for healing, and the vinegar is primary. The same applies regarding Yom Kippur, for their laws are equal, as above. However, this proof can be rejected: perhaps what the Rosh wrote, "and he put in much vinegar," does not mean that the quantity of vinegar was large, but rather that it was more than the soup, for this is the way of drinking it for healing. Rather, the Magen Avraham's proof is that if one recites a blessing over a large amount of plain vinegar, why did he need to establish it as a case where he put it into soup and had tooth pain? He should have answered more simply that it speaks where he drank a large amount of undiluted vinegar.
