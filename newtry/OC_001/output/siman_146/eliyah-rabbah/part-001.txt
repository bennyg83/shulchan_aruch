[א] <b>אסור לצאת וכו'.</b> אפילו בין פסוק לפסוק (טור) וכן משמע בשבועות שאומרים אקדמות (מגן אברהם). ולעניות דעתי משמע מלבוש דמותר לצאת באקדמות דהא גוללין הספר תורה בשעת אקדמות, מה שאין כן בין פסוק לפסוק אף בזמניהם שהיו מתרגמינן משמע שלא היו גוללין דאי היה גוללין למה אין רשאי הקורא לסייע למתרגם וכן משמע בסימן קל"ט סעיף ד' וה':
**** ENGLISH ****
[1] <b>It is forbidden to leave, etc.</b> Even between one verse and the next. Tur. This likewise applies on Shavuos when Akdamos is recited. Magen Avraham. In my humble opinion, however, Levush implies that one may leave during Akdamos, since the Sefer Torah is rolled closed during Akdamos. By contrast, between one verse and the next, even in their times when the verses were translated, it appears that they did not roll it closed. If they had, why would the reader not be permitted to assist the translator? This is also implied by siman 139, seifim 4 and 5.

[ג] <b>כיון שהתחיל הקורא וכו'.</b> משמע ולא מפתיחת ספר תורה מיד, וכן בלחם חמודות דף מ"ד. ומלבושי יום טוב כתב לא ידעתי מה ראה הלבוש לחלוק, ואף שדקדק מדכתיב ובפתחו משמע מיד שנפתח יש לומר שלא היה הפסק בין פתיחה לקריאה אבל אם יש הפסק מותר עד כאן דבריו. ואני תמה הוא פירוש רש"י בסוטה דף ל"ט על ובפתחו וכשהתחיל הרי דפתחו הוא לשון התחלה וזה נראה דעת הרמב"ם הנזכר לעיל:
**** ENGLISH ****
[3] <b>Once the reader began, etc.</b> This implies that the prohibition does not begin immediately upon opening the Sefer Torah. Lechem Chamudos, folio 44, likewise rules this way. Malbushei Yom Tov writes: “I do not know what Levush saw that led him to disagree. Although he inferred from the verse, ‘When he opened it,’ that the prohibition begins as soon as it is opened, one may say that there was no interruption between the opening and the reading. If there is an interruption, however, it is permitted until the reading begins.” I am astonished, for Rashi in Sotah 39a explains “when he opened it” to mean “when he began,” so the word “opened” denotes beginning. This also appears to be the view of Rambam cited above.

**** ENGLISH ****
[4] <b>Between one aliyah and the next, etc.</b> So writes Rabbeinu Yonah. Bach disagrees and permits even studying aloud between one aliyah and the next, since no one is then listening whose concentration he might disturb. I have not understood his reasoning, for the concern is that the study may continue while the Torah is being read, just as with the matters in the following seif, as Beit Yosef writes. Be’er Sheva, page 57, asks from the permission granted when the Sefer Torah has been opened but the reading has not yet begun. There is no difficulty. If we permit study between every aliyah, a person’s speech may become habitual and continue even during the reading. Before the reading begins, however, he knows to stop when the reading starts. This is implied by Beit Yosef and is also written by Lechem Chamudos there. Bach himself concludes that it is proper to be concerned for Rabbeinu Yonah and that this was his own practice. However, Seder HaYom writes that one may study privately between one aliyah and the next. It appears that the same applies to issuing a practical ruling for the moment, since there is no concern that his speech will become habitual and continue. Beit Yosef implies that according to all opinions one may read the verse twice and once in translation between one aliyah and the next. Lechem Chamudos, however, is inclined to require caution even here, lest one become confused because it is impossible to ensure that he completes the verse each time the reader begins. I say: what is wrong if he interrupts even in the middle of a verse? His further concern, that because he is listening to the reading he may not know where to stop, is also not significant. Presumably, in case of doubt he will begin again from the place he knows he reached, as above in siman 64. It is also possible to be careful not to forget the place. Nevertheless, I do not think one should be lenient except where Torah is his occupation. For such a person, that status helps even nowadays. So it appears to me. Rashi’s commentary appears to have the same status here as the translation, as stated below in siman 285; see there, seif katan 8. Levush writes that the requirement of silence is derived from the verse, “When he opened it, all the people stood,” where “stood” means silence. If it meant literal standing, Scripture says, “And you, stand here with Me,” which applies specifically to the intermediary and the reader, not to the entire people. Taz challenges him and writes that this is a fabrication not found in the Gemara. In my humble opinion, he did not examine Beit Yosef at the beginning of siman 141, where Levush’s explanation is explicit as the Gemara’s reasoning. This is also implied by Mordechai. As for practical halachah, Rama writes that some are stringent and stand, and Maharam did so. Bach writes likewise at the beginning of siman 141. Mateh Moshe writes that this is the custom, while Lechem Chamudos there implies that standing is required as a matter of law, not merely as a stringency, since silence is derived from “the ears of all the people,” as Levush writes. Responsa Menahem Azaryah, siman 91, likewise writes that it is required by law and derives it from the verse, “And the people stood in their places.” Between one aliyah and the next, however, the end of Masas Binyamin states that even those who sit during the reading must stand when the reader says “Bless Hashem,” because it is a davar shebikdushah. Responsa Menahem Azaryah writes likewise. Taz writes that they should not sit until they have first answered, “Blessed is Hashem, Who is blessed forever and ever.” All this differs from Magen Avraham, who writes in their name that everyone must stand during the blessings over the Torah. That is incorrect; the requirement applies only to Barchu and the response “Blessed,” etc. The remaining laws of standing will be explained at the end of siman 149. It also appears to me that one who studies between one aliyah and the next must be careful to stop, hear Barchu, and answer “Blessed is Hashem,” etc. Malbushei Yom Tov. Levush writes that although one may leave between one aliyah and the next, study is different, because the flow of discussion may continue, etc. This was explained in Eliyahu Zuta. I also wrote in the name of Shelah, page 256, that it is proper not to read along with the chazzan at all, but to listen silently. Mateh Moshe, however, writes that it is proper to read quietly along with the chazzan. Lechem Chamudos, page 5, and Perishah likewise imply this, provided that one is careful not to raise his voice at all. Mateh Moshe writes: Those who read along with the chazzan from a Chumash must take care not to raise their voices, but should remain silent and listen to the chazzan’s voice. Concerning them Scripture says, “If only you would heed My commandments, your peace would be like a river.”
