<b>אבל</b> עט״ז נראה שנוסחא ברמ״א היה אבל מי שאינו מתענג מותר לאומרה שיתענג חבירו וא״כ היל״ל אפילו מי כו׳ ובלבוש העתיק אסור לספרם כמו שהגי׳ לפנינו (בד״א ציינו אסור ואינו כי לפניו ז״ל היה הגי׳ מותר לאומרם) ועש״ר ב׳ כ״כ שנוסחא מוטעת היא. אבל הט״ז חילק אם מספר רק למלאות רצון חבירו אסור אבל אם מכוין שיהיה להם עונג שרי וכנראה שהסכים ללבוש דוקא אם אין לו נחת גם לא להתענג חביריו רק למלאות רצונם שיתענגו הם ודאי יש להמספר צער אסור אבל אם אין לו נחת רק עושה שיתענג חבירו אין להמספר צער ושרי ועא״ר. ולי העני יש בכאן הרהורי דברים והנני פורטם בעזה״י:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>But.</b> See Taz. It appears that the Rama's text read: “But one who does not derive pleasure from it may relate it so that his fellow will derive pleasure.” If so, however, it should have said, “Even one who,” etc. The Levush copied the text as, “It is forbidden to relate them,” as in the corrected reading before us. In the abbreviated gloss we noted “forbidden,” but this is incorrect, for the reading before the Levush was, “It is permitted to relate them.” Another authority likewise writes that the text is corrupt. The Taz, however, distinguishes as follows: if he relates the matters merely to satisfy his fellow's wishes, it is forbidden; but if his intention is that they derive pleasure, it is permitted. It appears that he agrees with the Levush only where the speaker himself has no satisfaction, nor is his purpose that his fellows derive pleasure, but merely to comply with their wish. Since relating the matter then causes the speaker distress, it is forbidden. If, however, he derives no satisfaction himself but relates it so that his fellow will derive pleasure, the speaker suffers no distress and it is permitted. See Eliyah Rabbah. In my humble opinion, this matter requires consideration, and with Hashem's help I shall set out my thoughts in detail.

<b>Know</b> that the prohibition of speaking on Shabbos has three categories. The first is speech that has no connection to melachah, but is prohibited because one's speech on Shabbos may not resemble one's weekday speech, such as saying, “Tomorrow I shall do such and such.” Rambam writes in chapter 24, halachah 1, that these matters neither resemble melachah nor lead to melachah, but concern attending to one's affairs and speaking about them. They are discussed in siman 306 and in this siman, and the Sages linked them to the verse in Yeshayahu 58, “refraining from pursuing your affairs and speaking of matters.” Tosafot on Shabbos 150a, s.v. “But is speech forbidden,” appears to ask whether speech is forbidden according to the Torah. Their intent is that speech is certainly forbidden, as is evident from several baraisos and mishnayos there, including the rule that one may not hire workers. Rashi explains this from “refraining from pursuing your affairs.” Tosafot therefore understand that the verse itself teaches the prohibition and that it is according to the Torah, as Tzemach Tzedek writes in siman 25 concerning oneg Shabbos, see siman 242, because the verse concludes, “for the mouth of Hashem has spoken.” Tosafot ask that, if so, why is speech for a mitzvah permitted? They answer that the Torah itself interprets the verse: “your affairs” are forbidden, while affairs of Heaven are permitted, both in action and in speech.

One might ask that instructing a non-Jew to perform melachah is only a D'Rabbanan prohibition, whereas it should already be forbidden according to the Torah by “speaking of matters.” The answer is that this distinction applies even when one tells him to light a lamp so that one can learn by its light, which is an affair of Heaven. Nevertheless, as stated below in seif 5 and in siman 276, seif 3, one may not instruct a non-Jew to perform a melachah prohibited according to the Torah even for a mitzvah. The principle of weekday speech is that whatever one may not do today, one may not say that he will do tomorrow. Where there is some possible permitted manner, however, such as by means of guard huts or the like, speech and involvement are permitted, as stated in seif 8 here and siman 306, seif 1. This applies even where the act would otherwise involve a Torah prohibition. One may say, “Tomorrow I shall travel beyond three parsahs.” Do not say that guard huts are effective only regarding a D'Rabbanan techum, for this is incorrect. See Beit Yosef and simanim 263 and 317. The Gemara's kal vachomer concerning bringing produce likewise shows that even regarding a Torah prohibition, speech is permitted whenever a permitted manner is conceivable.

Is only speech addressed to another prohibited, or is speaking to oneself also forbidden? See Magen Avraham, seif katan 11, who rules that even speaking privately to oneself is forbidden; only thought is permitted. Tosafot on Shabbos 150a, s.v. “What,” write the same, for otherwise the Gemara's question would not arise.

Our Sages also said that one may not engage excessively in idle conversation on Shabbos. Rambam writes this in chapter 24, halachah 4, based on the Yerushalmi, and Tosafot cite it on Shabbos 113b. This raises a difficulty. Idle speech that serves no purpose is forbidden even during the week, for whoever engages in it violates the positive commandment of “you shall speak of them,” meaning words of Torah and not idle matters. Why, then, did the Sages specifically prohibit excessive idle conversation on Shabbos? One might answer that women, who are not obligated in Torah study, may engage in idle conversation. Yet Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai's statement, “My mother, today is Shabbos,” and the unqualified language of the poskim imply that this warning applies to men as well. According to the Rama's ruling, however, the matter is clear. During the week one may relate reports from which one's fellow derives pleasure. On Shabbos, when the speaker himself derives no pleasure from relating them, it is forbidden. See the Yerushalmi and its commentary. The Yerushalmi states that Shabbos contains thirty-five forms of shevus, twenty-five of which arise from speech.

The second category is instructing a non-Jew. I have explained that this is forbidden even for a mitzvah. Whatever one may tell another Jew, one may also tell a non-Jew; see Magen Avraham, seif katan 10. If one tells the non-Jew to perform the act on Shabbos, this involves a D'Rabbanan shevus, lest one come to perform it himself, and because the non-Jew appears to act as his agent. One is liable to disciplinary lashes, as Rambam writes at the beginning of chapter 6 of Hilchos Shabbos. If, however, one tells him to perform the melachah after Shabbos, the prohibition is only that of “refraining from pursuing your affairs and speaking of matters,” and it is possible that disciplinary lashes are not imposed. We discussed this in siman 306. Even where a permitted manner could theoretically be created through guard huts or an eruv, it is nevertheless forbidden to tell a non-Jew to perform the act on Shabbos, because the decree still applies, as Magen Avraham writes in seif katan 12. A double shevus is permitted for a mitzvah, as stated in seif 5 and siman 586, seif 21. One might suggest that instructing a non-Jew to go beyond the techum is forbidden even for a mitzvah because the prohibition was linked to a verse and is therefore more stringent than other forms of shevus. This would explain siman 306, seif 9, which forbids instructing a non-Jew to go beyond the techum even though the matter concerns a mitzvah; see Magen Avraham there, seif katan 17. The general language of the poskim, however, does not support this distinction.

The third category is instructing a Jewish apostate, who is treated like a non-Jew in this regard. The issue is not limited to the prohibition of placing a stumbling block before the blind. Consider an innkeeper who cooks his own food for travelers and happens to host on Shabbos a mildly ill person whose condition is not dangerous. One might argue that instructing a non-Jew is permitted for a sick person who is not dangerously ill, and that placing a stumbling block before the blind does not apply because the innkeeper cooks several pots in any event for possible guests. Nevertheless, in siman 304, on Magen Avraham seif katan 1, we wrote that the matter depends upon a dispute. According to the Rama in Choshen Mishpat 388:15, if one instructs a person known to act as an informer, the sender is liable, because the principle, “Whose words should be obeyed, the Master's or the disciple's?” does not apply to one habitually prepared to sin. All the more so here, where he acts at the sender's instruction, and it is possible that the sender would be liable to stoning. According to the Levush, however, the liability there is based on indirect causation because the resulting damage is certain, which would not establish liability for the sender here. The Shach there also questions the Rama. We discussed this in the general introduction, and with Hashem's help the laws of instructing a non-Jew will be explained further below. See siman 276, seif 1. With Hashem's help I shall explain more in paragraph 4.

<b>Although he understands.</b> See Taz. Regarding the case of “light it,” one may answer that the Sages permitted it because of financial loss, but without such loss it would be forbidden. As for hinting on Friday that the act should be done the next day, although speech is forbidden either by the words of the Sages or according to the Torah through “speaking of matters,” as explained in paragraph 1, the Torah permitted thought. See Shabbos 103a. Instructing a non-Jew, however, is a D'Rabbanan shevus and is more stringent in this respect. <b>The ordinary writer says:</b> I have a small question here, which I shall present, and through it several other details will also be clarified. It is obvious to me that instructing a non-Jew on Shabbos is a shevus even where the act itself involves only an ordinary negative prohibition. For example, if a Jew's load is upon a non-Jew's donkey, it is forbidden to tell the non-Jew to lead it. Do not argue that this should be a double shevus and therefore more lenient, for it is all included in one decree, while the underlying prohibition of Shabbos carries kares and stoning. From this I derive the law discussed in Yoreh Deah 297:4 and Even HaEzer 5:14, based on Bava Metzia 90a. The Gemara resolves that regarding every prohibition involving a negative commandment, it is forbidden to instruct a non-Jew to perform it. We rule that a descendant of Noach is not commanded concerning castration, and the case of “those bulls,” etc., must therefore be prohibited because a Jew instructed the non-Jew. All the more so is a Shabbos shevus forbidden where the underlying act violates a negative commandment.

It is also obvious to me that regarding other prohibitions, a double shevus may be permitted. The Gemara's question and resolution necessarily concerned instructing a non-Jew to violate an actual negative commandment, not a double shevus, and one cannot derive a stringency for other prohibitions from Shabbos, which is more severe. I remain uncertain, however, whether instructing a non-Jew is forbidden regarding an affirmative prohibition in other areas of halachah. The Rama's language in Yoreh Deah refers specifically to an act prohibited by a negative commandment. This implies that an affirmative prohibition, which is more lenient than a negative commandment, is not included. As the Sages state at the beginning of Yevamos, a negative commandment is more stringent than an affirmative commandment. We therefore have no source to prohibit it.

Accordingly, after the sixth hour on Erev Pesach, it would be permitted to tell a non-Jew to purchase wine or the like for a Jew with the Jew's money and to keep the chametz in the non-Jew's possession until after Pesach. A non-Jew cannot serve as a halachic agent, and the chametz does not belong to the Jew. Whether one may instruct a non-Jew after the sixth hour depends upon the dispute whether one then violates bal yera'eh and bal yimatzei, or only the affirmative commandment of tashbisu. Most authorities agree that even in Rambam's view only the affirmative commandment applies at that time. The question is therefore whether the Sages did not prohibit instructing a non-Jew where the underlying prohibition is only affirmative. Yom Tov and Chol HaMoed may be different, because they are called Shabbos, as Tosafot write there in Bava Metzia.

<b>ואגרת</b> עט״ז הנה דבריו בנוים עפ״י מ״ש הב״ח ז״ל דשמא ימחוק ל״ש בשמש שאין בידו למחוק רק בעה״ב וז״ש מונה אדם אורחי״ו ולא מכתב אבל החשש שטרי הדיוטות יש בשמש לרש״י דטעם ממצוא חפצך כו׳ ולהרמב״ם ורמב״ן מותר באג״ש ה״ה משמש בנעטי״ל אין לגעור בו כמ״ש הב״י בשם הרשב״א הסומך על הרמב״ן אין לגעור בו ומ״מ קשה אג״ש מה שייך ממצוא חפצך דכל דבר שאין בו איסור לעשות ע״י ציור מותר לומר בשבת לכרך פלוני כו׳ כ״ש לקרות אג״ש ועא״ר ל״א ובח״מ דלרמב״ם י״ל דאסור לג״ש רק לרמב״ן מותר אג״ש יע״ש ובמ״א אי״ה יבואר עוד. דהעיון ג״כ אסרו דא״א שלא יאמר בפה:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And a letter.</b> See Taz. His words are based upon Bach’s statement that the concern lest one erase applies only to the homeowner, who has authority to erase, and not to an attendant, who lacks such authority. This is the meaning of, “A person may count his guests from memory, but not from a written list.” The concern regarding ordinary business documents, however, applies even to an attendant according to Rashi, who derives the prohibition from “refraining from pursuing your affairs.” According to Rambam and Ramban, a letter of greeting is permitted. The same therefore applies to an attendant on Nittel night, and one should not rebuke him, as Beit Yosef writes in the name of Rashba: one who relies upon Ramban should not be rebuked. Nevertheless, the ruling regarding a letter of greeting is difficult. How does “refraining from pursuing your affairs” apply? Any matter whose performance involves no prohibition may be discussed on Shabbos, such as saying that one will go to a certain city; all the more so should reading a letter of greeting be permitted. See Eliyah Rabbah, seif katan 31. In Machatzis HaShekel it is explained that according to Rambam one may say that even a letter of greeting is forbidden, whereas according to Ramban it is permitted; see there. With Hashem’s help, this will be explained further in Magen Avraham. They also forbade examining it, because it is impossible that one will not articulate something aloud.

<b>בכדי</b> עט״ז ועיין ב״מ צדיק א׳ הלין תירין דגנבין ארמאי והנה שבת לאחר שבת היה באפשר לעשות בהיתר לא קנסו משא״כ סירוס לעולם א״א ע״י ישראל בהיתר קנסו אלא המרכיב כלאים ע״י עכו״ם אפ״ה אין קונסין עט״ז י״ד רצ״ז אות ב׳ דמצוי הוא סירוס קנסו לא בכלאי בהמה וב״ש אה״ע סי׳ ה׳ אות י״ז בשם דרישה סירוס ניכר האיסור קנסו משא״כ כלאי בהמה אין ניכר לא קנסו ולומר סירוס קנסו הא כלאים דרבנן טמא עם טמא וטהור עם טהור (כי לא מתעבר בטמא וטהור ולאסור הבהמה במה שלא נהנה כ״כ י״ל דלא קנסו) ז״א דע״כ קאמר הר״מ ז״ל בהנהגה דטמא עם טמא דרבנן הא ברביעה מד״ת ועש״ך י״ד רצ״ז א׳ וכבר עמדנו על זה בג״ו באורך יע״ש וכאן אין להאריך. וע׳ ר״מ ז״ל פרק ששי מהלכות שבת הלכה ח׳ כתב בפירוש אע״פ שאמר ישראל לעכו״ם לעשות ולוקה מכל מקום מותר לערב בכדי שיעשו ועמ״א שם שלא מצינו איסור זה בגמרא יע״ש:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Until enough time has elapsed for it to be done.</b> See Taz, and see Bava Metzia 90a regarding those oxen that a non-Jew castrated. Here, from one Shabbos to the next, the act could have been performed permissibly, and therefore the Sages did not impose a penalty. Castration, by contrast, can never be performed permissibly by a Jew, and therefore they imposed a penalty. Yet when one causes animals to mate in violation of kilayim through a non-Jew, no penalty is imposed. See Taz, Yoreh Deah 297, seif katan 2, who explains that castration is common and therefore the Sages imposed a penalty, whereas crossbreeding animals is not common. Beis Shmuel, Even HaEzer 5, seif katan 17, writes in the name of Derishah that with castration the prohibited act is recognizable, and therefore the Sages imposed a penalty, whereas with crossbreeding animals it is not recognizable, and therefore they did not. One cannot explain that they imposed a penalty for castration because crossbreeding an impure animal with another impure animal, or a pure animal with another pure animal, is only D’Rabbanan. An impure animal cannot conceive from a pure one; and perhaps they did not forbid the animal where the owner did not derive substantial benefit. This explanation is untenable, because Rambam necessarily refers to leading an impure animal together with another impure animal, which is D’Rabbanan, whereas causing them to mate is prohibited according to the Torah. See Shach, Yoreh Deah 297, seif katan 1. We have already discussed this at length in the general introduction; see there. This is not the place to elaborate. See also Rambam, chapter 6 of Hilchos Shabbos, halachah 8, who writes explicitly that although a Jew instructed a non-Jew to perform the act and receives disciplinary lashes, it is nevertheless permitted after Shabbos once enough time has elapsed for it to have been done. See Magen Avraham there, who writes that we have not found this prohibition in the Gemara; see there.
