<b>(ס"ק א') אעשה למחר אפי' ד"מ ס"ח.</b> ובספר א"ר הקשה למה יגרע מהאי דסי' ש"ו דבחפצי שמים מותר לדבר בשבת ובספר ת"ש תירץ דשאני האי דסי' ש"ו דיש צורך בדבורו היום ופה מיירי דאין צורך בדבורו היום עיין שם אלא שבספר א"ר כ' דאין ענין מ"ש בס"ח למ"ש מ"א וז"ל עיינתי בספר חסידים דמיירי כשאומר לישן כדי לכתוב ס"ת למחר דאז אין תענוג השינה בשביל כבוד השבת אבל אומר שיעשה מצוה למחר מנ"ל עכ"ל ואני עיינתי בס"ח וראיתי שכ' וז"ל בשבת לא יאמר אדם נישן כדי שנעשה מלאכתינו במ"ש שאסור לומר למחר אעשה כך ואפי' חפץ לכתוב תורה מפני שמראה מה שישן ונח בשבת שעושה בשביל החול אלא יאמר לנוח כי שבת היום עכ"ל ונראה שהוא טעות וראוי לכתוב בהיפך כי לפי מ"ש לפנינו קשה איך נתן טעם על מ"ש לא יאמר אדם נישן כדי כו' שאסור לו לומר כו'. ות"ל מה שסיים בסוף מפני שמראה כו' שעושה בשביל החול וגם למה הוצרך בסוף ליתן עוד טעם מפני שמראה כו' הלא כבר אמר שאסור לומר כו' אע"כ שהדברים נדפסו בטעות וכצ"ל לא יאמר כו' כדי שנעשה מלאכתינו במ"ש מפני מה שמראה שישן כו' בשביל החול אלא יאמר לנוח כי שבת היום. ואסור לומר למחר אעשה כך ואפי' חפץ לכתוב ס"ת ובזה אתיא הכל נכון:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>(s.k. 1) I will do it tomorrow, even according to Darkei Moshe in siman 306.</b> In Eliyah Rabbah he asks why this should be inferior to the case in siman 306, where one may speak on Shabbos about matters of a mitzvah. In Tevuos Shor he answers that the case in siman 306 is different, because there is a need for his words today, whereas here the discussion concerns a case in which there is no need for his words today; see there. However, Eliyah Rabbah writes that what is stated in Sefer Chasidim has no connection to what Magen Avraham wrote. He states: "I examined Sefer Chasidim, which discusses one who says that he is going to sleep in order to write a sefer Torah tomorrow. In that case the pleasure of sleeping is not for the honor of Shabbos. But from where do we know that one may say that he will perform a mitzvah tomorrow?" End quote. I examined Sefer Chasidim and saw that it states as follows: "On Shabbos a person should not say, 'Let us sleep so that we can do our work after Shabbos,' because it is forbidden to say, 'Tomorrow I will do such-and-such.' This applies even if he wishes to write Torah, because he thereby demonstrates that his sleeping and resting on Shabbos are for the sake of the weekday. Rather, he should say, 'Let us rest, for today is Shabbos.'" End quote. It appears that there is a textual error and that the passage should be written in the reverse order. According to the text before us, it is difficult to understand why, after stating that a person should not say, 'Let us sleep so that we can do our work after Shabbos,' it gives the reason that it is forbidden for him to say, 'Tomorrow I will do such-and-such.' It could instead have given the reason stated at the end, that he thereby demonstrates that he is doing this for the sake of the weekday. Furthermore, why was it necessary to add at the end the reason that he thereby demonstrates this, after it had already stated that it is forbidden to say such a thing? Rather, the passage must have been printed in error, and it should read as follows: "A person should not say, 'Let us sleep so that we can do our work after Shabbos,' because he thereby demonstrates that he is sleeping for the sake of the weekday. Rather, he should say, 'Let us rest, for today is Shabbos.' It is also forbidden to say, 'Tomorrow I will do such-and-such,' even if he wishes to write a sefer Torah." With this reading, everything is correct.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>It is explicitly implied that this applies even if he did not tell him to purchase it, etc.</b> The words in Hagahos Ashri, "that he should purchase it for him tomorrow," are not words spoken by the Jew to the non-Jew. Rather, they are the words of Hagahos Ashri itself. The Jew's words to the non-Jew were unstated. Nevertheless, we know on our own that the Jew intends him to purchase it tomorrow, because the discussion concerns villages and small towns where purchases are ordinarily available only on market day. The words, "for this is comparable to stipulating, etc.," are from Hagahos Maimoniyos, chapter 6, and complete its statement cited above. In the case of siman 252, the non-Jew can, if he wishes, refrain from doing the work until tomorrow. Concerning this it concludes that the case is comparable to stipulating that he rest. That is to say, at the beginning of siman 248 it is stated that one may set sail on a ship, provided that he stipulates with the non-Jew that he rest on Shabbos. If the non-Jew subsequently does not rest, that is of no consequence; see siman 248. The case in siman 252 is likewise comparable to that case.

**** ENGLISH ****
He writes that on Wednesday and Thursday it is permitted. Eliyah Rabbah leaves this ruling requiring study, for certainly, if the Jew explicitly tells the non-Jew to purchase or sell on Shabbos, it is forbidden even on Wednesday or Thursday. The same should therefore apply when selling or purchasing is available only on Shabbos, which is market day. Even on Wednesday or Thursday it should be forbidden, because it is as though he expressly told him to purchase it on Shabbos. This is not comparable to siman 252, for there the non-Jew is not compelled to perform the work on Shabbos; see there. Eliyah Rabbah further comments on the Taz's statement that, even in a case that is permitted because the Jew did not tell the non-Jew to perform the work on Shabbos, the Jew may nevertheless not benefit from it, since the non-Jew in any event performed it for the Jew, which is comparable to a non-Jew who lit a lamp for a Jew, etc. Eliyah Rabbah writes that siman 252 explicitly states that one may wear a garment whose work a non-Jew completed on Shabbos, and that this is not comparable to siman 276 concerning the lighting of a lamp, as Magen Avraham writes in siman 252, s.k. 11. These are his words. However, Rama above in siman 252 writes that one should be stringent and not wear it. Yet if it is uncertain whether the non-Jew completed it on Shabbos or before Shabbos, it is explained there that according to all opinions it is permitted.

<b>עכ"ד מ"מ כו'</b> ר"ל כל מ"ש בס"ק זה מריש דבריו שכתב דאלו בחולה ממש כו' ע"כ הכל היא דברי מ"מ והמ"מ כתב דבריו ליישב השגת הראב"ד שכ' ע"ד הרמב"ם וז"ל א"א אם היה מקצת חולה הוא עצמו עושה שהרי גונח יונק חלב בשבת כו' וכן אמרו במקום הפסד כגון צנור שעלו בו קשקשים ממעכן ברגלו ועז"כ מ"מ דבריו ליישב בקוצר ומ"א כתב דבריו בביאור ע"ש וז"ל המ"מ כוונת הרמב"ם כאן שאין כאן חולי כולל כל הגוף ולא סכנת אבר אלא קצת צער וזה שכתב מקצת חולי או מצטער ועוד שזה מה שעושה בו הוא כדרכו עכ"ל. ומשמע מדבריו שהיה גורס כו'. אבל מדברי הראב"ד משמע דגריס ליה כו' ר"ל דהראב"ד ע"כ גריס ברמב"ם או יהיה צריך לדבר צורך הרבה דאל"כ אלא לא היה גורס אלא מקצת חולי או מצוה ותמורות צריך לדבר צורך הרבה היה גרסתו או מצטער אם כן מאי קשיא לראב"ד הא במקום הפסד הוא עצמו הותר לו שבות כדאשכחן גבי צנור כו' מאי ענין פסידא לכאן דהא הרמב"ם לא דיבד כלל מענין פסידא א"ו דהיה גורס כמו שכ' בש"ע או שהיה צריך לדבר צורך הרבה והיינו אם לא נתיר לו שבות ע"י גוי יהי' לו הפסד ולכן הוקשה לראב"ד ב' קושי' א' על מ"ש מקצת חולי כו' ק"ל מגונח דמותר לישראל עצמו לעבור שבות ועל צריך לדבר הרבה דהיינו פסידא הקשה מהאי דצנור אבל המ"מ ע"כ לא גרס צריך לדבר הרבה אלא תמורתו גרס מצטער א"כ א"צ ליישב הקושי' מצנור כ"א הקושי' מגונח ולכן פי' תחלה דברי הרמב"ם קודם שבא ליישב השגת הראב"ד וכ' דרמב"ם דייק בלישני' וכתב מקצת חולי כו' דאלו חולי גמור שכולל כל הגוף או אפי' סכנת אבר אפי' ישראל עושה ואח"ז כתב ליישב השגת הראב"ד מגונח והם סיום דברי המ"מ ועוד שזה מה שעושה בו כדרכו משא"כ בהאי דגונח אע"ג דגם גונח ליכ' אלא צער בעלמא אפ"ה הותר שבות לישראל דשאני התם דהוי שבות שלא כדרכו משא"כ כאן דהוי שבות כדרכו מדלא הזכיר המ"מ בדבריו כלל ליישב האי דצנור ניהו דבדברי המ"מ ממילא מיושב גם ההשגה מצנור ולגרסת הראב"ד או שהיה צריך כו' דגם בצנור הוי שבות שלא כדרכה מ"מ א"א דהמ"מ הוי גריס או שהיה צריך כו' וא"כ צריך ליישב גם ההשגה מצנור ה"ל מ"מ להזכירו בתירוצו:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>These are the words of the Maggid Mishneh, etc.</b> That is to say, everything written in this seif katan from the beginning, where Magen Avraham states, "But in the case of an actual illness, etc.," until this point, is from the Maggid Mishneh. The Maggid Mishneh wrote these words to resolve the Raavad's objection to the Rambam. The Raavad writes: "This is untenable. If there is a slight illness, the person himself may perform the act, for one who is groaning may suck milk directly from an animal on Shabbos, etc. They likewise permitted this in a case of financial loss, such as a drainpipe in which vegetation had grown, which one may crush with his foot." Magen Avraham cites the Maggid Mishneh's brief resolution and then explains it. The Maggid Mishneh writes: "The Rambam's intent here is that there is no illness affecting the entire body and no danger to a limb, but only some pain. This is why he writes, 'a slight illness or one who is in pain.' Furthermore, the act performed for him is done in its ordinary manner." End quote. It is implied from his words that his text read, etc. However, the Raavad's words imply that his text read, etc. This means that the Raavad necessarily had the Rambam's wording, "or if there is a great need for the matter." Otherwise, if his text had read only, "a slight illness or a mitzvah," and in place of "a great need for the matter" it had read, "or one who is in pain," what difficulty would the Raavad have raised from the fact that, in a case of financial loss, the person himself may perform a rabbinic prohibition, as we find regarding the drainpipe? Financial loss would have no relevance here, since the Rambam would not have discussed financial loss at all. Rather, the Raavad must have had the reading found in the Shulchan Aruch, "or if there is a great need for the matter." This means that, if we do not permit a rabbinic prohibition to be performed through a non-Jew, the Jew will suffer a financial loss. The Raavad therefore raised two objections. Against the Rambam's words, "a slight illness, etc.," he objected from the case of one who is groaning, where a Jew himself may violate a rabbinic prohibition. Against the words, "a great need for the matter," meaning a financial loss, he objected from the case of the drainpipe. The Maggid Mishneh, however, necessarily did not have the reading "a great need for the matter," but instead read "one who is in pain." He therefore needed to resolve only the objection from one who is groaning, not the objection from the drainpipe. For this reason, before addressing the Raavad's objection, he first explained the Rambam's words and wrote that the Rambam was precise in his language when he wrote, "a slight illness, etc." In the case of a full illness affecting the entire body, or even danger to a limb, a Jew himself may perform the act. He then addressed the Raavad's objection from one who is groaning. This is the conclusion of the Maggid Mishneh's words: "Furthermore, the act performed for him is done in its ordinary manner." This is unlike the case of one who is groaning. Although there too there is only pain, a Jew is nevertheless permitted to perform the rabbinic prohibition, because there it is performed in an unusual manner, whereas here it is performed in its ordinary manner. The Maggid Mishneh does not mention the case of the drainpipe at all. Although his explanation also implicitly resolves the objection from the drainpipe according to the Raavad's reading, "or if there is a great need, etc.," since the act with the drainpipe is likewise performed in an unusual manner, it is nevertheless impossible that the Maggid Mishneh had the reading, "or if there is a great need, etc." Had he read it that way, he would also have needed to resolve the objection from the drainpipe, and he should have mentioned it in his answer.

Even so, the Maggid Mishneh may agree that when one merely says regarding himself, “Tomorrow I will perform such-and-such an act,” without asking another person to do anything for him, it is still forbidden unless there is some manner in which the act could be permitted today, such as by means of guard huts or the like. See Tosafot. That is, Tosafot likewise imply that even saying, “Tomorrow I will perform such-and-such an act,” is forbidden. First, Tosafot on Shabbos 150a, s.v. “What difference is there,” address the Gemara’s question on the Mishnah: “A person may not hire workers on Shabbos, nor may he tell his fellow to hire workers for him.” The Gemara asks: What difference is there between himself and his fellow? Rashi explains that his fellow is also a Jew like himself and therefore cannot hire them. Tosafot ask: “Why not say that ‘his fellow’ means that he tells him, ‘Hire workers for me tomorrow’?” They answer: “If so, why mention his fellow? Even he himself may not say, ‘I will hire workers tomorrow.’ The statement that one may say, etc., ‘I am going tomorrow to such-and-such a city,’ applies only because, were there guard huts, etc.” Tosafot write the same explicitly there on 150b, s.v. “But one may wait at the edge of the Shabbos boundary,” as follows: “I know of such-and-such a place, etc. One might have thought that it is permitted to speak about one’s own plans, even to say that he will hire workers tomorrow, etc., because his words accomplish nothing. But according to Shmuel this cannot be so, for all such statements are forbidden, even concerning oneself, except where the act could be performed today, such as where there might be guard huts, etc.”

<b>(Seif katan 12) Outside the Shabbos boundary, but within the boundary, etc.</b> The Bach asks why the ruling specifies produce outside the Shabbos boundary. Even within the boundary it should be forbidden, since the produce may not be moved and therefore cannot be brought. The first clause therefore also speaks of produce outside the boundary.

<b>(Seif katan 17) A sunken engraving, but a raised engraving, etc.</b> The concern is that one may come to read ordinary documents, for the Sages decreed because they held that writing on a wall may be confused with a document. If we permit reading what is written on a wall, one may also read what is written on paper or in a document. When there is an additional distinction, namely that the writing is engraved below the surface, there are two differences: it is on a wall, and the writing is sunken. Such writing is unlike ordinary writing, and therefore will not be confused with documents. By contrast, with a tablet or ledger, even when the engraving is sunken, the Sages decreed lest one read ordinary documents, because there is only one difference, namely that the writing is sunken. A tablet or ledger resembles a document in that it is movable, so there is no distinction in that respect, as the Magen Avraham writes immediately afterward.
