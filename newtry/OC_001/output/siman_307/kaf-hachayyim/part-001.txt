<b>א) [סעיף א'] ודבר דבר שלא יהא דיבורך של שבת וכו'  </b>מוז"ל היה נזהר מאד שלא לדבר בלשון לעז ביום שבת לא בלילה ולא ביום זולתי כשהיה אומר לנו איזה דרוש והיה צריך לאומרו בלשון לעז כדי שיבינוהו העם השומעים. גם היה נזהר שלא לדבר שיחה בטילה או דיבור של חול אפי' בלשון הקודש וכן היה נוהג בכל היו"ט כמו בשבת. שער הכוו' דף ע"ד ע"ד. פע"ח שער ח"י סוף פ' כ"א:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>1) [Seif 1] 'And speak a word,' so that your speech on Shabbos shall not be, etc.</b> My teacher, of blessed memory, was exceedingly careful not to speak a foreign language on Shabbos, either at night or during the day, except when he delivered a teaching that had to be given in the vernacular so that the listeners would understand. He was also careful not to engage in idle conversation or weekday speech, even in the holy tongue. He followed the same practice on every Yom Tov as on Shabbos. Shaar HaKavanos, folio 74d; Pri Etz Chaim, gate 18, end of chapter 21.

<b>י) אסור  </b>לאדם להלך בחפציו בשבת ואפי' לדבר בהן כגון שידבר עם שותפו מה ימכור למחר או מה יקנה או האיך יבנה בית זה ובאיזה סחורה ילך למקום פלוני כל זה וכיוצא בו אסור. הרמב"ם פכ"ד דין א' וכ"כ בסמ"ג. והב"ד א"ר או' ב' וכתב דשותפו דנקט אורחא דמילתא וה"ה לאחר כמ"ש רמ"א סעי' ח' וכן בכלבו השמיט שותפו ונראה נמי אפי' לא אמר לאחר כלל אלא בפני עצמו כדמשמע מסתימת הש"ע ולבוש ומהר"ש וכלבו אלא דמסתמא אומר לאחר דמה ליה לדבר בפני עצמו יעו"ש:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>10) A person may not</b> pursue his affairs on Shabbos or even speak about them, such as discussing with his partner what they will sell or purchase the next day, how they will build a house, or with which merchandise they will travel to a particular place. All this and similar matters are forbidden. Rambam, chapter 24, halachah 1. Sefer Mitzvos Gadol writes the same. Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 2, cites them and writes that Rambam mentions one's partner because this is the ordinary case, but the same law applies to anyone else, as Rema writes in seif 8. Kol Bo likewise omits 'his partner.' It also appears that this is forbidden even if he speaks to no one else but only to himself, as implied by the unqualified words of the Shulchan Aruch, Levush, Maharash, and Kol Bo. Ordinarily, however, he speaks to another person, because there is little reason to speak to himself; see there.

<b>יא) שם בהגה. מותר לספרם בשבת וכו'  </b>לא משנת חסידים היא והנשמר מלדבר דברי חול קדוש יאמר לו ואנשי מעשה נזהרין בזה שלא לדבר אפי' הכרחיים כ"א בלשון הקודש וקבלתי אדם המבקר לחבירו בשבת בשחרית לא יאמר לו כדרך שאומר בחול צפרא טבא רק יאמר לו שבת טבא לקיים זכיר את יום השבת לקדשו. של"ה. א"ר או' ג' וכ"כ המחב"ר או' א' דהוראה זו עיילוה למערתא דחסידי ולא קבלוה הגאון החסיד בעל של"ה ואחריו נמשך הרב החסיד בעל דעת חכמה פי"א דף י"ב ע"ב ואמור רבנן קדישי הנ"ז דלפי' ר"ת ופסקו הש"ע דאפי' שיחת חולין אסור להרבות גם זה אסור (וכצ"ל בש"ע ולא כמ"ש בדפוס ואפי' בשיחת דברים בטלים שהוא ט"ס אלא צ"ל ואפי' בשיחת חולין אסור להרבות) ומפורש בדברי הזוה"ק שהוא מחלל שבת כמ"ש הרב דעת חכמה שם בטוב טעם ודעת וכ"ש דמזה ימשך ודאי לדיבור אסור ובעל נפש יגדור גדר שלא יהיה בסוג מחלל שבת ח"ו. וריב לו להרב בעל דעת חכמה על מנהג שנהגו לבקר כתות כתות בש"ק לאוהביהם וכו' יעו"ש באורך דברי הקדושים חיים וקיימים עכ"ל ועיין לעיל או' א' שכ"כ בשם האר"י ז"ל:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>11) There, gloss: It is permitted to recount them on Shabbos, etc.</b> This is not the teaching of the pious. One who guards himself from weekday speech will be called holy. Scrupulous people take care not to speak even necessary matters except in the holy tongue. I received a tradition that when a person visits his friend on Shabbos morning, he should not say the weekday greeting tzafra tava, but rather Shabbos tava, thereby fulfilling, 'Remember the day of Shabbos to sanctify it.' Shelah; Eliyah Rabbah, paragraph 3. Machazik Berachah, paragraph 1, similarly writes that this permissive ruling was brought before the assembly of the pious, but the pious sage Shelah rejected it. He was followed by the pious author of Daas Chochmah, chapter 11, folio 12b. These holy sages said that according to Rabbeinu Tam's explanation and the Shulchan Aruch's ruling that even weekday conversation may not be excessive, this too is forbidden. This is how the Shulchan Aruch should read, rather than the printed phrase, 'Even idle conversation may not be excessive,' which is a scribal error. The sacred Zohar explicitly states that such speech desecrates Shabbos, as Daas Chochmah explains with sound reasoning. All the more so, it will certainly lead to forbidden speech. A conscientious person should establish a boundary so that he is not included, God forbid, among those who desecrate Shabbos. Daas Chochmah strongly challenges the custom of groups visiting friends on Shabbos, etc.; see his holy, enduring words at length. These are the words of Machazik Berachah. See above, paragraph 1, where we cited the same practice from the Arizal.

<b>טו) אסור  </b>לומר לעכו"ם שיאמר לחבירו עכו"ם לעשות מלאכה. מהר"ר גרשון אשכנזי בתשו' הובאה בשו"ת חו"י סי' מ"ט ונ"ג. וכן הסכים מהרי"ע בס' בני יהודה דף ס"ז ע"ב. ברכ"י או' ה' וכ"כ בס' שלחן עצי שטים סי' א' דישראל שאומר לעכו"ם. ועכו"ם לעכו"ם אחר הוי רק שבות אחד. וכ"כ בשו"ת שנות חיים סי' ל"ח וסי' קע"ה. אמנם בתפא"י על המשניות בכלכלת שבת בדמי אמירה לנכרי סי' ה' כתב דהוי שבות דשבות יעו"ש. ונ"מ לענין מ"ש לקמן סעי' ה' דשבות דשבות במקום מצוה או לצורך הרבה מותר יעו"ש. וא"כ כיון דרובא דרבוותא חשבה לה אמירת עכו"ם לחבירו לשבות אחת הכי נקטינן ורק אם יש עוד איזה צדדין להקל אפשר דיש לסמוך על המתירין ועיין שד"ח כללים מע' א' או' קע"ד:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>15) It is forbidden</b> to tell one non-Jew to tell another non-Jew to perform labor. Rabbi Gershon Ashkenazi in a responsum cited in Responsa Chavos Yair, simanim 49 and 53. Mahari Ayash agrees in Bnei Yehudah, folio 67b. Birkei Yosef, paragraph 5. Shulchan Atzei Shittim, siman 1, similarly writes that when a Jewish person tells a non-Jew, and that non-Jew tells another non-Jew, this constitutes only one rabbinic prohibition. Responsa Shenos Chaim, simanim 38 and 175, writes the same. Tiferes Yisrael on the Mishnah, in Kalkeles Shabbos, laws of instructing a non-Jew, siman 5, however, writes that it is a rabbinic prohibition compounded with another rabbinic prohibition; see there. A practical difference concerns the law below in seif 5 that a compounded rabbinic prohibition is permitted for a mitzvah or great need; see there. Since most great authorities consider instructing one non-Jew to instruct another a single rabbinic prohibition, this is the ruling we adopt. Only when additional grounds for leniency exist may it be possible to rely upon those who permit it. See Sedei Chemed, principles, system of alef, paragraph 174.

<b>17) There: Even telling him before Shabbos, etc.</b> This implies that it is forbidden even to instruct him to hire workers on Shabbos to build during the week, although hiring is only rabbinically forbidden. Responsa Rashba, siman 875, however, implies that on Friday one may tell a non-Jew to hire workers on Shabbos to build during the week. Rokeach, siman 88, and Rivash, siman 387, folio 297, write the same. Ri ibn Sasson, siman 156, similarly writes that this appears from Rambam's wording; see there. Radbaz, volume 1, siman 132, likewise writes that telling a non-Jew on Friday is forbidden only when one tells him to perform labor; see there. Erech HaShulchan, paragraph 3. See there.

<b>18) I saw</b> that Tashbetz, volume 2, siman 281, forbids telling a non-Jew to carry articles from one house to another without an eruv, even for a mitzvah and even when told before Shabbos; see there. This follows his own view that a compounded rabbinic prohibition was permitted only for bris milah, which overrides Shabbos, as he writes in siman 197, and not all authorities agree with Rambam; see there. According to our practice, however, that a compounded rabbinic prohibition is permitted for a mitzvah, it is permitted, especially when he is told before Shabbos, for some permit this even without a mitzvah. Radbaz similarly writes that by strict law one may tell a non-Jew on Friday to come play music the next day. Telling a non-Jew before Shabbos was forbidden only regarding labor, whereas here the Jewish person himself is prohibited only because of a decree. Some also permit telling a non-Jew to play music on Shabbos, and one should not extend the prohibition. See there. Although Rosh, cited by Beis Yosef in siman 517, forbids a similar case regarding pigeons for a Jewish person's needs, we rely upon these great authorities to permit it straightforwardly before Shabbos for a mitzvah. Even one who is stringent regarding a compounded rabbinic prohibition for a mitzvah would not be so stringent before Shabbos, especially in this case, for Hagahos Mordechai writes that in the public domain instructing a non-Jew is independently permitted because the article could be brought through human partitions; see there. See what is written in siman 655 in the name of Rabbi Yitzchak and Ritva, that instructing a non-Jew was not forbidden regarding a rabbinic prohibition that does not involve an action upon the object itself, such as carrying from one domain to another; see there. Erech HaShulchan there.

<b>19) There: But after Shabbos he may say, 'Why did you not do it,' etc.</b> His intent is that the non-Jew do it the following Shabbos. Sefer HaTerumah, Sefer Mitzvos Gadol, and Hagahos Maimoniyos, chapter 6, write this. Their words in their plain sense are difficult, because a complete Mishnah in the chapter Kol Kisvei teaches that if a non-Jew kindled a lamp on his own, a Jewish person may not benefit from it, and similar rulings appear below in siman 325. Ginas Veradim, klal 3, siman 21, questions this, followed by Rav Yaakov Castro; see there. Tosefta, chapter 14, teaches that when a familiar non-Jew acts for himself, benefit is forbidden because the Jewish person accustoms him to do so and he acts for the Jewish person on another Shabbos. How, then, could this be permitted? It therefore appears that one should be lenient only when the Jewish person's body derives no benefit, as Ginas Veradim writes, or when a lamp and fire already exist and the non-Jew merely adds to them, as Rav Yaakov Castro writes. Birkei Yosef, paragraph 6. Taz, paragraph 2, inclines to be lenient in his comments on Tur, but Mishbetzos Zahav, paragraph 2, questions him; see there. Accordingly, one should also not be lenient regarding any of Bach's permissive suggestions, such as telling a non-Jew, 'Wipe your nose,' so that he understands to remove the charred wick from the lamp; Knesses HaGedolah's suggestion in Hagahos HaTur, paragraph 2, of saying, 'The lamp is not giving light,' so that a maidservant understands and trims the wick; or saying, when one wants a book from a place without light, that the maidservant should bring another object from there, so that she kindles the lamp and he follows her and takes the book. Ikarei HaDat, siman 14, paragraph 100, writes the same in the name of Mor U'Ketziah; see there. Knesses HaGedolah, Hagahos HaTur, writes in the name of Shiltei Gibborim in the chapter Shoel that all opinions forbid hinting on Shabbos itself for a Shabbos need. Magen Avraham, paragraph 31, writes the same, contrary to Bach; see there. See below, paragraph 64. Some permit only saying, 'The house is dark,' whereupon the maidservant independently understands to remove the charred portion, as stated below in paragraph 55.
