**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Even according to the opinion that a manner of soiling is permitted.</b> Soaking the sheet in clean water for washing the deceased is not a manner of soiling. Magen Avraham therefore explains that the prohibition was stated because of squeezing, rather than relying upon laundering, since some maintain in siman 302, seif 9, that a clean sheet is not subject to laundering, and the sheet used for washing the deceased is presumably clean. Nevertheless, it remains forbidden because of squeezing. One must explain that although we rule that where there is no laundering and the extracted liquid is discarded we do not decree lest one squeeze, for there is no reason to squeeze either for cleansing or for use of the liquid, Magen Avraham nevertheless writes in siman 320, seif katan 23, in the name of Ran that squeezing in such a case is still prohibited D'Rabbanan. Accordingly, “lest he come to squeeze” here does not mean a decree lest he squeeze, which would be a decree upon a decree. Rather, he must take care not to reach a situation in which squeezing is certain or nearly certain, comparable to a psik reisha. Although he does not desire it, we do not rule like the Aruch, who permits such a case, as explained in siman 320. Magen Avraham writes similarly in siman 523, seif katan 2.

<b>(ס"ק ז') בי"ט כו'</b> שדרים שם יהודים רבים כו' דתנן במס' מ"ק דף ט' ע"ב וארון עם המת בחצר. ר"ל בחה"מ אסור לעשות ארון למת כ"א באותה חצר שהמת שם אבל לא בחצר אחרת ששם לא ידעו שיש מת ויחשדוהו שעושה לצורך דבר אחר ומה"ט כ' הטור בשם ר"י דמה שעושים לצורך המת בי"ט ב' יש לעשות בצנעה. די"ט שני ודאי לא קיל יותר מחה"מ. אולם אי' בירושלמי דבאדם מפורסם שמת אפי' בחצר אחר הואיל שנתפרס' מיתתו יודעים שלצרכו עושים. ומה"ט בזמן הזה שבכל עיר ועיר אין דרים כ"א יהודים מעטים א"כ אפי' כל אדם י"ל דין מפורסים כי מיתתו נודעת לכל. והכי קי"ל לקמן סי' תקמ"ז סעיף י'. וע"ז כ' כ"ה דבמדינת תוגרמה שיהודים רבים דרים בעיר א' חזר הדין לדין דש"ס וכמ"ש ר"י וכנ"ל. ואע"ג שנתפרסם כו' ר"ל שלא היה אדם מפורסם ואעפ"כ דרך מקרה נתפרסם מיתתו מ"מ לא מהני משום לא פלוג:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>(Seif katan 7) On Yom Tov, etc.</b> Where many Jews reside, etc. The Mishnah in Moed Katan 9b states, “and a coffin with the deceased in the courtyard.” Meaning, on Chol HaMoed one may make a coffin only in the courtyard where the deceased lies, but not in another courtyard, where people would not know that someone died and would suspect that it was being made for another purpose. For this reason the Tur writes in the name of Ri that work performed for a corpse on the second day of Yom Tov should be done discreetly, since the second day is certainly no more lenient than Chol HaMoed. The Yerushalmi, however, states that if a prominent person died, the coffin may be made even in another courtyard, because his death is publicized and people know that it is being made for him. Therefore, nowadays, when only a small number of Jews live in each city, every person has the status of a prominent person, since his death becomes known to all. This is how we rule below in siman 547, seif 10. Knesses HaGedolah therefore writes that in Turkey, where many Jews live in one city, the law reverts to that of the Gemara and to Ri's ruling. Even if the death happened to become publicized, this does not help if the deceased was not a prominent person, because the Sages did not distinguish.

<b>(ס"ק ח) כחול כו'</b> אבל כו' (ת"י ת"ד) והתוס' כתבו כו' לכאורה כוונתו דהתוס' חולקים ומקילים דאפי' י"ט שני של ימים ראשונים קיל מחה"מ אבל ז"א. והנה ס' ת"י ות"ד לא ראיתי ודברי התוס' הם פ"ק דמ"ק דף ח' ע"ב תנן אין חופרים כוכין וקברים בחה"מ ופירש"י לצורך מתים שימותו (ועיין מ"ש לקמן בס"ק י"א) וכתבו התוס' דהדין עמו דאלו לצורך מת שכבר מת אמרי' י"ט שני לגבי מת כחול שוינהו רבנן אפי' למיגז ליה אסא וגלימא כ"ש חה"מ. ואע"פ שי"ל דהא די"ט שני כחול היינו דוקא י"ט שני של עצרת או י"ט שני של פסח וסוכות י"ט שני מן ימים אחרונים דהיינו אחרון של פסח וסוכות דלא הוי אלא דרבנן ולכן הקילו בו. אבל חה"מ דהוי דאורייתא יש להחמיר. ז"א דהא אמר סתם י"ט שני כחול משמע כל י"ט שני אפי' מימים ראשונים של פסח וסוכות. ועוד דאפי' י"ט שני של ימים אחרונים ג"כ חמיר לענין מלאכה מחה"מ לכן פירש"י עיקר עכת"ד התוס' ולפ"ז מ"ש מ"א בשם ת"י ות"ד די"ט ב' של ימים הראשונים הוי. כחה"מ ע"כ ס"ל כס"ד דהתו'. דבי"ט שני מן ימים ראשונים אפי' כבר מת אין חופרים לו קבר. וס"ל דלא כפירש"י אלא כהראב"ד שהביא הרא"ש שם שחולק על רש"י וס"ל דאפי' בחה"מ אין חופרים קבר אפי' למי שכבר מת והתו' במסקנתם כ' דאין לחלק וגם בי"ט שני של ימים ראשונים כחול שוויוהו. ומותר לחפור קבר למי שמת כבר. לפי שהם הסכימו לפירש"י דהא דתנן אין חופרים כו' בחה"מ. ר"ל לצורך מתים שימותו וכנ"ל והכי קי"ל לקמן סי' תקע"ז:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>(Seif katan 8) Like a weekday, etc.</b> But, etc. The cited works are apparently Teshuvos Yeshanos and Terumas HaDeshen. Tosafos write, etc. At first glance, Magen Avraham appears to mean that Tosafos disagree and are lenient, holding that even the second day among the first festival days is more lenient than Chol HaMoed. This is incorrect. I have not seen the cited works, but Tosafos in Moed Katan 8b discuss the Mishnah, “One may not dig burial chambers and graves on Chol HaMoed.” Rashi explains that this means for those who will die later. Tosafos write that Rashi is correct, for regarding one who has already died we say that the Sages treated the second day of Yom Tov as a weekday for a corpse, even to cut myrtle and a shroud for him, and all the more so on Chol HaMoed. One might have argued that the second day is treated as a weekday only on Shavuos, or on the final festival days of Pesach and Sukkos, which are D'Rabbanan, whereas Chol HaMoed is according to the Torah and should be treated more stringently. Tosafos reject this because the Gemara states broadly that the second day is like a weekday, implying every second day, even among the first days of Pesach and Sukkos. Moreover, even the second day among the final festival days is more stringent regarding melachah than Chol HaMoed. Therefore Rashi's interpretation is primary. Accordingly, the authorities cited by Magen Avraham, who equate the second day among the first festival days with Chol HaMoed, must accept Tosafos' initial possibility: on such a second day, even when the person has already died, one may not dig his grave. They disagree with Rashi and follow Raavad, cited by the Rosh, who maintains that even on Chol HaMoed one may not dig a grave for one who has already died. Tosafos conclude that no distinction is made, and that even the second day among the first festival days was treated as a weekday, so one may dig a grave for one who has already died. They therefore agree with Rashi that the Mishnah's prohibition concerns those who will die later. This is how we rule below in siman 547.

<b>(Seif katan 11) A rope, etc.</b> Although Rashi appears to imply, etc., nevertheless it seems to me that this is a scribal error, etc. The Rosh writes in the first chapter of Moed Katan 8a, on the Mishnah that burial chambers and graves may not be dug on Chol HaMoed, that Rashi explains this as referring to graves for people who have not yet died, to be used after the festival, and it is forbidden because of excessive exertion. The general rule that even on the second day of Yom Tov Jews may attend to a corpse applies only to moving it or another matter involving no significant exertion, such as washing it or cutting its hair, but not digging. Rashi's words are difficult: he initially explained that the graves are for people who will die later, so why cite the law that Jews may attend to a corpse on the second day of Yom Tov, which concerns burial that same day? Moreover, his statement “but not digging” implies that one may not dig a grave on the second day, which is incorrect, for the second day was treated as a weekday. The Rosh explains that Rashi was troubled by the following comparison. If a person dies late on the second day of Yom Tov and there is insufficient time to bury him that day, Jews may nevertheless attend to him that day for burial after Yom Tov. Why, then, should one not similarly dig graves on Chol HaMoed for burial after the festival? Rashi answers that only moving and acts without significant exertion were permitted in such a case, but not digging, since there is no time to bury him that day. Similarly, one may not cut his shroud, and the same applies on Chol HaMoed. We learn from this that if one dies on the second day and they do not intend to bury him that day, no prohibited melachah may be performed for him that day, apparently not even through a non-Jew. Terumas HaDeshen, siman 82, infers from the Rosh's mention only of moving that even carrying into a karmelis is forbidden. He then distinguishes between an actual melachah, namely carrying, and cutting hair, which he does not consider a melachah. Maharshal disagrees, maintaining that cutting hair is more of a melachah than carrying into a karmelis. Rather, the distinction is between laborious and non-laborious acts. A burdensome melachah is forbidden when burial will not take place that day, whereas cutting hair, although a Torah melachah, involves little exertion and is permitted. Certainly carrying into a karmelis would also be permitted according to the Rosh. Maharshal then writes that the text is nevertheless erroneous and disagrees with the Rosh. He argues that Rashi's question cannot have been based on the rule that the second day is like a weekday even when burial that day is impossible, because perhaps Jews may not attend to the corpse at all in such a case. Indeed, Rashi's own conclusion, allowing even the cutting of a shroud and myrtle, plainly refers only to a case in which they intend and have time to bury him that day. The opening statement that the second day was treated as a weekday must likewise refer only to burial that day. Therefore the text must be corrected. The Mishnah's opening may refer either to graves for those who will die later, or to graves for those who have already died. Rashi's question applies only to the second interpretation. According to the first interpretation, which is the accepted halachah, there is no question. Consequently, neither interpretation proves that any act, whether cutting hair or carrying, is permitted when burial is not intended that day. Regarding moving, see siman 311, where he discusses this at length.

The Tur in siman 278 rules like Rabbi Shimon, that one is exempt for a melachah not needed for its own purpose. They therefore questioned Magen Avraham's conclusion that Rambam discusses one who needs the hair, meaning that Rambam rules like Rabbi Yehudah, who holds one liable for a melachah not needed for its own purpose, as Magen Avraham writes at the end of siman 334. In my humble opinion, this is not his meaning. If so, why did he mention a melachah not needed for its own purpose only regarding carrying and not regarding cutting hair? Moreover, he concludes that carrying still bears the name of a melachah, whereas here there is no melachah at all. He would also have had to say that Terumas HaDeshen disagrees with Rivash, cited by Magen Avraham in siman 303, seif katan 22, who holds that one is liable for cutting hair even when he does not need the hair, because that was how the melachah was performed in the Mishkan. Rather, Magen Avraham means that cutting hair from a corpse is not a melachah at all, even according to Rabbi Yehudah, who holds one liable for a melachah not needed for its own purpose. His reason is as he writes later: one who circumcises an eight-month infant on Shabbos has done nothing, etc. Although cutting skin or flesh from a living person incurs liability for detaching, as Taz writes at the end of siman 336, nevertheless an eight-month infant is considered dead, and cutting his flesh is like cutting ordinary flesh and involves no prohibition. What difference is there between circumcising and cutting the skin, or cutting hair? It follows that there is no prohibition of shearing or detaching from a corpse. The Yerushalmi and Rambam, who hold one liable even for cutting from a corpse because of shearing, refer to one who needs the hair, for then he assigns significance to the act as a melachah. A similar principle is cited by Magen Avraham in siman 318, seif katan 36, in the name of Maggid Mishneh: when one does not intend the act, it is inappropriate to call it an inevitable consequence and impose liability merely because, when intentional, it constitutes a melachah. When unintentional, there is no melachah at all, since he does not seek to fashion a utensil. This resembles cutting a twig, where one is exempt unless he cuts it to use as a toothpick. Whenever the liability is because of fashioning a utensil, one who does not fashion it is exempt, even according to Rabbi Yehudah, who generally holds one liable for an unintended act. See there.

<b>(Seif katan 12) It is permitted, etc.</b> As in the law of chalitzah. Yevamos 106b, and so we rule in Even HaEzer 169:50: if a yevamah falls before a yavam who is unsuitable for her and he refuses to perform chalitzah unless she gives him two hundred zuz, he may be deceived by promising payment after the chalitzah, and afterward she need not pay him anything. The same is done here, and it is permitted for the sake of a mitzvah. Beit Yosef explains there that Rashi gives the reason that Shabbos wages are forbidden because they resemble commerce. Commerce itself is prohibited only D'Rabbanan, so Shabbos wages resemble a decree upon a decree. The prohibition is therefore only an added stringency, and was permitted for a mitzvah. Taz, however, writes below in siman 585 that those who permit payment for a mitzvah must reconcile the opposing view with the baraisa stating that one who hires a worker to guard the red heifer or grain for the Omer may not pay him for Shabbos, although this is for a mitzvah. He therefore concludes that even according to the permissive opinion, it is allowed only for a mitzvah that is an obligation of that day, such as shofar blowing on Rosh Hashanah. The same must apply here: the deceased lies before him and must be buried that day so as not to violate the prohibition against leaving a corpse overnight, and it is therefore a mitzvah that is the obligation of the day.
