<b>א) [סעיף א'] אין נשיאות כפים בפחות מיו"ד וכו'  </b>ואם התחילו בעשרה ויצאו מקצתן גומרין כמ"ש לעיל בש"ע סי' נ"ה סעי' ב' והוא מהירושלמי דמגילה פ' הקורא והביאוהו הרי"ף והרא"ש שס בפ' הקורא והרמב"ם פ"ח מה"ת כמ"ש בב"י לעיל סי' נ"ה ומ"ש בשכנה"ג בזה הסי' או' ג' והביאו העו"ת או' א' לתמוה על הפו' שלא ביארו זה הוא תימה שהרי כתבוהו הרי"ף והרא"ש והרמב"ם וב"י לעיל בסי' נ"ה וביארוהו ואי משום שלא נזכר בהדיא בש"ע הרי זה נלמד ממילא וכבר מבואר בב"י ולמה צריך לפרט בש"ע דבר הפשוט שנלמד ממילא. ואח"כ ראיתי בא"ר או' א' שגם הוא הקשה על דברי השכנה"ג הנז' יעו"ש, ומיהו יש לישב דבריו דר"ל על האחרונים שאחרי מרן ז"ל שלא בארוהו בדין נ"כ ודו"ק. והא דאמרינן דאם יצאו מקצתן גומרין הוא שנשתיירו רובן כמ"ש בש"ע שם, וכ"כ הפר"ח בזה הסי' או' ב' ר"ז או' א' ועיין בדברינו לשם או' ל"ה:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>1) [Se'if 1] The Priestly Blessing is not recited with fewer than ten, etc.</b> If they began with ten and some left, they complete it, as stated above in Shulchan Aruch, siman 55, se'if 2. This comes from the Yerushalmi, Megillah, chapter HaKorei, and is cited by Rif and Rosh there and by Rambam, chapter 8 of Hilchos Tefillah, as Beis Yosef writes above in siman 55. Knesses HaGedolah, in this siman, os 3, cited by Olas Tamid, os 1, wonders why the poskim did not explain this. That objection is surprising, for Rif, Rosh, Rambam, and Beis Yosef wrote and explained it above in siman 55. If his objection is that Shulchan Aruch does not state it explicitly, it follows automatically and is already explained in Beis Yosef; why must Shulchan Aruch spell out an obvious consequence? I later saw that Eliyah Rabbah, os 1, also objects to Knesses HaGedolah; see there. His words may nevertheless be reconciled by explaining that he refers to the later authorities after Maran, who did not explain the rule in the laws of the Priestly Blessing. When we say that if some left they complete it, this applies only if a majority remains, as Shulchan Aruch states there. So write Peri Chadash here, os 2, and Shulchan Aruch HaRav, os 1. See our words there, os 35.

<b>ב) שם. אין נשיאות כפים בפחות מיו"ד וכו'  </b>עיין בשכנה"ג או' א' שנסתפק אם נשיאות כפים צ"ל דוקא במקום דאיכא ס"ת וכ"כ מהרי"ע בסוף ס' בית יהודה דף ק"ט ע"ד במנהגי ארגי"ל סי' ד' אבל הא"ר או' ב' כתב נראה פשוט דאף שמתפללים בבית שאין בה ס"ת נושאים כפיהם וכתב דכן נוטה דעת העו"ת יעו"ש. וכ"כ הפר"ח או' יו"ד וכתב דהכי נהוג, והביאו י"א בהגה"ט. וכ"כ הרב משאת משה ח"א חא"ח סי' ג' דבכל מקום שעבר נוהגין לברך ג"כ כל בעשרה דמכנפי באיזה בית דאקראי ואפי' בספינה דרך הילוכה יעו"ש, והביאו י"א במ"ב בהגה"ט או' א' ש"ץ דקמ"ב ע"ב, ברכ"י או' ח' זכ"ל או' ן' בי"מ או' א' וכן הסכים הנו"ש או' א' יעוש"ב. וכ"כ ח"א כלל ל"ב או' ט"ו פתה"ד או' ה' כף החיים סי' ט"ו או' ע"ב בן א"ח פ' תצוה או' י"ג וכן הוא לפי דברי האר"י ז"ל בשער הכוו' דרוש ה' דחזרת העמידה שנתן טעם בסוד לנ"כ שבתוך חזרת העמידה וכתב שיש איזה בחי' מכוונת תפלה שנעשית ע"י נ"כ כיעו"ש משמע שאין הדבר תלוי באתרא דאיכא ס"ת אלא כל היכא דאיכא עשרה ואומרים חזרה צ"ל נ"כ כדי לתקן הבחי' ההיא ולא תהיה התפלה חסירה וכן ראוי לעשות ואין לשנות:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>2) There: The Priestly Blessing is not recited with fewer than ten, etc.</b> Knesses HaGedolah, os 1, questions whether the Priestly Blessing must be recited specifically where a Torah scroll is present. Mahari Ayash writes similarly at the end of Beis Yehudah, page 109d, in the customs of Algiers, siman 4. Eliyah Rabbah, os 2, however, writes that it is obvious that even those praying in a house without a Torah scroll recite the Priestly Blessing, and says Olas Tamid inclines to this view. Peri Chadash, os 10, likewise writes that this is the custom; Yad Aharon cites him in Hagahos Beis Yosef. Responsa Masas Moshe, part 1, Orach Chaim, siman 3, rules that wherever the blessing is ordinarily recited, any ten who assemble in a temporary house recite it, even aboard a moving ship. Yad Aharon cites him in Mahadura Basra, Hagahos Beis Yosef, os 1, as do Shaliach Tzibbur, page 142b; Birkei Yosef, os 8; Zechor LeAvraham, os 50; Beis Menuchah, os 1; and Nehar Shalom, os 1, who agrees. So rule Chayei Adam 32:15; Pesach HaDevir, os 5; Kaf HaChaim, siman 15, os 72; and Ben Ish Chai, Tetzaveh, os 13. This also follows from Arizal in Shaar HaKavanos, discourse 5 on the repetition of the Amidah. He gives a mystical reason for the Priestly Blessing within the repetition and writes that it accomplishes a particular aspect of the prayer. The law therefore does not depend on the presence of a Torah scroll. Wherever ten are present and the repetition is recited, the Priestly Blessing must be recited to complete that aspect, so the prayer will not be deficient. This is the proper practice and should not be changed.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>4) There: With fewer than ten, etc.</b> If ten are present in the synagogue, but instead of a separate repetition the congregation and prayer leader recite the Amidah together aloud, and the kohanim finish their Amidah before the congregation reaches Retzeh, they may ascend the platform and recite the Priestly Blessing. Even if the prayer leader calls them, this is not an interruption because it is needed for the prayer. To avoid any concern, another person who has completed his prayer should call them before Retzeh. Knesses HaGedolah, Hagahos HaTur, os 2, writes that witnesses reported this as the custom in the western lands; Shaliach Tzibbur, page 142d, cites him. See the beginning of Maaseh Rokeach, page 1b, in the novellae to Rambam composed by his son Rabbi Avraham. It appears there that when the congregation prays aloud together with the prayer leader, the kohanim ascend even if they did not finish before Retzeh. Beis Menuchah, os 8, cites this. However, in light of what we wrote above in siman 124, os 2, in Arizal's name; that the repetition is obligatory and possesses greater stature than the silent Amidah; one should not rely on this to cancel the repetition except when unavoidable, such as before departing on a journey or when insufficient time remains to recite it.

<b>6) There, gloss: A non-kohen may not recite the Priestly Blessing, etc.</b> Regarding one who comes today and says, 'I am a kohen,' Maran writes at the beginning of Even HaEzer, siman 3, that he is not believed and cannot establish his priestly status through his own word. He is not called first to the Torah and may not recite the Priestly Blessing. Rama there, however, cites Ramach that he is believed for being called first and for the Priestly Blessing today, when we have no Torah-level terumah and need not fear that this status will lead people to give him terumah. Rama says this is the present custom wherever terumah is not practiced. Chelkas Mechokek, os 2, writes that Rama did not accept every aspect of Ramach's view. Where even rabbinic terumah is practiced today, such as Eretz Yisrael under Yoreh Deah 331:2, one must fear that being called first will lead to his receiving rabbinic terumah. Where terumah is not practiced at all, he may be called because no harm can result, as Ran and Maggid Mishneh explain the custom. Beis Shmuel, os 2, objects that Ran and Maggid Mishneh mention only being called to the Torah, which entails no prohibition, not reciting the Priestly Blessing. A non-kohen who blesses appears to violate a Torah prohibition, for the verse says, 'So shall you bless'; you, and not non-kohanim. He answers that we do not suspect a person of committing a prohibition from which he gains nothing. We therefore fear only that he may eat terumah, not that he will recite the Priestly Blessing or be called to the Torah. In Eretz Yisrael, where terumah is practiced under Yoreh Deah 331, he is not believed even today. He concludes that lineage requires two valid witnesses according to all views, while rabbinic terumah requires one witness and cannot be established by the person's own word. Why did the authorities not also fear that people might give him the money of pidyon haben, from which he benefits? A father does not give that money to just any kohen; he first determines that the recipient has established lineage and is learned, so the mitzvah is properly fulfilled. Terumah today, by contrast, is burned and may be given even to an unlearned kohen, as stated there in se'if 19; hence the concern. See our Chukei Chaim, discourse 1 on pidyon haben. Bayis Chadash nevertheless writes that he is believed today even in Eretz Yisrael; Baer Heitev there, se'if katan 1, cites him. Maharit, part 1, siman 149, writes that perhaps one who comes from a known place and says he is a kohen may be called first and recite the Priestly Blessing, because where travelers are common a person would not lie about something that could later bring him disgrace. But one who comes from a distant place and is unknown; such as forced converts arriving from a distant land where they did not observe Jewish law; is certainly not believed on his own, only through others. See Emes LeYaakov, laws of being called to the Torah, os 12, and Ledavid Emes, siman 5, os 12. It appears that present practice is as follows: If we see that he is learned, God-fearing, and says he is a kohen from a specified place, he is believed on his own to be called first and recite the Priestly Blessing, even in Eretz Yisrael. If he comes from a distant land with which travel is uncommon and is also unlearned, he is not believed even outside Eretz Yisrael without testimony from others. See further below, siman 135, os 17.

<b>7) There: A non-kohen may not recite the Priestly Blessing, etc.</b> A child was taken captive among non-Jews and redeemed, and his parentage is unknown. One witness says with certainty that he is a kohen, while another says with certainty that he is an ordinary Israelite. Pesach HaDevir, os 4, rules that he may not recite the Priestly Blessing when he matures; see his full discussion. See also there where he rejects Responsa Shevus Yaakov, part 1, siman 93, which appears to permit a doubtful kohen to recite it. Mishbetzos Zahav, os 27, likewise rules that one whose paternity is uncertain; whether he is the nine-month child of the first husband or the seven-month child of the latter; may not recite the Priestly Blessing. Pesach HaDevir cites and agrees with him.

<b>8) There: A non-kohen may not recite the Priestly Blessing, etc.</b> Kesubos 24b states that a non-kohen who recites it violates a positive commandment. Rashi explains that the verse says, 'So shall you bless'; you, and not non-kohanim; a prohibition inferred from a positive command is treated as a positive command. Rama specifies 'even together with other kohanim' because Shabbos 118b records Rabbi Yose saying, 'I never disobeyed my colleagues. I know that I am not a kohen, but if they told me to ascend the platform I would ascend.' Tosafos writes that Ri did not know what prohibition a non-kohen violates by ascending, other than a blessing in vain, since the Torah commanded the kohanim, not Israelites, to bless. Darkei Moshe, os 1, asks from Kesubos and suggests that Ri discussed only one who ascends with other kohanim, whereas one who ascends alone violates the positive command. Rama therefore rules stringently that even together with other kohanim it is forbidden. Bach answers that the positive prohibition applies only when he recites the blessing in the prescribed manner of the Priestly Blessing; merely ascending the platform without doing so does not violate it. In my humble opinion, the answer is that a non-kohen violates the command only when he intends to fulfill the mitzvah of blessing Israel as kohanim were commanded. If he merely intends an ordinary blessing such as one person gives another, he does not violate it. This is what Tosafos means by saying Ri did not know the prohibition. Haflaah on Kesubos cites Sefer Chareidim that an Israelite has a positive mitzvah to receive the blessing. A non-kohen who ascends the platform does not receive it, for se'if 24 below says those behind the kohanim are not included in the blessing. Shaarei Teshuvah, os 2, cites this.

<b>12) There: Among the matters that prevent, etc.</b> Even when a condition prevents him only rabbinically, he does not violate a positive command by remaining below. The person calling 'kohanim' intends only qualified kohanim, not those disqualified, even if only rabbinically. He therefore need not leave before the call. Bach rules accordingly. Magen Avraham, se'if katan 4, comments that this implies that if he is personally told to ascend, he must do so, because otherwise he violates the positive command. Yet Beis Yosef's wording in se'if 2 implies that one possessing any disqualifying condition does not violate the command even if told to ascend. This appears correct: He does not refrain on his own; the Sages barred him, and they have authority to do so, as stated in siman 191. Peri Chadash, os 4, likewise rules that a kohen with a rabbinic disqualification may not ascend even when told, because a rabbinic court may suspend a Torah law through passive nonperformance, as stated in Yevamos, chapter HaIshah Rabbah. So too Olas Tamid, os 6. If only disqualified kohanim are present, Eliyah Rabbah, os 4, says they should leave before Retzeh. Olas Tamid, however, says that even when only disqualified kohanim are present and the prayer leader will certainly intend his call for them, they need not leave because the Sages disqualified them. Nevertheless, the person calling 'kohanim' should preferably intend to call only those fit for the blessing and free of even a rabbinic disqualification.

<b>13) There: Although he has neglected one positive mitzvah, etc.</b> This is Rambam's language at the end of Hilchos Tefillah. <b>16) There: Or if they told him to ascend, etc.</b> Even before the prayer leader calls, if people tell the kohanim to ascend or wash their hands, they must ascend. <b>20) [Se'if 3] If he ascended once that day, etc.</b> If he ascends again, such as during Mussaf or Neilah, even before the same congregation, that same kohen recites 'Who sanctified us, etc.' Maharam Mintz, siman 12, says this is the custom; Magen Avraham, se'if katan 3, and Maamar Mordechai, os 6, cite him. Levush compares the mitzvah to lulav, which is not taken with another blessing after one has fulfilled it once, rather than tefillin, whose blessing is recited each time. Maamar Mordechai says the opposite is more logical: It resembles tzitzis and tefillin, and he blesses every time he ascends, even in the same synagogue, contrary to Levush's concluding doubt. Eliyah Rabbah, os 5, says Levush overlooked Maharam Mintz's explicit ruling and testimony to the custom. Birkei Yosef, Shiyurei Berachah, os 3, agrees and says this is the custom; so too Zechor LeAvraham, os 50, and Shulchan Aruch HaRav, os 4. Universal practice is for kohanim to bless again that day, whether before the same congregation during Mussaf, a fast-day Minchah, or Neilah, or before another congregation; for example, after blessing here he enters another synagogue that has not yet reached the Priestly Blessing. Se'if 28 below says he ascends and recites 'Who sanctified us, etc.,' and no one objects. See below, os 163. See also our words above, siman 17, os 2, that the rule of doubt concerning blessings does not override an established custom.

<b>21)</b> Zera Avraham, Orach Chaim, siman 12, writes that a Jerusalem kohen may recite the Priestly Blessing during Mussaf on the diaspora's second festival day for visitors from abroad who have no kohen. Although for him it is not a time of the Temple service, and their service is nothing to him because the day is entirely weekday for him, the law permits him to bless Israel whenever he wishes; he may therefore bless them just as one may bless several congregations. Rabbi Moshe ben Chaviv, cited at length in Ginas Veradim 1:13, agrees. He concludes that if they have a kohen from abroad, Jerusalem kohanim should not ascend with him, but if they have none, a Jerusalem kohen should ascend. Rabbi Aharon HaLevi, however, writes at length in siman 14 that no decisive proof resolves the doubt, so one should not risk a doubtful blessing. To accommodate the leading sages of Eretz Yisrael who permit it, they should recite the Priestly Blessing but omit 'Who sanctified us.' Shaliach Tzibbur, page 146b-c, and Ikarei HaDat, siman 5, os 7, cite this. See also the glossator in Shaliach Tzibbur, pages 235d-236a: If he can avoid the situation, that is best. Otherwise, if other kohanim are present he ascends with them and says 'Who sanctified us' without the Divine Name or Kingship; if he is alone, he ascends and blesses normally, following Rabbi Moshe ben Chaviv and Mahari. In my view, a Jerusalem kohen who travels abroad intending to return and joins Mussaf on the second festival day, or prays in Jerusalem with diaspora visitors, may ascend with diaspora kohanim and recite 'Who sanctified us' without the Divine Name or Kingship. With others present, his blessing is inconspicuous. If no other kohen is present, he should specifically avoid it and leave before Retzeh to escape the doubt.

<b>30) There: Wearing shoes.</b> Tur's words prove that shoes should be removed before washing the hands. Accordingly, they must be removed outside the synagogue. So writes Mateh Moshe, siman 178. Our present custom, however, is to leave the synagogue to wash and remove the shoes only upon ascending. The differing customs apparently reflect differing footwear. In the Ottoman lands, shoes are not tied and can be slipped off without touching them, so the kohanim wash first and remove them while ascending. In European lands, shoes must be tied and cannot be removed without touching them, so they must be removed outside before washing; otherwise the kohanim would have to wash again after removal. Knesses HaGedolah, Hagahos HaTur, os 4. He adds that our custom of removing shoes upon ascending is nevertheless improper, because the shoes remain visibly below the platform before the entire congregation throughout the blessing. Modest people hide them beneath the benches before ascending. I saw Rabbi David HaKohen leave his shoes outside the synagogue, and believe I likewise saw Rabbi Yosef HaKohen do so. Olas Tamid, os 9; Eliyah Rabbah, os 6; Peri Chadash, os 5; Shaliach Tzibbur there, page 141a; and Beis Menuchah, os 16-17, cite this. The rule depends on local circumstances. Where shoes left outside are not likely to be stolen, leave them there; where theft is a concern, conceal them under the synagogue benches. If tied shoes must be touched during removal, remove them before washing to avoid another washing afterward. Untied shoes that can be slipped off without contact may be removed after washing. See our words above, siman 4, os 69, and below, se'if 17, in the gloss.

<b>לה) [סעיף ו'] אעפ"י שנטלו הכהנים וכו'  </b>דהכי איתא במס' סוטה ט"ל ע"א א"ר יהושע ב"ל כל כהן שלא נטל ידיו לא ישא את כפיו שנאמר שאו ידיכם קודש וברכו את ה' ע"כ וכתב ב"י שמדברי הרמב"ם משמע דוקא דכשידיו טמאות כגון שלא נטל שחרית קאמר דצריך ליטול הא אם נטל ידיו שחרית ואינו יודע להם שוה טמאה א"צ לחזור ולנטלם וכתב שכן נוהגים בארץ מצרים שלא ליטול הכהן ידיו בשעת נ"כ אלא סומך על הנטילה שנטל ידיו שחרית יעו"ש. וכ"כ בא"ח בשם הר' אברהם בן הרמב"ם ז"ל ז"ל שהלכה למעשה לפני אביו הרמב"ם ז"ל וזולתו ממורי התורה שכל כהן העומד בתפלה יגש לב"כ וסומך על נטילת ידיו לק"ש ולתפלה עכ"ד והביאו ב"י וכ"כ התו' שם בסוטה ע"ש רש"י ז"ל שאמר לו רבו אלא שכתבו שזהו הועתק מהגהת תלמיד אבל מלשון רש"י ז"ל לא משמע הכי דפי' שלא נטל ידיו לפני עלותו לדוכן משמע ממש סמוך. וכתבו עוד שכן משמע מתלמודא דידן ומהירושלמי דאפי' נטל ידיו שחרית צריך לחזור וליטול סמוך לב"כ ולא ישהה יותר משיעור מהלך כ"ב אמה יעו"ש. והב"ד ב"י וכתב וכ"ה דעת הטור כדעת התו' יעו"ש. וכן הוא דעת מרן ז"ל כאן בש"ע. וכ"כ הלבוש, ב"ח, פר"ח אות ו', ר"ז אות ח' חס"ל אות ג' בן א"ח פ' תצוה אות ז'. וכ"מ מהזוה"ק לקמן אות מ'. וכן המנהג פה עיה"ק ירושת"ו כדעת מרן ז"ל ודעמיה, רק היכא דלא אפשר כגון ש"ץ העומד בתפלה דאיכא הפסק אם יטול ידיו וכדומה יש לסמוך על הרמב"ם. ועיין באות שאח"ז:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>35) [Se'if 6] Although the kohanim washed, etc.</b> Sotah 39a states in Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi's name: A kohen who has not washed his hands may not recite the Priestly Blessing, for it says, 'Raise your hands in holiness and bless Hashem.' Beis Yosef understands Rambam to require washing only when the hands are impure, such as when one did not wash in the morning. If he washed then and knows of no later impurity, he need not wash again. This was the Egyptian custom. Orchos Chaim likewise cites Rabbi Avraham ben Rambam that in practice before his father Rambam and other Torah teachers, every kohen standing in prayer proceeded to the blessing relying on his washing for Shema and the Amidah. Tosafos in Sotah cites a similar view in Rashi's name but says it was copied from a student's gloss; Rashi's own words, 'who did not wash before ascending,' imply washing immediately beforehand. Tosafos further proves from the Bavli and Yerushalmi that even one who washed in the morning must wash again near the blessing, with no delay exceeding the time to walk twenty-two amos. Beis Yosef cites this and says Tur agrees with Tosafos. This is Maran's ruling here, and so rule Levush; Bach; Peri Chadash, os 6; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, os 8; Chesed LaAlafim, os 3; and Ben Ish Chai, Tetzaveh, os 7. It also follows from the Zohar cited below, os 40, and is the custom here in holy Jerusalem. Only when impossible; such as a prayer leader for whom washing would interrupt the Amidah; may one rely on Rambam. See the next os.

<b>לו) שם אעפ"י שנטלו הכהנים ידיהם שחרית וכו'  </b>ומיהו אם נטל ידיו בשחרית אפי' רק עד סוף קשרי אצבעותיו ואין לו מים ליטול לדוכן כדאי הוא הרמב"ם וסיעתו לסמוך עליו ומותר לישא את כפיו והוא כשלא הסיח דעתו מהם. עו"ת אות י"ב, א"ר אות ח' ח"א כלל ל"ב אות ח' מ"ב אות ך' אבל אם לא נטל גם בשחרית אסור לישא את כפיו דלא מהני מידי דמנקי אלא לתפלה אבל לדוכן מים בעינן עו"ת שם, א"ר שם וכ"נ דעת הפר"ח שם ועיין לקמן אות ט"ל:

<b>39) There: Up to the joint connecting the hand and arm.</b> Rambam, Hilchos Tefillah 15:5, explains that this parallels sanctification for the Temple service; Tur and Beis Yosef cite him. If only enough water is available to reach the ends of the finger joints, Olas Tamid, os 12, permits one who did not wash in the morning but prayed after cleaning his hands to ascend after this limited washing, and says Maran may agree. It is a proper custom, since passive restraint is preferable in a dispute, especially when Maran and Rama agree and this is common practice. See above, os 36.

<b>40) There: The Levi pours water, etc.</b> Zohar, Naso 146a, teaches: A kohen who spreads his hands must add holiness to his holiness by sanctifying his hands through a holy hand. That holy hand is the Levi's, from whom the kohen receives the sanctifying water, for it says, 'You shall sanctify the Levites'; they are holy. It also says, 'And also your brethren, the tribe of Levi.' Thus every kohen who spreads his hands must be sanctified by a holy person, adding holiness to his holiness, and should not receive the sanctifying water from an unholy person. Tzinius DiSifra further teaches that the Levi through whom the kohen is sanctified must first sanctify himself. Why specifically a Levi rather than another kohen? Another kohen might be deficient, and a complete kohen should not be impaired through one who is incomplete. A complete Levi, however, was fit to ascend the platform and serve in the Tabernacle, and is called holy, as it says, 'You shall sanctify the Levites.' Beis Yosef cites this, and it is the basis of Maran's words here. The Zohar implies that when no Levi is present, neither an Israelite nor another kohen should wash his hands; he should wash them himself. Bach, however, cites Maharil that in the absence of Levites, a firstborn who opened his mother's womb should pour for the kohanim. Knesses HaGedolah, Hagahos Beis Yosef, os 3; Olas Tamid, os 13; Taz, se'if katan 3; Ateres Zekeinim, os 1; Magen Avraham, se'if katan 7; and Eliyah Rabbah, os 10, cite this. Magen Avraham notes that this means specifically a maternal firstborn; a paternal firstborn alone has no recognized sanctity today. Shaliach Tzibbur, page 143a; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, os 10; Chayei Adam 32:8; Chesed LaAlafim, os 3; Beis Menuchah, os 11; and Ben Ish Chai, Tetzaveh, os 7, cite it. I have not seen anyone follow this. They apparently rely on the Zohar's plain implication that only a Levi pours; without one, each kohen washes his own hands.

<b>41) There: The Levi pours water, etc.</b> If the kohanim are unlearned and the Levi is a Torah scholar who refuses to pour for them, he bears no guilt, even if he is the only Levi. This remains so even if he previously followed the practice and then changed his mind. Rabbi Yaakov HaLevi, siman 39; Rabbi Yitzchak HaLevi, his son Rabbi Shmuel HaLevi, and Rabbi Chaim Shabsai, simanim 40-41. Rabbi Leib Sarval, however, writes in siman 40 that a Levi may not exempt himself from pouring unless the kohanim are unfit for the blessing, such as people of Beis She'an and Beis Haifa or minors. Knesses HaGedolah, Hagahos HaTur; Olas Tamid, os 14; Magen Avraham, se'if katan 7; and Eliyah Rabbah, os 10, cite the dispute, and Eliyah Rabbah says one should follow the latter stringent view. Peri Chadash, os 8, disagrees. Since a Levi's pouring is not indispensable; a city consisting entirely of kohanim has everyone ascend though no Levi is present; a learned Levi who declines to pour for unlearned kohanim acts correctly even when he is the only Levi. On the contrary, he incurs death at Heaven's hands if he serves an unlearned kohen. Knesses HaGedolah further cites Rabbi Shmuel HaLevi, siman 41, that if learned and unlearned kohanim are both present the Levi must pour for all. Olas Tamid, Magen Avraham, and Eliyah Rabbah cite this, but Peri Chadash rejects it because of the liability just mentioned. Yad Aharon, Hagahos Beis Yosef; Shaliach Tzibbur, page 143b; Eshel Avraham, os 7; Chesed LaAlafim there; and Kaf HaChaim, siman 15, os 71, cite the discussion. Chesed LaAlafim says that although some permit it, caution is required and passive restraint is preferable, even when he is the only Levi. Ben Ish Chai rules likewise.

<b>44)</b> Taz 511:8 writes: I saw communities pour scented oil into water on Yom Tov for washing the kohanim's hands before the platform. They act improperly, because this creates fragrance in the water. Even if poured before Yom Tov, the kohanim create fragrance on Yom Tov when it transfers to them; they desire and intend this, so the practice must be stopped. Magen Avraham there, se'if katan 11, and here, se'if katan 8, agrees. Leket HaKemach, part 2, siman 20, likewise forbids washing face and beard with rosewater on Shabbos because it creates fragrance in the beard hair, and hair resembles clothing. Eliyah Rabbah here, os 8, says that because the oil itself is mixed with the water, this is no more creating fragrance than smelling the oil; it may be poured either into the water or onto the hands. Chacham Tzvi, siman 92, likewise disputes Taz and Magen Avraham and holds that creating fragrance does not apply to human flesh, foods, or drinks. Ginas Veradim 3:16 permits applying it to hands, face, and beard, but agrees with Taz and Magen Avraham that rosewater may not be added to water. Nechpah BaKesef, siman 4, defends the custom of adding rosewater to wine on Shabbos and Yom Tov and permits it plainly. He is stringent only about adding fragrance to water intended for washing; if it was added before Shabbos, washing is permitted. Mahari Ayash agrees at the end of Shevet Yehudah. Chida cites them in Birkei Yosef 511:5 and Machazik Berachah there, os 5. Likkutei Peri Chadash 511 says any intent to create fragrance is forbidden. Batei Kehunah, part 1, siman 293, likewise appears to forbid intentional creation of fragrance on a person or object, permitting it when unintended. Responsa Shoel UMeishiv, second edition, part 2, siman 7, discusses the dispute and rules like Taz. Shelah's summary of Yom Tov laws and Shulchan Aruch HaRav at the end of siman 511 rule likewise. Therefore, practically, anything intended to create fragrance is forbidden; for example, putting rosewater into washing water, or washing one's face, hands, and certainly hair with rosewater itself; because the prohibiting authorities are the majority. It is permitted to add it to foods and drinks to sweeten them, or to pour it into one's hand in order to smell the rosewater itself, as some communities do at celebrations so each person can recite the fragrance blessing, since he does not intend to perfume his hand. Rav Pe'alim, part 2, siman 51, rules accordingly.

<b>45) [Se'if 7] If the kohen washed his hands, etc., he should not recite another blessing, etc.</b> Maran rules this in Bedek HaBayis because doubtful blessings are omitted, rejecting Tur's view that one recites the hand-washing blessing for the Priestly Blessing. Rama likewise writes in Darkei Moshe, os 4, that it is best not to bless, since Rambam holds no washing is needed at all after morning washing; he should therefore wash without a blessing. So rule Bach; Knesses HaGedolah at the beginning of Hagahos HaTur and in Hagahos Beis Yosef; and Eliyah Rabbah, os 13. Eliyah Rabbah adds that perhaps the hand-washing blessing was instituted only for eating or morning washing, as Responsa Rashba, simanim 190-191, implies. The later authorities follow Maran; independently, the settled rule is that a doubtful blessing is omitted.
