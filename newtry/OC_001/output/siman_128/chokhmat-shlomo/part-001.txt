<b>Se'if 1, gloss: A non-kohen may not raise his hands, even together with other kohanim.</b> See Magen Avraham's resolution of Rema's question: Rabbi Yose follows his own view that women may optionally perform semichah. Atzei Arazim 3 objects: Tosafos suggests concern for a blessing in vain, yet Tosafos in Rosh Hashanah permits women to bless over time-bound positive mitzvos. Magen Avraham is correct. With an active mitzvah such as shofar, tefillin, or lulav, although a woman is not commanded she still performs a mitzvah-act and may bless. Birkas Kohanim, however, is only speech whose purpose is to bless Israel. If the blessing takes effect it is a mitzvah; if a non-kohen's blessing does not take effect, he has done nothing. Since non-kohanim are not commanded and their blessing may not take effect, how can a blessing be recited over a null act? Alternatively, Rashi, Berachos 20a, explains that a kohen or nazir becoming impure for a meis mitzvah is not an active override because their prohibition was never stated regarding a meis mitzvah, just as it was not stated regarding relatives. I explained in youth (and later found in Pnei Yehoshua) that when the exclusion is written beside the prohibition it is complete permission; when human dignity overrides an independently stated command, it is only a rejection and cannot override through action. The author's son notes that this accords with Ramban in Shaar HaGemul and Responsa Atzei Chaim. Similarly, ordinary positive commands are phrased generally for males and females; women's exemption from time-bound commands is learned elsewhere, so they can still say 'Who commanded us.' Birkas Kohanim itself says 'you' (kohanim and not non-kohanim) so one excluded there cannot say 'Who commanded us.' Magen Avraham aptly compares semichah, whose own verse says 'the sons of Israel,' not daughters; Rabbi Yose nevertheless permits women to perform it voluntarily, though without a blessing here. Rosh Hashanah 32a compares restraining women from shofar to the dispute over voluntary semichah. Regarding performance and bal tosif, there is no reason to distinguish whether the exclusion is local; if women may perform even where locally excluded, certainly they may where excluded only elsewhere. The prohibiting view likewise invokes bal tosif in either case, and no other tanna fits the baraisa. For the blessing formula, however, the distinction is clear: a local exclusion prevents saying 'Who commanded us,' whereas an external exemption does not. See my work on Orach Chaim from 5616, page 126a, for further discussion and resolution of Atzei Arazim's objection.

<b>Seif 5.</b> The kohanim should not ascend the duchan wearing shoes, etc. Note: See Magen Avraham, seif katan 5. What he wrote as a proof from siman 594 is no proof at all, for there the primary obligation is affliction and not pleasure, and therefore shoes were forbidden because of pleasure; with a garment that is not leather there is no pleasure. But here the primary prohibition is because perhaps one strap will break, and from what is written in Beis Yosef in this siman, which is from the Gemara, what difference is there between leather and cloth? See Beis Yosef in this siman, where this law is clearly proven, that there is no distinction between leather and non-leather wherever there is concern that a strap may break. Especially since one opinion is cited that they should walk entirely barefoot, therefore even those who permit do so only with stockings worn the entire foot; otherwise it is forbidden. I saw in Shaarei Teshuvah of the Gaon Rabbi Zalman Margolios, of blessed memory, that he wrote to see Responsa Knesses Yechezkel, siman 11, and I do not have it to examine. In my humble opinion it is forbidden; examine this well.

<b>Seif 6.</b> Although the kohanim washed their hands in the morning, they wash their hands again until the joint, etc. Note: I was asked whether the washing of hands for Nesias Kapayim requires a vessel, like washing for eating, or not. I answered that at first glance it would seem to require a vessel, like washing for a meal, since it resembles sanctifying the hands for the Temple service, which required a vessel from the laver. This also appears from the language of the Rambam cited in Beis Yosef, siman 205, who wrote: How so? A kohen who did not wash his hands may not perform Nesias Kapayim, but washes his hands until the joint, as they sanctify for the service. Since he wrote ‘as they sanctify for the service,’ this implies the law of washing for the service, which requires a vessel. But according to this it is difficult: how did the Beis Yosef then write that if he washed his hands in the morning he need not wash them again, since in the morning no vessel is required and now a vessel would be required? One cannot say that he is speaking of where he washed in the morning with a vessel, for then why did the Beis Yosef first write that this implies that when his hands are impure, such as when he did not wash in the morning, he must wash? It can be found even where he washed in the morning but not with a vessel. Rather he should have said: if he washed properly in the morning from a vessel he need not wash. Since he did not write so, it would prove that he holds no vessel is required. But if so, his proof from the service is astonishing, for there he sanctifies properly from the laver in the morning. Also Tosafot’s question against those who hold the morning washing suffices is difficult; why not answer that for words of Torah a vessel is not needed, whereas for Nesias Kapayim a vessel is needed? This proves that here too a vessel is not required. Yet one may say that it depends on this dispute: those who hold morning washing suffices hold no vessel is required, while those who hold it does not suffice hold a vessel is required. For now the practical law requires study, and it is astonishing that the early and later authorities did not note this. The view that morning washing suffices is not a proof, for perhaps the Sages enacted that if his hands have a presumption of purity through the morning washing, since then no vessel is required, they did not enact another washing for Nesias Kapayim; but if he did not wash and his hands are impure, they enacted washing for Nesias Kapayim like the Temple service, with a vessel. Similar to Chagigah, where terumah requires intent and chullin does not, but immersion for chullin that establishes him for chullin helps even for maaser. So too here one may say that according to all opinions a vessel is required when washing now. In any event, the words of Beis Yosef require study; examine this.

<b>Seif 22.</b> They endeavor that the prompter should be an Israelite, etc. Note: See Taz in the name of the Rambam, who learned this law from the verse ‘say to them,’ implying that the one saying is not from among them; see there. In my eyes this is very astonishing from the Gemara, page 38, which says that Rabbi Chisda said: we have a tradition that a kohen calls ‘Kohanim’ and an Israelite does not call ‘Kohanim,’ as it says ‘say to them,’ meaning that it shall be from their own. Thus we see the opposite, that ‘say to them’ explicitly means from their own. Although Abaye concludes there that the halacha is not like Rabbi Chisda, that only means that it need not specifically be from their own. From where do we derive the opposite, that it specifically should not be from their own? Why should we create opposite readings, that for Rabbi Chisda ‘say to them’ means from their own, while for Abaye it means the exact opposite, that he says to them and he is not from among them? This requires study; examine it.

<b>Se'if 38: If he drank a revi'is of wine at once, he may not raise his hands, etc.</b> Rambam, Hilchos Issurei Mizbe'ach 1:16, writes that the rule for entering from the altar inward and leaving there is the same: one who drinks a revi'is between the Ulam and altar, or tears his garments there, and leaves, is flogged. Raavad objects: on Rambam's reading, he need not drink between the Ulam and altar; Toras Kohanim's inclusion of exit means that even if he drank outside and entered the Heichal he is liable; during entry there must be no drunkenness in the Heichal or at the altar, with death also applicable. Kesef Mishneh explains Raavad as objecting that Rambam requires bringing wine inside to drink, asking who would bring it there. This is astonishing: one could have brought ordinary wine unlawfully or drunk libation wine, incurring both misuse and lashes for intoxication. Raavad instead means that if Toras Kohanim teaches liability for exit, the Tosefta need not be placed where he drank inside; it could concern drinking outside and receiving two sets of lashes, for entry and exit. That is the plain meaning. Rambam can be defended. Toras Kohanim derives exit from 'you and your sons with you.' 'With you' means similar to you, like the Sages' exposition of 'with you' regarding Noach. Aharon was then inside, as Parashas Shemini says, 'Do not leave the entrance of the Tent of Meeting'; the verse hints that if they then drank wine they would be liable, proving liability upon exit. This cannot teach two liabilities for entry and exit, since Aharon had not entered intoxicated and would not be 'similar to you.' It must concern drinking inside and liability on exit. Kesef Mishneh may share this reading but explain Raavad's objection differently. Ordinarily, 'if you grasp much, you grasp nothing,' so it is preferable to apply the inclusion to one who drank inside and incur one liability rather than to drinking outside and incur two. Kesef Mishneh explains that Raavad sees bringing wine inside as uncommon, making it more reasonable to derive two liabilities in the common case of drinking outside than one liability in the uncommon case. This resolves the matter well.

<b>Se'if 43: After seven days of mourning he raises his hands; during the seven days he should leave the synagogue when kohanim are called.</b> Magen Avraham writes, 'all the more so for us,' etc. Even if aninus does not apply because he does not intend burial today, he should apparently not raise his hands, since he lacks joy, as Magen Avraham says. This depends on the two rationales: according to Shach, once he is not an onen he may raise his hands; according to Magen Avraham he may not. Under Shach's view, a case I encountered can be resolved. A deceased kohen had a kohen brother; this occurred on Yom Tov, and the synagogue was so close to the house that other kohanim could not raise their hands there. The brother could seemingly enter and perform Birkas Kohanim. Some opinions in Shulchan Aruch permit relatives to become impure even not for burial needs. Although we rule otherwise, it remains a doubt. Shach, Yoreh De'ah 371, also treats impurity transmitted through an adjacent handbreadth as rabbinic. Magen Avraham disputes him and calls it Torah law, but Pnei Yehoshua in Berachos 3 and Sukkah 1 disagrees with Magen Avraham, preserving a second doubt. Thus perhaps non-burial impurity is permitted; and even if not, perhaps adjacent impurity is only rabbinic. Within a rabbinic matter one may rely on the permitting view, a double doubt like that used regarding blood in eggs. Even if he is an onen, if he does not intend burial today (or on Shabbos where Birkas Kohanim is performed) the brother may raise his hands in that adjacent synagogue when the others cannot. Under Magen Avraham's lack-of-joy rationale, however, he remains forbidden. The egg-blood comparison itself is difficult, since an opposite double doubt exists: perhaps the blood was in the prohibited place, and even if not, perhaps egg blood is Torah-prohibited. The matter requires consideration. My allowance to enter for Birkas Kohanim does not permit entering the adjacent house for a discretionary purpose. One may not deliberately create a rabbinic doubt or double doubt, comparable to deliberately nullifying prohibition. For a mitzvah, however, one may create such a doubt, just as siman 626 permits deliberate nullification for a mitzvah because the ordinary prohibition does not apply. Thus entry is permitted for the mitzvah but forbidden for personal purposes. I wrote similarly in my Yoreh De'ah responsa, siman 201. One may also say this is a double doubt involving something that will become permitted later, which ordinarily does not help; for a mitzvah it is not considered later-permissible, since tomorrow the mitzvah opportunity is gone.

<b>Se'if 45, gloss: It is forbidden to make use of a kohen even nowadays, as this resembles misuse of consecrated property, unless he waives it.</b> Taz permits it because 'you shall sanctify him' is for the kohen's satisfaction, and he would not be satisfied if forbidden to serve a Torah scholar. Tosafos, Sotah 40b, is difficult for this: even a king may not stand before a Torah scholar, because 'a mitzvah is different' applies only where that mitzvah exceeds royal honor; here the command that the king's awe be upon you is greater. Why not say the king gains satisfaction from standing? Evidently royal honor is not his to waive. Kohanic honor is likewise not his; indeed 'you shall sanctify him, for he offers your God's bread' expressly makes it Heaven's honor. Nor should a kohen be able to violate 'you shall sanctify him' for a scholar when the scholar is commanded to sanctify the kohen and not conversely. Saying the verse excludes scholars would likewise allow a king to stand, since royal awe would apply only to ordinary people and the king remains commanded in reverence for Hashem. Tosafos therefore implies that a kohen's waiver does not help. This explains King Shapur's words to Rav Huna bar Nasan when he raised his belt: 'A kingdom of priests and a holy nation is written about you.' 'A holy nation' is necessary. A Jewish king is himself within the holy nation; compared with a Torah scholar he has only the additional royal distinction, so royal honor takes precedence. A Jewish Torah scholar has two distinctions (kingdom of priests and holy nation) whereas the gentile king has one. Honoring the scholar is therefore the greater mitzvah, allowing the king to waive his honor. Thus Shapur acted consistently whether honoring Torah or royal dignity. Regarding Rema's ruling, I do not understand Taz and Magen Avraham's questions from Rabbeinu Tam's incident, Rava's kohen slave, or the rule that a kohen slave's ear is not pierced. There is a distinction between a Torah scholar and an am ha'aretz. An am ha'aretz may not use a kohen, but a Torah scholar is greater than an ignorant kohen; the Sages even say he should not yield precedence to such a kohen as a fixed kohanic right. Nedarim calls Torah scholars kohanim from 'David's sons were kohanim,' which Rashi explains as first precedence. Since a kohen may use another kohen, certainly a Torah scholar, himself called a kohen and superior to an ignorant kohen, may use him even without waiver; Taz's satisfaction rationale is unnecessary. Rema and Semag, cited by Magen Avraham in siman 201, allow waiver. Taz says waiver alone does not help, except where serving gives the kohen honor. This is difficult: universal custom lets a kohen honor another with leading Birkas HaMazon, although first blessing is also derived from 'you shall sanctify him.' If waiver helps there, why not for service, when both derive from one verse? The matter requires consideration.
