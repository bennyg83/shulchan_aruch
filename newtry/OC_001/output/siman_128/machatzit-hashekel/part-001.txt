**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Magen Avraham wrote in Even HaEzer, etc.</b> This requires examination, for Ramach, from whom Rama's ruling there derives, asks: What concern is there? We learned in Gittin 59b that a kohen reads first for the sake of peace, so that people should not quarrel and each say, 'I will read first.' Since the kohen reads first, there is no quarrel when one who is presumed a kohen by his own statement does so, and there is no prohibition. See siman 201. Ramach must hold like Tosafos and Tur that the exposition from 'you shall sanctify him' requiring that he bless first is only a support. Yet Magen Avraham there necessarily regards it as a full exposition, since the Gemara asks why the Mishnah attributes the kohen's reading first to peace when it is a Torah law from 'you shall sanctify him,' and answers that peace is needed to teach that the kohen may not waive his honor. If it were merely a rabbinic law supported by a verse, what would the Gemara's question be? Perhaps they supported it with the verse only because of peace. If it is truly a Torah law, however, he should not be believed on his own testimony even merely to read first.

**** ENGLISH ****
Perhaps Ri holds that the positive commandment violated by a non-kohen who raises his hands is the mentioning of the Divine Name in vain and the recitation of a blessing in vain, unlike Rashi's explanation. Thus Tosafos asks that, were it not a blessing in vain, no positive commandment would be violated. Although Berachos 33a says that one who recites an unnecessary blessing violates 'You shall not take,' Magen Avraham in siman 215 cites the dispute: Rambam regards it as a Torah prohibition, while Tosafos regards it as only a Scriptural support. In Temurah 4a the prohibition against pronouncing the Divine Name in vain is derived as a positive commandment from 'You shall fear Hashem your God,' whereas the exposition from 'You shall not take' is only a support. It is therefore a positive rather than a negative commandment, and there is no distinction between pronouncing the Name in vain and reciting a blessing in vain. Accordingly, the statement in Kesubos that a non-kohen violates a positive commandment is correct.

<b>(ס"ק מ) יכול כו'</b> וגדולה מזה אמרו כו' וכ"מ ברשב"א. ובס' א"ר הקשה דהא בתפל' קי"ל בסי' ק"ד דאינו מפסיק גם הרשב"א ס"ל דאפי' בק"ש אינו מפסיק ע"ש וכן בש"ע סי' ס"ו הביא ב' דעות לענין ק"ש ולענ"ד לק"מ דהכי פירושו וגדולה מזו אמרו גבי ק"ש דפוסק לקר"ה כ"ש דיש לפסוק לנשיאות כפים אי א"ל דעובר בעשה ואפי' במאן דפליג בקראוהו לתורה מ"מ הכא דאיכא עשה מודה וכן בתפלה דלכ"ע אינו מפסיק לעלות לתורה מ"מ הכא דאיכא עשה מפסיק וראיה מתשו' רשב"א שהביא הרב"י סי' קל"ה וז"ל כהן שקראו אותו לעלות לתורה והוא קורא ק"ש אינו רשאי להפסיק כו' שלא אמרו להקדימו אלא משום כבוד כהונתו ואי הוא פנוי ומקדימים אחר לפניו נמצאו מקילים בכבוד כהונתו אבל זה שאינו יכול לעלות משום ק"ש אין בכאן קול' אל כהונתו עכ"ל משמע דאי הוי זלזול בכבוד כהונה היה מודה רשב"א דמפסיק א"כ הכא דאיכ' עשה מודה רשב"א דמפסיק וכן לדידן מה"ט בתפל' לכ"ע אינו מפסיק דחמיר מק"ש היינו דוקא לקרות בתורה דאינו אלא משו' כבוד משא"כ הכא:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>He may interrupt in order to duchan.</b> Eliyah Rabbah asks that prayer may not be interrupted, as stated in siman 104, and Rashba forbids interruption even during Shema; siman 66 records two views. The answer is clear. Shema may be interrupted for a Torah reading according to the lenient view, and all the more for duchaning when he was called, since otherwise he violates a positive commandment. Even the stringent view regarding Torah reading agrees here because a positive commandment is involved. During the Amidah all agree that one does not interrupt for Torah reading, but duchaning is different because of that positive commandment. Rashba's responsum 135 proves this: A kohen called to the Torah during Shema does not interrupt because the call is only an honor, and inability to ascend causes no disgrace. This implies that if disgrace were involved he would interrupt; all the more so here, where a positive commandment is involved. The same applies during the Amidah.

**** ENGLISH ****
What is the law if all the kohanim wish to lower their talleisim? Lowering it for only one kohen certainly does not help, because his difference from the others attracts attention; it appears that even all of them may not lower them. Taz asks that if the congregation lowers its talleisim, it should be permitted even if the kohanim do not. Eliyah Rabbah answers that only lowering by the kohanim helps. The congregation lowers its talleisim voluntarily, and members may raise them and look at a kohen whose appearance is unusual.
