<b>אין לדבר</b>. צ"ע דלפעמים מפסיקין בתחינות כגון אל רחום. וצריך ליזהר שלא להפסיק בדבור מיהו בסי' נ"א ס"ד משמע דאין קפידא כ"כ וכ"כ בב"י בשם ת"ר דוקא כשמפסיק ועוסק לדבר דברי' אחרים אסור אבל שיח' בעלמא לית לן בה וכ"מ סי' קכ"ד ס"ד דדוקא משום חזרת הש"ץ יש להם לשתוק הא לא"ה אין איסור ויש לדחות מ"מ נ"ל מ"ש, ובריב"ש סימן תי"ב כתב שאשתו של ר"א היתה לוקחת אותו בדברים עד שהיה שוכח מליפול ע"ש ואם כן אין ראיה שלא להפסיק עב"י, מותר להתפלל במקום א' וליפול במקום אח' [ס"ח ועיין במגילה דף כ"ב]:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>One should not speak.</b> This requires study, for at times interruptions are made with supplications such as El Rachum. One must be careful not to interrupt with speech. Nevertheless, siman 51, seif 4, implies that the matter is not treated so strictly. Beit Yosef likewise writes in the name of Terumas HaDeshen that it is forbidden specifically when one interrupts and occupies himself with other matters, but casual conversation is not of concern. This also appears from siman 124, seif 4, which states that the congregation must remain silent specifically because of the chazzan’s repetition, implying that otherwise there is no prohibition. This may be rejected, but what I wrote nevertheless appears correct. Rivash, siman 412, writes that Rabbi Elazar’s wife would engage him in conversation until he forgot to perform Nefilas Apayim. See there. There is therefore no proof that interruption is prohibited. See Beit Yosef. One may pray in one place and perform Nefilas Apayim in another. Sefer Chassidim; see Megillah 22b.

It appears to me that this is why that psalm is not recited in these countries.

It therefore appears to me that on Chanukah it is preferable for each person to recite Hallel at home. I likewise found this written in the name of Maharan.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>At Minchah.</b> Bach writes that Maharshal ruled that Tachanun is not recited when the congregation prays in the presence of the infant, and this is the practice in Brisk of Lithuania. Hagahos Maimoniyos, he explains, discusses a case where they do not pray in the infant’s presence. Its wording, however, is: ‘Nefilas Apayim is not performed during the Yotzer prayer on the day of a bris, and likewise on the wedding day in the place where the groom prays.’ Since it mentions the groom on the wedding day but the bris specifically during the Yotzer prayer, this implies that at Minchah Nefilas Apayim is performed even when praying in the infant’s presence. Otherwise it should have combined the two cases and stated that on the wedding day and the day of the bris it is omitted where the groom or infant is present. Nevertheless, where a contrary custom exists, it should be followed. Mahari writes that in the Rhineland grooms would leave the synagogue on Thursday before Retzei so the congregation could recite Tachanun, and all the more so on Friday, the principal day of the wedding. Darkei Moshe. In these countries too, the custom is that a groom does not enter the synagogue for one or two days before the wedding, perhaps for this reason. If he does enter, however, Tachanun is recited except on the wedding day itself, as Rama writes here. Some have the custom not to perform Nefilas Apayim throughout all seven days when the groom is present in the synagogue. Even on Friday, which was the eighth day from the wedding, they did not perform it because the wedding had taken place close to evening. Knesses HaGedolah.

**** HEBREW ****
<b>אם יציעו</b>. פי' כשמציעין עשבי' להפסיק בין הרצפה דאז שרי בהטיה ובקצת ספרים הגיה או יציעו וכו' פי' ואז אפילו בלא הטיה שרי דדוקא על הקרקע אוסר בלא הטיה שמא יש תחתיו רצפת אבנים ונבנה עליה דקרקע לא חשיב הפסק שארצו של בית כמוהו עד התהום כדאי' פט"ו דכלים אבל עשבים חשיבי הפסק וכ"כ הרמב"ם פ"ל מע"א וכן עיקר וכנ"ל דלא כמ"ש הריב"ש סי' תי"ב ע"ש ומשמע בר"ה א"צ עשבים דא"נ על פניהם רק כורעים ומשתחוים בעלינו אבל ביה"כ נופלים על פניהם בסדר העבודה:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>If they spread coverings.</b> Meaning, if they spread grasses to interpose between themselves and the floor, then prostration is permitted with an inclination. Some texts read, ‘or if they spread,’ meaning that in that case it is permitted even without inclining. The prohibition without an inclination applies specifically upon the ground, lest a stone floor lie beneath it and the ground was built over it. Earth is not considered an interposition, since the ground of a house is legally continuous down to the depths, as stated in chapter 15 of Keilim. Grasses, however, are considered an interposition. Rambam writes likewise in Hilchos Avodah Zarah, and this is the primary ruling, unlike Rivash, siman 412; see there. This implies that on Rosh Hashanah grasses are unnecessary, because people do not fall upon their faces but merely kneel and bow during Aleinu. On Yom Kippur, however, they fall upon their faces during the Avodah service.
