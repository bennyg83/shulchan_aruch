<b>A new fruit.</b> For the two days are like one long day, and how could one say Shehecheyanu over the Yom Tov twice on one day? This is only initially. But if he has no new fruit, he should certainly say Shehecheyanu, as on other Yamim Tovim, for there is no difference between them and Rosh Hashanah except regarding an egg and something attached to the ground, that what belongs to one day is forbidden on the other. But not regarding other matters. The reason is that even in the time of the beis din, they would sometimes observe two days even though there was no doubt, for the second day was the main day, and they would complete the first day in sanctity so that people not treat it lightly the following year. Regarding Shehecheyanu we say whichever way you look at it: if it is a doubt, one says Shehecheyanu; and if we rely on the custom of the beis din, then today is holy and tomorrow is holy, for from the second day they count for establishing the festivals, and that is the main Rosh Hashanah. <b>Question.</b> One congregation had a shofar on Erev Rosh Hashanah, and robbers came and robbed them and took the shofar as well. The congregation sent to another congregation to send them one, but the messenger was delayed by compulsion and did not arrive until the end of the second day. Rosh Hashanah was on Thursday and Friday. At the time the shofar arrived, they had already prayed the Shabbos prayer, but it was still broad daylight. What is the law: may they blow at that time, and we not be concerned for the shevus that we do not blow on Shabbos? <b>Answer.</b> Let us copy the words of the Mordechai in chapter Bameh Madlikin. This is his language: We establish the Mishnah that when it is doubtful whether it is dark one may make an eruv chatzeiros. Ri and Rabbeinu Chananel explained, and so it is explicit in the Yerushalmi, that the same applies to eruv tavshilin: when it is doubtful whether it is dark, one may make an eruv. Rabbeinu Yoel ruled that the same applies after the Maariv prayer: they may make an eruv if it is still day, for answering Barechu is like shofar blowing. Before doubtful darkness they would accept Shabbos and blow, in order to add from the weekday onto the holy. Even so, on Erev Shabbos he permits making an eruv chatzeiros when it is doubtful whether it is dark, and the same applies after answering Barechu. I later found in the name of Rabbeinu Shemaryah that he ruled so, and he brought proof from what Rabbeinu Yoel wrote. Some of his students forbade it and said that there is a difference between where one accepted Shabbos upon himself and where he did not accept it. The Rav retracted and forbade it, bringing proof from the Tosefta in Sukkah: once he began the third blast, even if he had a kettle in his hand he places it on the ground. Yet regarding doubtful darkness we learned that one may make an eruv and insulate hot food. We see from here that acceptance is more stringent than doubt. End quote. I am not worthy to decide, but this proof from the Tosefta is astonishing to me, for it is not difficult at all. In our Mishnah we say, and they insulate the hot food, meaning insulation alone, not any cooking. But that Tosefta speaks of placing the kettle on the fire to cook, and that is forbidden after the third shofar blast. Know that in chapter Bameh Madlikin, folio 38, it says: and the pots are placed on the stove; when he began the third blast, the one who removes removes and the one who insulates insulates. Thus insulation is permitted after the third blast. Here it says that he places the kettle on the ground after the third blast. It must be that there is a distinction as I wrote. In Orach Chayim, siman 342, the Beis Yosef brings in the name of the Rambam: All matters forbidden because of shevus were not decreed during bein hashmashos, but only on the day itself. During bein hashmashos they are permitted, provided there is some matter of mitzvah or pressing need. How so? During bein hashmashos it is permitted to climb a tree to bring a shofar, etc. But if there is no pressing need or mitzvah, it is forbidden. Therefore one may not tithe definite tevel, even though the prohibition of separating maaser on Shabbos is only because of shevus, but one may tithe demai. End quote. He says that the prohibition of shevus is only on the day itself; this implies that it is not so during the addition that one adds and accepts upon himself as extra sanctity. It appears that even those above who are stringent regarding acceptance more than regarding doubtful darkness would agree here that acceptance is more lenient. Specifically above, regarding making an eruv, which is a discretionary matter, acceptance is more stringent, for since he accepted upon himself additional sanctity, and he has permission to do this, and there is even an obligation in it, the prohibition of Shabbos itself certainly takes effect upon him. This is unlike doubtful darkness where he did not accept it, but the sanctity takes effect automatically; there is reason for leniency, for perhaps the time of sanctity has not yet arrived. But here, regarding a mitzvah, such as blowing shofar and the like, the reasoning is the opposite. During doubtful darkness there is reason to forbid, for perhaps it is night. But where he accepted it upon himself and prohibited upon himself the Shabbos prohibitions, there is certainly reason to say that he did not accept it with this in mind. Certainly every person wants to fulfill the mitzvos of the Holy One, blessed is He, and had he known that afterward he would be able to fulfill the mitzvah of the day, he would not have accepted the sanctity of Shabbos upon himself. This is like acceptance in error regarding a cloudy day, which is not an acceptance, as written in siman 263. What difference is there between an error due to cloudiness and an error regarding fulfillment of a mitzvah? In both, had he known the truth, he would not have accepted it. If so, since during bein hashmashos they permitted shevus and did not decree in that time, it is obvious that they did not decree before bein hashmashos where it is for a mitzvah. It further appears that even if he knew that a shofar would be brought afterward and nevertheless accepted Shabbos upon himself, it does not help him to uproot the obligation of shofar, for his acceptance is no stronger than an oath, and we rule that an oath does not take effect to violate a mitzvah. The stringent view in the Mordechai regarding acceptance was in a discretionary matter. This does not contradict what is written in Orach Chayim regarding Shemini Atzeres, that if one accepted the sanctity of Shemini Atzeres upon himself he may afterward eat before nightfall without the blessing of sukkah, unlike Maharshal in a responsum as mentioned there. There it is different, for the obligation of the mitzvah of sukkah is not upon him in a way that you can say his acceptance is against the mitzvah, since if he wants he need not eat at all and it will be passive. This is unlike here, regarding shofar, where his obligation is active, and his acceptance would nullify it; he does not have the power to do that. All the more so here, where he was inadvertent, for he did not know that a shofar would be here. <b>All</b> this I write only to broaden the matter, but there appears to be an explicit proof for our case from the law of milah in Yoreh Deah, siman 262. The Beis Yosef brings there in the name of the Semag as follows: It does not depend on prayer at all; if they accepted Shabbos while it was still day, neither to be lenient nor to be stringent. End quote. This must mean as follows: neither to be lenient and push off Shabbos, namely where the child was born on Shabbos after the Maariv prayer, for example where there was still day after the prayer, we do not say that he already made it night and the child will be circumcised specifically on Sunday and not on Shabbos. It teaches us that we do not follow the prayer, and he is circumcised on Shabbos. Nor to be stringent, for example if he was born on Erev Shabbos after accepting Shabbos in prayer, we do not say that he made it night and he should be circumcised on Shabbos; rather, it is as though he had not prayed the Shabbos prayer, and it does not push off Shabbos. If so, the same law applies exactly here: if it was impossible to circumcise on Friday until its end, and he had already prayed the Shabbos prayer while it was still day, then he may circumcise him, for in this case he is obligated to circumcise him, and we do not pay attention to the prayer through which he made it Shabbos. What difference is there between the coming Shabbos regarding circumcising him on Shabbos and the case where he could not circumcise until the end of the day? The reason is that the mitzvah does not depend on his prayer to push aside the mitzvah at its time. This is not comparable to what is written in Yoreh Deah, siman 402, in Shulchan Aruch, that one who prayed Maariv should not then begin mourning, because that would be two leniencies contradicting one another. There, it is a stringency; what loss is there if he does not begin until tomorrow? So too regarding the seven clean days, that she should not begin counting until tomorrow if they already prayed Maariv even though it is still day; that too is because of stringency, and in truth after the fact it works, as stated there. The general rule in this matter is that in a place where there is an obligation of a mitzvah at that very time, this person's acceptance of Shabbos does not help to nullify that obligation of the mitzvah, since it is impossible to fulfill the mitzvah properly in another way. It follows that in the cases of shofar and milah that we mentioned, he is obligated to blow and to circumcise at that time while it is still day, even if he already prayed the Maariv prayer of Shabbos. However, it appears to me that in any event there are two contradictory matters here, for everyone among us knows that we do not blow shofar on Shabbos, and he has already made it Shabbos. Since blessings do not prevent fulfillment, he should therefore blow without a blessing. But with milah there is no contradiction, because milah applies even on Shabbos when it is in its time. Do not ask: with shofar too there is a contradiction, since we do not blow on Shabbos. That is not difficult. Certainly we do not nullify a mitzvah in its time because of contradiction; we say this only where the mitzvah does not depend on that time. Furthermore, even on Shabbos there is no prohibition to blow, for it is a skill and not a melachah, as stated in the Gemara. Rather, one does not blow on Shabbos because of the decree lest he carry it in the public domain. Whenever he blows by way of a voluntary act, there is no such concern. If so, this blowing at the end of the day does not contradict what he accepted upon himself as Shabbos, for it is possible that he is blowing voluntarily, and the contradiction is not evident. This is unlike the blessing over the blowing, which certainly creates a contradiction. So it appears to me clearly in this matter. <b>Afterward</b> I saw in the Tosefta, chapter 5, which the Mordechai I mentioned brought, and it has another text. This is its language: The third blast was to separate the people, etc. If one took a scroll to read, they take it from him; one removes a cooked dish from the stove, they insulate a kettle for him, and they light the lamp. When he finished reading, even if he had a kettle in his hand, they do not insulate it, but he places it on the ground; even if he had a lamp in his hand, etc. End quote. The phrase finished reading means finished blowing, and so it is in Semag in Hilchos Shabbos. It appears to me that since it compares insulating hot water to lighting the lamp, and in our Gemara it says that when it is doubtful whether it is dark one may not light lamps, yet nevertheless one may insulate hot water, this implies that the insulation of hot water in the Tosefta is a severe melachah. That is, they insulate a cold cooked item in a way that it has not yet been cooked, and they insulate it so that afterward it will cook, like our manner of insulation. It emerges that the question of the students mentioned there is no question at all. What is mentioned, that they insulate hot water, refers to cooked hot water that they insulate only so it should not cool, and that is permitted specifically when it is doubtful whether it is dark, not before then. There is no proof from this at all to distinguish between accepting Shabbos and doubtful darkness. So it appears to me, David the small, HaLevi.
