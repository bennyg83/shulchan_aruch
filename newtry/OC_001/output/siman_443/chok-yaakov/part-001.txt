<b>אסור בהנאה. </b> וכ' המ"א ומ"מ אינו עובר בבל יראה ובל ימצא עד הלילה רק מ"ע דתשביתו מ"מ פ"ג עכ"ל וכ"כ בתשובת מהר"ם ב"ב סי' קצ"ב וע' בתמים דעים סי' רמ"ה וע' בב"ק דף כ"ט ע"ב בפירש"י ד"ה משש שעות ולמעלה וכו' לעבור עליו בב"י וב"י מבואר להדיא דסובר דעד הלילה ג"כ קעבר בבל יראה ובל ימצא:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Forbidden in benefit.</b> The Magen Avraham wrote: nevertheless he does not transgress bal yera'eh and bal yimatzei until night, only the positive mitzvah of tashbisu; Maggid Mishneh chapter 3, end quote. So too is written in Responsa Maharam Bar Baruch, siman 192, and see Tamim De'im, siman 245. See Bava Kamma folio 29b in Rashi, s.v. 'from six hours and onward,' etc., 'to transgress concerning it bal yera'eh and bal yimatzei.' It is explicit that he holds that until night he also transgresses bal yera'eh and bal yimatzei.

<b>מתחלת שעה. </b> לכן ימהר לאכול בשעה ד' וכ' במהרי"ל מת בע"פ יוציאו המת לבית הקברות ויחזרו לביתם ויאכלו ואח"כ יקברוהו עכ"ל ובמ"א מתמיה על מהרי"ל דאיך יפסיקו לאכילה ונעלם ממנו מנהג אשכנז שכן נוהגים אף בשאר ימים להוציא את המת לבה"ק וחוזרים לביתם עד שחפרו החופרים ושאר צרכי המת ואחר כך חוזרים וקורין לקבורת המת וא"כ ליתא כאן הפסקה כלל. ועוד דאם יאכלו קודם הוצאת המת ממילא ימשוך האכילה וביעורו עד זמנו ואחר כך ע"י הוצאת המת חוץ לעיר וקבורתו ימנעו מלהתעסק בצרכי י"ט ולישות המצות על כן הקדימו בהוצאתו והפסיקו לאכילה דאל"כ ימנעו לגמרי מלהתעסק בקבורת המת וק"ל:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>From the beginning of the hour.</b> Therefore one should hurry to eat in the fourth hour, etc. Maharil writes: if someone died on erev Pesach, they take the deceased out to the cemetery, return home and eat, and afterward bury him, end quote. The Magen Avraham wonders about Maharil: how can they interrupt for eating? The Ashkenazic custom was hidden from him, for this is what they customarily do even on other days: they take the deceased out to the cemetery and return home until the diggers have dug and the other needs of the deceased are prepared; afterward they return and call people for the burial of the deceased. If so, there is no interruption here at all. Furthermore, if they eat before taking the deceased out, the eating and the destruction of chametz will naturally continue until its time, and afterward, because of taking the deceased outside the city and burying him, they will be prevented from attending to the needs of Yom Tov and kneading the matzos. Therefore they first took him out and then interrupted for eating; otherwise they would be completely prevented from attending to the burial of the deceased. This is clear to me.

<b>אפי' הרבה ביחד. </b> ואפי' כותח או יין שרף אף שנקרא שם בעליו עליו מותר למכרו קודם פסח בשעת היתר לנכרי אף שיודע שלא יכלה קודם פסח כן הסכמת האחרונים דלא כב"ח שמחמיר בזה והוא חומרא יתירה:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Even a large amount together.</b> Even kutach or liquor, although its owner's name is associated with it, may be sold before Pesach, at a permitted time, to a non-Jew, even if he knows that the non-Jew will not finish it before Pesach. This is the consensus of the later authorities, unlike the Bach, who is stringent in this, and that is an excessive stringency.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And from the beginning of the sixth hour and onward they forbade it also in benefit.</b> The Tur concludes: and if he betrothed a woman with it, she is not betrothed. The Beis Yosef asks from the Tur's words here against his words in Even HaEzer, and the Bach and the Taz present the Beis Yosef as having made a complete error. Also in Shulchan Aruch, Even HaEzer siman 28, the later authorities are very astonished at the Beis Yosef in Shulchan Aruch. In my humble opinion, however, the words of the Beis Yosef here and there in Even HaEzer are correct without any doubt at all. I will therefore first set out the source of the law. We learn in Pesachim folio 7 regarding what Rabbi Yehudah said, that one who checks must nullify, etc. The Shas asks: let him nullify it in the sixth hour. The Shas answers: since a rabbinic prohibition has taken effect on it, it is like a Torah prohibition; it is not in his possession and he cannot nullify it, for Rav Giddel said in the name of Rav: one who betroths a woman from six hours and onward, even with wheat of Kordanya, we are not concerned for the kiddushin. Rashi explains: from six hours and onward means from the beginning of the sixth hour, for it is a rabbinic prohibition, end quote. Tosafos wrote: this is not clear to Rabbeinu Tam, for 'from six hours and onward' implies from the end of six hours; nevertheless the proof is from wheat of Kordanya, which is hard and is chametz nukshah, which is also forbidden only rabbinically. The later authorities understood here and in Even HaEzer that even according to Rashi, wheat of Kordanya is only rabbinic chametz, so that there are also rabbinic hours, namely the beginning of the sixth hour; nevertheless there is no kiddushin even with two rabbinic factors. In truth, in my humble opinion, this is not at all compelled in Rashi's view. It is possible that according to Rashi too, with two rabbinic factors there is no kiddushin, but he holds that wheat of Kordanya is not chametz nukshah, or he holds like the opinion that chametz nukshah is a Torah prohibition. So one must say, and there is a clear and necessary proof from Rashi's view in the chapter Kol Sha'ah, Pesachim folio 28, where he wrote on Rav Giddel's statement: 'from six hours' must mean from the beginning of six, for if it were after six, it is forbidden according to the Torah; what does he come to teach us? It is obvious that one cannot betroth with a benefit-prohibition. It is explicit in Rashi's view that if wheat of Kordanya became chametz, it is a Torah prohibition; otherwise what did Rashi prove by saying that if after six it is forbidden according to the Torah, what does he come to teach us? He could still be teaching us about rabbinic chametz nukshah. Rather, it is certainly Torah-level chametz. This is also implied by Tosafos at the beginning of Pesachim, who originated this law that it is rabbinic chametz on their own, not that this is Rashi's view. Indeed, from Tosafos at the end of HaIsh Mekadesh, Kiddushin folio 58, on the sugya of non-sacred animals slaughtered in the Temple courtyard, where Rabbi Shimon says she is betrothed, the Shas asks that apparently according to Rabbi Shimon non-sacred animals slaughtered in the Temple courtyard are not prohibited according to the Torah, and challenges from another source, etc. It answers that here we are dealing with one who slaughtered and it was found to be a tereifah, which is slaughter that is not fit, and even if slaughtered in the Temple courtyard they are permitted in benefit. Tosafos wrote there: it implies that non-sacred animals slaughtered in the Temple courtyard are not a Torah prohibition, only a rabbinic prohibition of benefit, and it is difficult: how can he betroth with them? Rav Giddel said in the first chapter of Pesachim that one who betroths from six hours and onward, even with wheat of Kordanya, which is forbidden only rabbinically, we are not concerned for the kiddushin. One can answer that there, at least the hours are Torah-level according to the way Rabbeinu Tam explained there, but here there is only a rabbinic prohibition. According to Rashi's explanation too, there is no difficulty according to the conclusion, for even according to Rabbi Shimon she is not betrothed, end quote of Tosafos. From Tosafos writing that according to Rashi there is no difficulty according to the conclusion, it implies that before the conclusion the matter did not fit Rashi's explanation, because even with two rabbinic factors she is not betrothed. The Maharsha explains Tosafos there this way, and this is also the view of the Rav of Bartenura at the beginning of Kol Sha'ah. According to this, one must force Rashi's language in Kol Sha'ah that I cited and explain it as follows: since he says 'even with wheat of Kordanya,' it implies that the law itself refers to rabbinic hours, for if it could be speaking of Torah-law hours, why say 'even'? Even with wheat of Kordanya, which is rabbinic, the whole law would have been stated only for that, because where the chametz is Torah-level and the benefit-prohibition is Torah-level, what would he be teaching us by saying she is not betrothed? Rather, he certainly speaks of rabbinic hours, and therefore he says that even with two rabbinic factors she is also not betrothed. After these words of Tosafos the later authorities may have followed. But in my opinion and the opinion of the Beis Yosef, this explanation is forced and very distant from Rashi's meaning. The intent of Tosafos at the beginning of HaIsh Mekadesh can be explained that they spoke only by way of broad analysis: according to their own approach, that wheat of Kordanya is only rabbinic, even if Rav Giddel's words are explained as Rashi explains, namely with rabbinic hours, nevertheless according to the conclusion there is no difficulty. But in truth, Rashi's own view is that he is speaking of Torah-level chametz, and one must say so, because otherwise the plain meaning of the Kiddushin sugya is difficult for Rashi. There the Gemara says, 'apparently he holds that non-sacred animals slaughtered in the Temple courtyard are not forbidden according to the Torah,' which implies that with a rabbinic prohibition of benefit she is betrothed. Although according to the conclusion this does not stand, that is because Rabbi Shimon holds that non-sacred animals in the Temple courtyard are a Torah prohibition and the case is where one slaughtered and it was found to be a tereifah; but with this point the plain implication of the Shas is that with rabbinic benefit-prohibitions she is betrothed. Thus the Beis Yosef's words are correct: the Tur's ruling here follows Rashi and the Rambam regarding Torah-level chametz and rabbinic hours, while in Even HaEzer he discussed Rabbeinu Tam's approach and the Rosh's view. This resolves the contradiction, and the challenges of the later authorities are not decisive.

<b>He is obligated to destroy it at the time of its prohibition.</b> Even though the chametz is not his, nevertheless it is incumbent upon him to destroy it so that the depositor will not transgress concerning it, if we do not know that he sold it; a doubt does not remove a certainty. Bach. The Magen Avraham wrote: it appears that the bailee is obligated to pay the value of the deposit to the depositor, for he should have sold it before the time of its prohibition. One cannot say that the depositor was negligent, for he should have sold it to a non-Jew wherever it was, because we rule that the bailee is not permitted to rely on this, since a doubt does not remove a certainty and he is obligated to destroy it; and he is obligated to sell it. If so, he was negligent by not selling it. However, if the chametz is intact, he can say to him: here is yours before you. So too it is implied in Bava Kamma folio 45, end quote of the Magen Avraham. Regarding the law of 'here is yours before you,' this is correct, as is explicit there in the Shach and on folio 98. Although I saw in Responsa Sha'ar Ephraim, siman 28, that he is uncertain about this law because Maharshal in Yam Shel Shlomo, chapter HaGozel Kama, siman 4, distinguishes between one who stole chametz and a guardian, and concludes there that in all benefit-prohibitions in the world we say 'here is yours before you' regarding a robber but not regarding a guardian, end quote of Maharshal. However, the Shach to Choshen Mishpat siman 363 disagreed with him; see there, and in Sha'ar Ephraim he discusses the matter at length to decide between the views. In truth, however, this is a great error and he labored over it for nothing, for Maharshal did not write there to decide that regarding a guardian we do not say 'here is yours before you' as a general halachah against the Rabbis. He said it only regarding an ox whose judgment was completed, where one could have sent it to the marshes; but regarding chametz on Pesach, where the prohibition comes automatically, everyone agrees that we say 'here is yours before you.' Otherwise they should have disputed regarding chametz on Pesach, as is explicit in the Shas there in two places. This is clear to one who studies Maharshal's words carefully. However, regarding the main law that the Magen Avraham wrote, that if the chametz is not intact, such as where he destroyed it during its time of prohibition, the bailee must pay, and as is also implied there in Responsa Sha'ar Ephraim siman 28, his words are not correct. The Rambam and Choshen Mishpat siman 292 wrote that the bailee is obligated to sell it only because of returning a lost object to its owners. So too it is somewhat implied from Rashi in Bava Kamma folio 98, who explains that since the Gemara disputes concerning an ox and a guardian, it should also have disputed concerning one who stole chametz and Pesach passed over it. Since, regarding chametz, it mentioned theft and did not mention a guardian, this implies that a guardian is certainly exempt even without saying 'here is yours before you.' In a responsum to one scholar I explained this law at greater length, and I will copy it nearby. See in the words of the later authorities that the same applies if a Jew pledged a pledge with another Jew; regarding this matter its law is like that of a guardian. See the Rosh and the rulings of the Rosh in chapter HaGozel Etzim: although one who stole chametz and Pesach passed over it can say 'here is yours before you,' nevertheless if he swore falsely about it, he does not achieve atonement until he pays him money value for it. This requires analysis, because this law is not mentioned in Choshen Mishpat siman 363 regarding theft; see there in Yam Shel Shlomo. I was asked as follows: an incident occurred in a country near Hamburg, where one Jew deposited his chametz, worth three hundred, which was actual chametz, with another Jew who lived in another city. The chametz remained with him into Pesach, and the bailee did not sell that chametz to a non-Jew. Then that chametz was stolen from him during Pesach without negligence on his part, and we were uncertain upon whom the loss falls. According to what the Magen Avraham wrote at the end of siman 443, that specifically if it is intact he can say 'here is yours before you,' but if it was stolen and he cannot say 'here is yours before you,' the bailee must pay the depositor. But it seems obvious to me that even if the failure to sell the chametz is called negligence, nevertheless when it was stolen during Pesach he is exempt, since for not selling the chametz he is exempt because it is damage that is not visible, and for the theft he is exempt because he was not negligent in the theft and he is an unpaid guardian. Furthermore, the very law written by the Magen Avraham, to obligate him for not selling because it is negligence for which an unpaid guardian is liable, requires analysis. From Rashi in Bava Kamma folio 98, s.v. 'since he disputed regarding an ox,' it sounds that with a guardian there is no basis to obligate him at all for chametz because he was negligent and did not sell it. The Rambam and Choshen Mishpat siman 292 se'if 17 wrote: one who deposits chametz with another and erev Pesach arrives after the fifth hour, he goes out and sells it in the market because of returning a lost object to the owners. Thus he is not obligated under the law of guardianship, but under the law of returning a lost object to the owners; and one is not obligated with a lost object to involve himself so extensively in a sale. I later saw in Responsa Divrei Rivos, siman 123, regarding a guardian of orphans who was negligent by not selling the orphans' property and thereby caused a loss, that this is not called negligence, for it is merely indirect causation, end quote. Although the questioner wrote at length, in my humble opinion most of it is not relevant to this case, as I answered him at length. Therefore I abbreviated here and copied only what pertains to the main law. This is what I answered: if the bailee were being claimed during Pesach, when he could not say 'here is yours before you,' since everyone is commanded to destroy it then it is as though it is already burnt, so he is not returning anything. For this reason the Shas and poskim specifically spoke of chametz over which Pesach had passed, where he says 'here is yours before you,' unlike during Pesach. As stated in Bava Kamma folio 98, if one stole chametz before Pesach and during the festival another came and burned it, he is exempt because everyone is commanded to destroy it. If so, even if it was stolen or became an unavoidable loss during the festival, it could be considered beginning in negligence and ending in unavoidable loss, for which one is liable, as stated in siman 291 se'if 6; see the Sema there. Here too we could connect the unavoidable loss to the negligence, for had he sold it to a non-Jew outside the house, it would have been better guarded by him, and all the more so if he could have sold it directly to a non-Jew and guarded the money. But what was written to reject the Magen Avraham's view, that this is not called negligence, from Rashi in Bava Kamma and from the Rambam and Choshen Mishpat siman 292, in this his lips clearly spoke, and this too is what I intended in my work in siman 243 s.k. 7, in the law though not in his reasoning. Still I must clarify: from his words it sounds that he challenges the Magen Avraham only because an unpaid guardian should not be liable for this negligence, but if he were a paid guardian he would be liable; so too it is implied in Responsa Sha'ar Ephraim siman 28. If so, the difficulty from Rashi returns: Rashi explains that the Gemara should have disputed regarding theft, so why did it not also explain it regarding a paid guardian? In truth, the main proof from Rashi can be questioned, because Rashi may have explained theft because the Mishnah discusses theft, and from the Mishnah it is proven that they do not dispute, as Rashi explicitly explains in Bava Kamma folio 45, s.v. 'they should dispute,' etc.; therefore I was precise in my language in my work, and it is only somewhat implied from Rashi, not a complete proof. Further, what is clear from his words, that since the bailee is obligated to sell only because of returning a lost object, he is not obligated regarding a lost object to involve himself so extensively in selling it, is not clear to me at all. According to the Rambam in the laws of lost objects, one who guards a lost object is a paid guardian. If he sees the lost object going to loss, he must make an arrangement so that it should not be lost, since he has already acquired responsibility for the lost object; if he does not do so, even though it may not be called negligence, it certainly is not unavoidable, and he must pay according to the Rambam, like a paid guardian. According to those who disagree, he is an unpaid guardian, and it is a doubt in law, as explained in the Beis Yosef, siman 72. If so, the same applies in our case: even though he was an unpaid guardian, when the time close to six arrived and he became obligated because of returning a lost object, he became like a paid guardian at that hour, since the deposit was in his hand and he was then like one guarding a lost object; according to the Rambam, he is like a paid guardian because of Rav Yosef's perutah, as I wrote in my work Toras HaShelamim, question 15; see there. Therefore according to the Rambam there is at first glance reason to obligate him as a paid guardian at that time for not selling. Do not answer from what the Rambam and Shulchan Aruch, Choshen Mishpat at the end of siman 292, wrote, that if he sold the deposit by instruction of beis din he is permitted to use the money and therefore is a paid guardian for it, which implies that if he were not permitted to use the money he would not be a paid guardian. Why do we not say he is like a paid guardian because he became a guardian of a lost object at the time of sale, since it was going to be lost? That is no refutation, for after he has already sold the deposit he has fulfilled the return of the lost object in that regard, and afterward he is only over the money as he was initially over the deposit, an unpaid guardian unless he is permitted to use it. This is unlike the very hour when it is going to be lost, when he is an actual guardian of a lost object. However, there is another reason to exempt him, namely what the Rambam and Shulchan Aruch write at the end of siman 292: this applies to a deposit whose owners went overseas, but if the owner is with him in that land, he should not touch it even though it will be lost, because he caused the loss to himself and it is like knowingly abandoning it. If so, the same applies here. Although the language of the Rosh and Tur says specifically that he is with him in that city, the Sema already wrote there that city is not literal; see there. And even if you say city is literal, that is only with items going to loss, becoming wormy, or becoming chametz, where if the depositor is not in the city and would not know that his fruit is going to loss, the bailee is obligated to return his lost object. But with chametz on Pesach, since the depositor knew that his chametz was in the bailee's hand and that its time of prohibition would arrive, he should have sold it in his own place. Even if he was far from the chametz, he could have made arrangements regarding the sale, as explained in Responsa Maharash Levi, siman 12, and above in siman 448. Although the bailee could not rely on this and is nevertheless obligated to destroy it, as the Magen Avraham wrote, the depositor should have notified the bailee in writing or through an agent. If he did not sell it and did not notify the bailee, he caused his own loss and it is a knowing loss. Nevertheless, the bailee is obligated to destroy it so that the depositor should not transgress bal yera'eh. However, if the depositor was overseas in a manner that he could not notify, and even if he sold it in his own place the bailee would still have to destroy it, then the bailee is obligated to sell it because of returning a lost object. It is possible to obligate him if he did not sell it according to the Rambam, as we wrote; nevertheless it is certainly not negligence, as we proved. An administrator who did not sell chametz certainly cannot be obligated, since he is liable only for negligence, as explained in Choshen Mishpat siman 290; see there. This seems clear.

<b>מותר </b> וכ' מ"א בשם ב"ח דוקא אם שפחה קנא' בלי ידיעתו אז מותר הבעל הבית ליהנות ממנו אבל אותו האיש שקנאה אסור להנות ממנו ע"כ. וקצת מאחרונים מקילין בזה וכן נראה דעת הש"ע כאן וגיסי הרב בספרו א"ז כ' דאחר שש עד הלילה אכן חליפי חליפין יש להתיר אף לדידיה ול"נ דאף המחמיר לדידיה בחליפין תוך הפסח היינו דוקא כסות פירות וכלים שהוא דבר הניכר ונראה כ"כ תוס' פ"ק דחולין דף ד' ע"ב ומבואר שם בפסקי תוס' שתירוץ זה עיקר להלכה אבל דמיו מותרין אפי' לכתחלה וכן משמעות התו' ריש פ' כל שעה ועיין בתשובת שער אפרים סי' ד' וכל זה דוקא חליפי החמץ מותרין אבל החמץ עצמו אף שנחלף וחזר וניתן לישראל מ"מ באיסור הראשון קיימא כמשמעות הש"ס ופוסקים דחמץ של ישראל שעבר עליו פסח דאסור בהנאה דלא כמשמע שם בתשובת שער אפרים להתיר אף החמץ עצמו כה"ג בהנאה ובקונטרס אחרון הרגיש ג"כ גיסי הרב עליו בזה שהוא פשוט לאיסור:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Permitted.</b> The Magen Avraham wrote in the name of the Bach that this is specifically if a maidservant bought it without his knowledge; then the householder may benefit from it, but the person who bought it is forbidden to benefit from it, end quote. Some later authorities are lenient in this, and so appears to be the view of the Shulchan Aruch here. My brother-in-law the Rav wrote in his work Even HaOzer that after six hours until night, an exchange of an exchange should indeed be permitted even for him. It seems to me that even one who is stringent for him regarding exchanges during Pesach refers specifically to clothing, fruit, and utensils, which are recognizable items; so it appears from Tosafos in the first chapter of Chullin, folio 4b, and it is explained there in the rulings of Tosafos that this answer is the main one for halachah. But its money is permitted even initially; so is implied by Tosafos at the beginning of Kol Sha'ah. See Responsa Sha'ar Ephraim, siman 4. All this is specifically that the exchange of the chametz is permitted, but the chametz itself, even if it was exchanged and then given back to the Jew, nevertheless remains under its original prohibition, as implied by the Shas and poskim regarding chametz of a Jew over which Pesach passed, which is forbidden in benefit. This is unlike what appears there in Responsa Sha'ar Ephraim, to permit even the chametz itself in benefit in such a case. In his final kuntres, my brother-in-law the Rav also objected to him in this, for it is plainly forbidden.

<b>שחמץ אינו תופס דמיו. </b> וחליפיו מותרין ונראה לי דה"ה בדגן שנתחמץ ועבר עליו פסח ונזרע דגדוליו מותרין דחליפין כגדולין ועיין בנדרים דף מ"ז וצ"ב נ"ז בסוגיא דש"ס ובפ"ק דשבת אע"ג דגדולי תרומה אסורין משום דדלמא משהי ליה ואתא לידי תקלה אבל בחמץ שעבר עליו פסח דרבנן לא גזרו האי בדבר שזרעו כלה וכ"כ בספרים ותוס' פ"ק דפסחים דגדולי איסורי הנאה מותר:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>For chametz does not seize its proceeds.</b> Its exchanges are permitted. It seems to me that the same applies to grain that became chametz, Pesach passed over it, and it was planted: its growths are permitted, because exchanges are like growths. See Nedarim folios 47 and 52 and 57 in the sugya of the Shas, and the first chapter of Shabbos. Although growths of terumah are forbidden because perhaps he will keep it and come to a stumbling block, regarding chametz over which Pesach passed, whose prohibition is rabbinic, they did not decree this in a case where what he planted was entirely consumed. So too is written in Sefarim, and Tosafos in the first chapter of Pesachim writes that growths of benefit-prohibitions are permitted.
