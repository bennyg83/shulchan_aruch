**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Complete writing.</b> This means that every letter must be surrounded by parchment. So wrote the Tur. My teacher and father-in-law, of blessed memory, wrote: Regarding halacha, it appears that in tefillin, if one letter touches another, even in other words, whether above or below, it is invalid and has no remedy; but in a sefer Torah it has a remedy, to scrape it, etc. It is obvious that some mistaken student wrote this, for below in this siman, seif 25, it is stated explicitly that because of this it is not called out of order, and one may scrape where the letters adhere. He himself also wrote so below. Therefore one should not take account of this, for certainly it did not come from his mouth.

<b>חלוקה לשתים. </b> לכאורה משמע דאם יש נקב בתוך גג האות או ירך שלו אע"פ שנראה חלל הנקב מכל מקום כשר כיון דהנקב באמצע ודיו מקיפו דאז אינו חלוק לשתים אבל באמת אינו כן שהרי כ' ב"י בשם הריא"ס פירוש נקב קטן כחוד' של מחט עד שאם מעביר עליו הקולמס בדיו נסתם שאין האות נחלק לשתים אינו נקרא נקב וכותבין ע"ג וכו' עכ"ל הרי שקורא נחלק האות לשתים  עד שלא נכתב עדיין ותו דבעי דוקא נסתם אלא ע"כ דאף שהדיו מקיפו מכל מקום מקרי חלוק לשתים שם אותו מקום הנקב אלא בעי שיהיה שם נקב קטן כל כך שהדיו סותמו ואינו נראה כלל נגד השמש והיינו בשעת כתיבה  אבל אם אחר שנכתב ניקב בתוך האות כשר כמ"ש הטור וש"ע  בסמוך לענין ניקב תוכו:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Divided in two.</b> At first glance it implies that if there is a hole within the roof of a letter or its leg, although the empty space of the hole is visible, nevertheless it is valid, since the hole is in the middle and ink surrounds it, so that it is not divided in two. But in truth this is not so, for the Beit Yosef wrote in the name of Rabbi Yitzchak Aksandari: The meaning of a small hole is one as fine as the point of a needle, such that if one passes the inked quill over it, it becomes closed; since the letter is not divided in two, it is not called a hole, and one may write upon it, etc. End quote. Thus he calls the letter divided in two even before it was written. Further, he specifically requires that it become closed. Rather, one must say that even if the ink surrounds it, nevertheless the place of the hole is called divided in two; therefore the hole must be so small that the ink closes it and it is not visible at all against the sun. This is at the time of writing. But if after it was written a hole was made inside the letter, it is valid, as the Tur and Shulchan Aruch wrote nearby regarding the inside being pierced.

<b>אפי' ניקב כל תוכו כו'. </b> בפ' הקומץ ניקב תוכו של ה"א כשר יריכו פסול. פירש"י תוכו רגל פנימי יריכו רגל ימיני. ל"א תוכו גויל החלק שבתוכו ע"כ והרי לכל א' מהלשונו' קולא וחומרא דללשון א' יש קולא ברגל פנימי של ה"א שאפי' נשתייר כ"ש כשר ויש חומרא דלא מיירי מן הגויל שבתוכו כלל ממילא סמכינן אירושלמי שמביא הטור שגם מבפנים צריך להיות מוקף גויל. וללשון השני יש חומרא ברגל הפנימי דהיינו שהוא שוה לרגל ימיני ופסול אם לא נשתייר כמלא אות קטנה ויש קולא דתלמוד שלנו סותר הירושלמי וס"ל מבפני' אין צריך להיו' מוקף גויל והרא"ש הסכים לפירוש קמא כמ"ש כאן בש"ע וש"פ שמביא רמ"א ס"ל כפי' בתרא ממילא לפסק רמ"א לא משגחינן בירוש' דמצריך היקף גויל מבפני' ואפשר דדעת רמ"א לפסוק כחומרא של שני הפי' שזכרנו וכן נכון אלא דהטור מסיים  הלכך אם ניקב כל תוכו פסול משמע דוקא כל תוכו ולא קצת ממנו והטעם נראה דכל הגויל שבתוך האות הוי כחלק א' וע"כ אע"פ שנפסק הגויל בתוך האות מצד א' מגין עליו מה שנשאר מצד השני ודומ' אם נפסק  חלק מצד א' בחוץ והנשאר קיים באותה צד גופה דלא מצינו בזה פסול:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Even if its entire inside was pierced.</b> In chapter HaKometz: If the inside of a hei was pierced, it is valid; if its leg was pierced, it is invalid. Rashi explained: “its inside” means the inner leg, and “its leg” means the right leg. Another version: “its inside” means the parchment space within it. Thus each explanation has both a leniency and a stringency. According to the first explanation, there is a leniency regarding the inner leg of the hei, that even if the smallest amount remains it is valid; and there is a stringency, that it does not address the inner parchment space at all, so by implication we rely on the Yerushalmi cited by the Tur, that even inside it must be surrounded by parchment. According to the second explanation, there is a stringency regarding the inner leg, namely that it is like the right leg and is invalid if there did not remain the size of a small letter; and there is a leniency, that our Talmud contradicts the Yerushalmi and holds that inside it need not be surrounded by parchment. The Rosh agreed with the first explanation, as written here in the Shulchan Aruch. The other decisors cited by the Rama hold like the latter explanation, and therefore according to the Rama’s ruling we do not take account of the Yerushalmi that requires surrounding parchment inside. It is possible that the Rama’s view is to rule according to the stringencies of both explanations that we mentioned, and this is proper. However, the Tur concludes: Therefore, if its entire inside was pierced, it is invalid. This implies specifically its entire inside, but not part of it. The reason appears to be that all the parchment within the letter is considered one unit. Therefore, although the parchment inside the letter was severed on one side, what remains on the other side protects it. This is similar to where part was severed on one side outside, but what remains on that same side is intact, where we have not found any invalidation.

<b>או שנפסק רגל הא'. </b> דבר זה א"א להולמו ואציע לפניך שיטת התלמוד והפוסקים וז"ל אגרא איפסק ליה כרעא דה"א דהעם בנוקבא [פי' שבא נקב בה"א של העם בתפילין שלו] א"ר אבא אם נשתייר כמלא אות קטנה כשר ואם לאו פסול. רמי איפסק לי' כרעא דוי"ו דויהרוג אמר ר' זירא זיל אייתי תינוק דלא חכי' ולא טפש אי קרי ליה ויהרוג כשר ואם לאו פסול ופי' המרדכי דבנפסק כרעא דוי"ו דויהרוג אין מועיל מה שנשאר כמלא אות קטנה דאדרבה אי הגיע לאורך וי"ו או אם כשיעור יו"ד ולכך תועיל קריאת התינוק דגילוי מלתא בעלמא הוא דאורך וי"ו יש לה מדלא קראה יו"ד אבל היכא דפשיטא שאין האות נשאר בצורתה מה תועיל בראיי' התינוק ועינינו רואות שאין האות רמ"א גם אין להגיה ה"א בדברי רמ"א במקום הא' דעל אות ה"א אמר אם נפסק רגל שלה דגם באות ה"א אין מועיל תינוק כדכתב בהדיא המרדכי הביאו ב"י דשם הוי השיעור כשיעור אות קטנה דוקא ע"כ נראה דט"ס יש כאן והך או שנפסק רגל הא' שזכר רמ"א מקומו הוא אח"כ וכן צריך להיות מיהו אם אנו רואין שלא נשאר צורת האות כתיקונו או נפסק רגל הא' פסול כו' כנ"ל בלי ספק ורוח אחרת:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Or the leg of the alef was severed.</b> This matter cannot be understood as written, and I will set out before you the approach of the Talmud and the decisors. This is the wording: Agra had the leg of the hei in the word “ha’am” severed by a hole, meaning that a hole came in the hei of “ha’am” in his tefillin. Rabbi Abba said: If there remains the size of a small letter, it is valid; if not, it is invalid. Rami had the leg of the vav of “vayaharog” severed. Rabbi Zeira said: Go bring a child who is neither wise nor foolish; if he reads it as “vayaharog,” it is valid, and if not, it is invalid. The Mordechai explained that where the leg of the vav of “vayaharog” was severed, it does not help that there remains the size of a small letter. On the contrary, if only the measure of a yud remained, it would certainly be invalid, for we certainly require a vav. The same applies to all simple letters, such as zayins and final nuns. But in the case where the leg of the hei was severed, it is valid if there remains the measure of a yud. Maharik, root 69, wrote regarding alefs whose yud does not touch the body of the letter, and regarding the point of the peh that does not touch its roof, that it is invalid, and this is his wording: It is obvious that one cannot validate it through the reading of a child, for these matters were stated only where the letter was severed and its measure was diminished, such as a shortened vav, where we are uncertain whether it reached the length of a vav or only the measure of a yud. Therefore the reading of a child helps, for it is merely revealing the matter that it has the length of a vav, since he did not read it as a yud. But where it is obvious that the letter has not remained in its form, what can the child’s seeing help, when our eyes see that the letter is not in its form? End quote. The Rivash, siman 120, wrote similarly, that a child’s reading helps only where the invalidation is because this letter resembles another letter. But where the invalidation is because its form has been broken, it does not help. This is his wording: Similarly, one cannot validate an alef whose left leg does not touch the middle line because the child reads it as an alef, for due to the separation he will not err to say that it is another letter, namely an inverted ayin, etc. End quote. If so, the Rama’s statement that where the leg of the alef is severed the child’s reading helps requires study against the decisors. One should not force an answer and say that once it had already been written validly and then was severed, a child’s reading helps in all matters. This is not so, for the reason I mentioned in the name of the decisors, and further, Maharik deals with a case that was written invalidly from the outset, and nevertheless he does not distinguish between his law and the severed leg of the vav in the Talmud, which deals with a later severance. Rather, certainly there is no distinction in this. This also appears from seif 25 below, where the Shulchan Aruch wrote that if the leg of the alef does not touch, he may repair it and this is not considered out of order, implying that without repair it is certainly invalid. Maharik wrote this reasoning there as well. The Levush also did not mention this phrase, “or the leg of the alef was severed,” which the Rama mentioned. Nor should one emend the Rama’s words to read hei instead of alef, for regarding the letter hei he said that if its leg was severed, a child helps; but even with the letter hei, a child does not help, as the Mordechai wrote explicitly, cited by the Beit Yosef, for there the measure is specifically the size of a small letter. Therefore it appears that there is a textual error here, and the phrase “or the leg of the alef was severed” mentioned by the Rama belongs afterward. It should read: However, if we see that the form of the letter has not remained properly, or the leg of the alef was severed, it is invalid, etc. This appears to me without doubt or any other spirit.

<b>If a child, etc.</b> It appears to me that this severance refers to where part of the length of the letter downward was completely cut off, and only the part before the break remains. Then it depends on the child’s reading if we are uncertain whether the measure of that letter remains. But if something also remains below the break, namely a gap was made across the width of the leg of the letter and below the gap part of the leg remains, a child does not help, for the child will combine what is below with the upper part, whereas in truth it cannot be combined. This is similar to the severed leg of the alef that I mentioned in the name of the Rivash, where a child does not help. In this case it appears to me that one must cover the part that remains after the break, so that it is not before the child, except for what is above it. This is not comparable to what the Shulchan Aruch wrote here, that one need not cover the other letters, for in this case he certainly agrees that one must cover it. This appears correct and clear. <b>And I saw</b> that my teacher and father-in-law, of blessed memory, wrote: We validate through a child only if the letter was initially written according to law and afterward became invalid; but if it was initially written this way, a child does not help, even where there is no hole. End quote. His words are surprising, for certainly there is no distinction in this. If it happened that the scribe wrote a simple letter and was uncertain whether it is as long as it should be, and he cannot correct it because it would be out of order, certainly he shows the letter to a child. A proof is from the words of Maharik and Rivash that I brought above regarding the yud of an alef being separated, and they speak of the scribe having made it so from the outset, as is clear in their words there, and nevertheless they discussed whether showing it to a child applies, for other reasons. Rather, there is certainly no distinction in this. What the decisors wrote in this matter, “if it was severed,” follows the language of the Talmud, and there the incident happened that way.

<b>Except for the last parsha, etc.</b> Meaning that in the Torah it is closed, and therefore one must make it closed in tefillin as well. Although the reason it is closed in the Torah is that it relates to what precedes it, unlike in tefillin, nevertheless he should write it as it is in the Torah. The Beit Yosef wrote in the name of the Rambam that if he made a closed parsha open, or the reverse, they are invalid, and that the first three parshiyos in tefillin are all open and the last parsha is closed. He raised a difficulty: in HaKometz 32, regarding the parshiyos of mezuzos, we make them closed, but if he made them open they are valid, because in the Torah they are not adjacent. If so here with tefillin, granted Kadesh and VeHaya Ki Yevi’acha, which are written in the order in which they are written in the Torah, must also here be open. But Shema and VeHaya Im Shamoa are not adjacent in the Torah, so why should it be invalid if he made VeHaya Im Shamoa an open parsha? The Beit Yosef answered that since with some parshiyos of tefillin, if he changed them they are invalid, we do not distinguish among them. This is forced, for from where does the Rambam know this? <b>And the Maharshal</b> in responsum siman 37 explained that when the Rambam says “invalid,” he means that he invalidated them from their essential proper arrangement initially. It appears to me to explain the Rambam’s view, that he proved this from an explicit Talmudic passage. Necessarily there is a difference between tefillin and mezuzos regarding the parsha of VeHaya Im Shamoa, for in tefillin we require it to be closed even after the fact. This is from HaKometz, where Rav Nachman bar Yitzchak said: It is a mitzvah for a mezuzah to be with closed parshiyos, but if one made them open, this is acceptable. The Gemara suggests: Shall we say this supports him, for similarly, worn-out sifrei Torah and tefillin may not be made into a mezuzah, because one may not lower from a severe sanctity to a lighter one. This implies that if one were allowed to lower, one could make them into a mezuzah. But why? Here they are closed, and here, in a mezuzah, they are open. Rather, necessarily, even a mezuzah is with closed parshiyos. The Rambam inferred that this proof, “one may not lower,” must also refer to tefillin, for if it referred only to a sefer Torah, the Gemara could answer that indeed one may not make a mezuzah from a sefer Torah because in a sefer Torah they are closed and in a mezuzah they must be open, while the reason given, that one may not lower, refers to tefillin, that one may not make a mezuzah from them even though it would also be valid with open parshiyos. Therefore it gave one reason for both, namely that one may not lower. Necessarily, then, tefillin too require closed parshiyos as in a sefer Torah and mezuzah. And if you say that with open parshiyos it is valid after the fact, why did the Gemara not say: Shall we say there is a support from tefillin, that the reason is that one may not lower, but if one may lower then one could make them; but why, tefillin are closed and a mezuzah is open? This would have supported that after the fact it is valid when open. Since it did not answer so, but gave a forced answer there, we learn that in tefillin it is invalid if open even after the fact. This appears correct according to the Rambam.

According to the Rosh, as explained in Yoreh Deah, siman 275, whenever there is blank space in the line and one begins writing in the middle of the line, and likewise when one writes and leaves blank space at the end of the line and begins the following parsha at the beginning of the second line, this is open. The measure of the blank space is enough to write nine letters. Closed means wherever he leaves blank space in the middle of the line to that measure and begins to write at the end of that very line; the same applies if he finished the parsha at the end of the line, he leaves one blank line and begins on the second line. The Rambam wrote as follows: An open parsha has two forms. If he finished in the middle of the line, he leaves the rest of the line blank to the above measure and begins the open parsha at the beginning of the second line. This is like the Rosh. If there did not remain blank space to the required measure, or he finished at the end of the line, he leaves the following line blank and begins the open parsha at the beginning of the third line. According to the Rosh this is closed. A closed parsha has three forms. If he finished in the middle, he leaves a space to the required measure and begins one word at the end of the line. This is like the Rosh. If there did not remain enough for nine letters and to write one word at the end, he leaves it all blank, leaves a small space from the beginning of the second line, and begins writing the closed parsha from the middle of the second line. Possibly the Rosh also agrees to this. If he finished at the end of the line, he leaves at the beginning of the second line the required space and begins writing from the middle of the line a closed parsha. According to the Rosh this is open. Thus you find that an open parsha always begins at the beginning of a line, and a closed parsha always begins from the middle of a line. End quote. The Beit Yosef wrote that if the tefillin are made as the Tur wrote, namely that he leaves at the end of the line a blank space equal to three words of three letters, or at their beginning, except that the final parsha should be closed, then we have fulfilled the view of the Rosh, who holds that beginning in the middle of a line is called open, but not the view of the Rambam, who holds that beginning in the middle of a line is called closed. If so, if he leaves space at their beginning, all of them are closed, so how could our teacher the Tur write “or at their beginning”? Necessarily, the only validation is if he leaves blank space at the end of each parsha. But then even the last parsha is open, for whenever there is blank space at the end of a parsha, it makes the following parsha open. Therefore throughout the lands of Yishmael, where they follow the Rambam in all their ways, they do as follows: the first two parshiyos begin at the top of the page and end at the end of the page in the middle of a line; the third parsha, Shema, begins at the top of the page and ends at its end; and the fourth parsha begins in the middle of the line. Thus the first three parshiyos, according to the Rambam, are open, for the first, although there is no blank space before it, is called open like Bereishis, which is plainly open according to the Rambam, as the decisors wrote from his words. The two parshiyos after the first are made open by the measure of blank space before them, namely the space at the end of the preceding parsha, which makes the following parsha open, and it begins at the head of the line; this is so both according to the Rambam and the Rosh. Only the fourth parsha, which begins in the middle of a line, is called open according to the Rosh, and we require it to be closed. But according to the Rambam this is proper, for in this manner it is called closed. In these countries the custom is that all three parshiyos begin at the head of the column and end in the middle of the page, and the fourth parsha also begins at the head of the page and ends at the end of the last line. This is difficult, for it follows no view. According to the Rambam, the fourth parsha is not closed, since it begins at the head of the page. Even according to the Rosh, where we find a closed parsha at the head of a page, namely if he leaves the first line blank and begins from the second line, nevertheless in this manner, where he begins at the head of the page and already left blank space at the end of the third parsha, the fourth parsha is certainly open. One cannot say that the space at the end of the third parsha helps only to make that parsha itself open, but has no effect on the following parsha. Maharam Padua wrote this reasoning in responsum siman 77 in the name of Orchot Chaim, but did not agree to it except to validate tefillin that had already been written and had spread in practice, for the Rambam’s words do not prove this. The Maharshal too, in responsum siman 37, raised many difficulties against this reasoning, and there he nevertheless justified our form on the ground that the Baal HaItur holds that all four parshiyos in tefillin must be open. In this way we also fulfill the view of the Rosh, since after the fact he agrees that even the fourth parsha is valid if open; and even the Rambam, who wrote that it is invalid if the fourth was made open, invalidated it only initially. But according to what I wrote above from the Talmud, that even after the fact the fourth parsha is invalid if open, like the view of the Rambam, it is not proper that we should do this initially and recite a blessing on them every day. The Maharshal also raised a further difficulty, that the words of the Baal HaItur do not appear correct at all in what he wrote, that even the fourth parsha should be open, for this is against the Talmud and the custom we follow with a mezuzah; this view is nullified against all the early and later decisors. <b>Now</b> we have before us another way to satisfy both the Rambam and the Rosh. We must first introduce that which the Rambam wrote in the second form of a closed parsha: if there did not remain in the line enough to leave the required space and write one word at the end of the line, he leaves it all blank, etc. The same applies if this was done at the end of the page, where he leaves a blank space less than the measure of space between parshiyos, namely nine letters and enough to write one word, and begins at the head of the next page, where he also leaves a small blank space less than the above measure. This too is called closed. According to the Rambam, the reason for closed in this case is that since there is not a full measure of blank space in one place at the end of the line, even if you combine it with the additional space before the second parsha, we say it is as though the beginning of the second parsha was written at the end of the line that contains the blank space at the end of the first parsha, and therefore it is fully closed. By contrast, if there is at the end a full measure of space, then the following parsha is a matter unto itself and is open whenever it begins at the head of the line. The same applies at the end of a page together with the space at the head of the following page, for the reason is one. If so, one who wishes to do it in the most correct manner should do as follows: the first and second parshiyos should begin at the head of the page and end at the end with blank space equal to nine letters. But the third parsha should also begin at the head of the page and end with less than the measure, namely less than nine letters, and the fourth parsha should also have at the beginning of the page some blank space, in any case less than the measure. It emerges that the fourth parsha is closed according to everyone: according to the Rambam, this too is called beginning in the middle, and according to the Rosh, there is no open parsha here, since there is no full measure of blank space in one place. But one should not say that he should leave at the end of the third parsha a full measure of space, and nevertheless it is not called a measure regarding the closed parsha that follows because he did not also leave enough blank space to write the first word of the following parsha, as the Rambam wrote explicitly regarding a closed parsha, that there must be space at the end of the parsha to write nine letters and also to write one word. If we did so, we would not fulfill the view of the Rosh, who holds that whenever one leaves the measure of nine letters alone, the following parsha is called open, even though it begins at the very head of the page, and if so the fourth parsha would be open. Nor should one say that he should leave no space at all at the end of the third parsha, and at the beginning of the fourth parsha leave less than the measure at the head of the line, for in any case we require space between one parsha and another equal to nine letters, whether closed or open. Only here it is impossible to make it in one place, for then it would be open; therefore we divide the measure into two parts, as we mentioned, and in this way one satisfies both the Rambam and the Rosh. All the above I have written according to my humble opinion as correct. Although one should not, Heaven forbid, cast doubt on any custom, nevertheless in this matter the Maharshal already wrote that one should not conduct oneself this way from now on. He wrote one form that seemed to him to satisfy also Maseches Soferim and the order of the early authorities brought by the Beit Yosef in Yoreh Deah, siman 275. In my humble opinion, one need be concerned only to satisfy the Rambam and the Rosh, who saw all the baraisos and interpreted them according to their view, and it is enough for us to satisfy all. This is exactly like our custom, except that we needed to distance the beginning of the fourth parsha slightly, unlike the form written by the Maharshal, which is an entirely different arrangement. The form I mentioned is also cited in Responsa Maharam Padua there, and he praised it. He wrote that although our Ashkenazic custom that I mentioned above can be justified, nevertheless from now on they should not continue to do so. The Levush wrote: If my strength were sufficient, I would abolish it and institute writing according to the custom of the land of Yishmael. I say that since there is a mitzvah here to change, why should we do according to the way of the land of Yishmael, through which we do not satisfy the view of the Rosh? Therefore one should make a slight change as I wrote, and thereby satisfy all views, as it appears to me.

<b>Even if there is no space between them.</b> Many were troubled by this matter, as explained in the Beit Yosef. End quote. Rabbeinu Yerucham wrote: If he wrote them on one skin, they are valid even if there is no space between them, provided he placed them in four batim. According to this, one must say that when Rabbi said that he must put space between them, it does not refer to the writing, for even according to the Sages one must put space between parsha and parsha so that he can insert them into four batim. Rather, it means that one must place a large space between one bayis and another, and the Sages say this is not necessary to that extent, but a thread or cord between each one suffices. The Beit Yosef wrote: So ruled the Nimukei Yosef. But what Rashi explained, that there must be space between the parshiyos when he writes them, is difficult, for it implies that this is according to Rabbi, but according to the Sages it is not necessary; and if so, it is impossible to insert them into four batim, etc. End quote. He pressed himself greatly to explain Rashi’s words. See there. <b>After this</b> he brought in the name of Mahari Abuhav and Mahari Chaviv, who asked that if they are on one parchment it is impossible for them to stand upright in the batim, and if they are not upright, they are invalid. The Beit Yosef answered that the requirement that they stand upright applies only initially. It appears to me that this is not difficult, for he can cut between each parsha from above until close to the bottom, leaving only a little attached below, and then each parsha can be stood upright in its own bayis, while all remain one skin. The requirement of space between them is only enough for this cutting and insertion, and where one made them in a way that each stands in its bayis, it is valid even if there was no additional space between the written parshiyos.
