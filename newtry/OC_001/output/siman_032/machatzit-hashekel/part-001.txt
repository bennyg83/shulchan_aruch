<b>(ס"ק ב) מעשן כו'</b> וגם בי"ד כ' שלכתחלה כו' הבא מן העץ ר"ל הרב"י כ' שם בסי' רע"א וז"ל ס"ת צריך שיכתבנו בדיו העשוי מעשן הסמנים שרוי במי עפצים וע"ז כת' רמ"א שם ולכתחלה טוב ליזהר שלא לעשות דיו כ"א בדברים הבאים מן העץ עכ"ל. והלא עשן השמני' ועפצים הם דברי' הבאים מן העץ אע"כ דרמ"א בא למעט שלא להקפיד לעשות מן עשן השמני' ועיקר הקפידא בדברים הבאים מעץ וזה סותר למ"ש רמ"א כאן ועפ"ב דשבת דמשמ' שהיו עושים דיו מעשן שמנים. ז"ל הש"ס שם דף כ"ג ע"א דתני ר"ש בר זוטרי כל השמנים יפים לדיו ושמן זית מן המובחר. רבי שמואל בר זוטרי מתני הכי כל העשני' יפים לדיו ועשן שמן זית מן המובחר ע"כ מ"מ נראה דשמנים ועשנים שוין הם לדיו:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>(Seif katan 2) From soot, etc.</b> And in Yoreh De'ah he also wrote that initially, etc., from something that comes from a tree. Meaning, Beis Yosef wrote there in siman 271: A Torah scroll must be written with ink made from the soot of substances soaked in gallnut water. On this the Rama wrote there: Initially, it is proper to be careful not to make ink except from things that come from a tree. But surely soot from oils and gallnuts are things that come from a tree. It must be that the Rama meant to exclude the notion that one must specifically use soot from oils; the main concern is only that the ingredients come from a tree. This contradicts what the Rama wrote here. And in chapter 2 of Shabbos it implies that they made ink from the soot of oils. The Talmud there, 23a, states: Rabbi Shimon bar Zutri taught: All oils are suitable for ink, and olive oil is the choicest. Rabbi Shmuel bar Zutri taught it this way: All soots are suitable for ink, and the soot of olive oil is the choicest. In any case, it appears that oils and their soot are equivalent regarding ink.

Magen Avraham added that even regarding the first law, which he brought in the name of Mahardach, one may say as Olas Tamid wrote above, that this is explained in the Yerushalmi, which distinguishes between attachment above and attachment below. Olas Tamid understood that it referred to the words of Mahardach. See Beis Yosef, s.v. 'Semak wrote, if it fell,' where he brings the Yerushalmi and the responsum of Rashba to explain the Yerushalmi another way. He also wrote that we rule like that Yerushalmi. Also below, in seif 18, it is explained that attached letters become valid only by scraping between them and separating them; as long as the adhesion was not scraped, they are invalid, and we do not say that whatever is fit for mixing is not impeded by lack of actual mixing. This is also explained there in Magen Avraham concerning a final kaf that reaches the end of the parchment, which in seif 16 is written to be invalid; nevertheless Magen Avraham wrote in seif katan 27 that one may scrape a little at the end of the final kaf until it is surrounded by blank parchment. From all this it is clear that the law does not follow Mahardach.

<b>(ס"ק ד) בשמאל פסול היינו בשולט כו'</b> שלא תטעה כיון שהשוה הרב"י השולט בימין עם השולט בשני ידיו שצריכים לכתוב בימין לכתחלה א"כ מ"ש אח"ז ואם כ' בשמאל פסול קאי ג"כ על שניהם אפי' על שולט בשני ידיו לזה כ' מ"א דליתא דלענין דיעבד אינו שוה לשולט בימין לבד ודע דמ"ש הרב"י פסול אם אפש' למצוא אחרים משמע דאם א"א למצו' אחרי' יניח תפילין הנכתבים בשמאל מ"מ כתב ב"ש באה"ע סי' קכ"ג סק"ד דמ"מ אין לברך עליהן דהא ברוב פוסקים מבואר להדי' דפסולים אלא שהרא"ש וסמ"ג לא כתבו להדיא שפסולים לכן באין לו אחרים יניח אלו אולי יצא לדעת הרא"ש וסמ"ג אבל לברך אינו רשאי דספק ברכות להקל וביותר דברא"ש וסמ"ג אינו מבואר להדי' דכשרים דלא כלבוש שכ' לברך עליהן עכת"ד ע"ש:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>(Seif katan 4) With the left hand it is invalid, that is, where he is dominant, etc.</b> Do not err and say that since Beis Yosef equated one who is right-handed with one who controls both hands, that both must initially write with the right hand, therefore when he wrote afterward that if one wrote with the left hand it is invalid, this also refers to both of them, even to one who controls both hands. For this reason Magen Avraham wrote that this is not so, since after the fact he is not the same as one who is right-handed alone. Know that Beis Yosef's statement that it is invalid if others can be found implies that if others cannot be found, he should put on tefillin written with the left hand. Nevertheless, Beis Shmuel in Even HaEzer siman 123 seif katan 4 wrote that one still should not recite a blessing over them, for most decisors explicitly state that they are invalid. Only the Rosh and Semag did not state explicitly that they are invalid. Therefore, when he has no others, he should put these on, perhaps fulfilling the mitzvah according to the Rosh and Semag; but he may not bless, for in cases of doubtful blessings we are lenient. All the more so since the Rosh and Semag do not state explicitly that they are valid, unlike the Levush, who wrote to bless over them. These are his words; see there.

<b>(ס"ק ז) שטה כו'</b> דאסור דקי"ל דוקא מזוזה (וכן ס"ת לפרש"י והכי קי"ל בי"ד) צריכי שרטוט אבל תפילין א"צ שרטוט היינו דוקא שא"צ לשרטט כל שטה ושטה כמו במזוזה (וס"ת לדידן) מגזר' הכתוב אבל עכ"פ שטה העליונה צריך לשרטט דאסור לכתוב פסוק מן התורה בלא שרטוט כדי ליישר הכתיבה ולזה סגי בשטה עליונה כיון דכבר כתוב לפניו שטה עליונה ביושר יודע ליישר תחתיה שטה שני' אפי' בלי שרטוט דשור' עליונה הוי כשרטוט לשור' שני' ושני' לשלישית וכן כלם:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>(Seif katan 7) A line, etc.</b> That it is forbidden. For we hold that only a mezuzah, and likewise a Torah scroll according to Rashi, and so we rule in Yoreh De'ah, requires scoring. But tefillin do not require scoring. This means only that one need not score every line as in a mezuzah, and a Torah scroll according to our practice, because of the scriptural decree. Nevertheless, at least the upper line must be scored, because it is forbidden to write a verse from the Torah without scoring in order to straighten the writing. For this, the upper line suffices, since once the first line before him is written straight, he knows how to align the second line beneath it even without scoring, for the upper line serves like scoring for the second, the second for the third, and so for all of them.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And Be'er Heitev asked, etc.</b> At the end of chapter 12 of Yevamos, Abaye said that when beis din writes a chalitzah document for the yevamah who performed chalitzah before them, they should write in it: They had him read from 'my yavam refuses' until 'my yavam refused,' meaning they should write the whole verse, from the beginning and end. They also had him read until 'to take her,' for the yavam says the verse, 'I do not desire to take her.' They had her read from 'so' until 'the removed shoe,' but not the entire verse. The Gemara concludes that Mar Zutra scored, etc., for the entire section. Be'er Heitev understood that he scored all the lines and wrote all the above verses as they appear in the Torah because it is forbidden to write without scoring, and on this he objected. However, the wording of Magen Avraham's question in the name of Be'er Heitev is not precise, for it implies that Be'er Heitev objected only to his scoring the whole section, when it would have sufficed to score the upper line alone, and to that Magen Avraham's answer fits. In truth, Be'er Heitev asked a stronger question, and therefore Magen Avraham's answer is brief and somewhat obscure. He quotes the Rosh in Megillah chapter 2 and Tur Yoreh De'ah, that where the upper line was written without scoring, such as where one wrote ordinary words or a letter of greeting in the upper line, from there onward the upper line is like scoring. This is like what is said here, that if he scored the upper line he no longer needs to score. It is not reasonable to distinguish and say that here is different because the first line was scored and therefore written straight, for ordinary people know how to train their hands to write the entire line straight without scoring. On this Be'er Heitev wrote that it requires study: why did Mar Zutra need to score the chalitzah document, since before reaching the reading text they had already written several lines mentioned in the chapter HaCholeitz and copied by the Rosh at the end of the chapter Mitzvas Chalitzah? Thus he asked that there should have been no need to score at all, not even the upper line, since several lines were already written in the chalitzah document before they wrote that they had read the verses. Accordingly, Magen Avraham's second answer, that Mar Zutra in fact scored only the upper line, does not fully resolve Be'er Heitev's question, since he asked that even the upper line should not have required scoring. It must be that Magen Avraham meant that the reason is explained in the Rosh: even if the upper line is not scored, one need not score the second line because ordinary people know how to train their hands to write all the lines straight without scoring. Mar Zutra, however, did not know how to write the upper line straight without scoring; but if the first line was scored, he could align the lines below it. Therefore he scored only the first line and afterward wrote the document. According to the first answer, however, even if the first line was scored Mar Zutra still could not align the lines below it, and therefore he had to score the entire section that he wrote. I did not find in the Rosh the phrase 'ordinary people, etc.' except regarding the upper line, as I cited above; perhaps Magen Avraham inferred by reasoning that this is also the reason for the lines below the upper one. This excludes the view that the whole section may not be written, namely Abaye as above. Afterward it says there that Mar bar Idi challenged: But it was not given to be written, which Rashi explains to mean that one may not write a single section alone. For this reason the Gemara says that Mar Zutra wrote the entire section, excluding the view of Mar bar Idi.

We rule that between one parashah and another, whether open or closed, one must leave a blank space large enough to write nine letters. However, the Tur here wrote a space large enough for three words of three letters each, and Rambam, chapter 7 of Hilchos Tefillin, wrote nine letters, like “asher asher asher.” The Bach in this siman, s.k. 4, wrote as follows: one should not ask why our teacher wrote that one should leave a blank space of three words, etc., and did not write briefly that one should leave a space of nine letters. Also, it is explained in seif 32 that between one letter and another one must leave a tiny space like a hairbreadth. If so, besides the nine letters one must also leave the spaces between the letters; but those two letters that the Bach added according to his explanation do not need space. If it had taught a measure of eleven letters, I might have thought that one also needs the measure of ten hairbreadth spaces between the letters, but this is not so. All this follows the Bach. But in Shulchan Aruch here, and likewise in Shulchan Aruch Yoreh Deah and in the Tur Yoreh Deah, three words are not mentioned, only nine letters, implying that he holds that nine letters alone suffice, unlike the Bach.

The matter of the form of an open or closed parashah is a dispute between Rambam and the Rosh. According to Rambam, every open parashah must begin at the beginning of the line. Therefore, if he finished the parashah in the middle of a line and there still remains empty space until the end of the line the measure of nine letters, he leaves it blank and begins the next parashah at the start of the line below it. That parashah, which begins at the start of the line, is called open, and in this the Rosh also agrees that it is called open. But if there is blank space from the end of the parashah he finished until the end of the line more than nine letters, enough for one word, and he left a blank space of nine letters and afterward wrote the first word of the following parashah, and the same applies to more than one word depending on the available space, this is called closed according to Rambam, since he did not begin at the start of the line; and in this the Rosh also agrees that it is called closed. However, there are two other forms in which Rambam and the Rosh disagree, and their positions are opposite. One is where he finished at the end of a line, left the entire line below it blank, and on the line below that blank line began another parashah. According to Rambam this is called open, since it begins at the start of a line; but according to the Rosh it is called closed, for we view all three lines, the line where the first parashah ended, the blank line, and the line below it where the parashah begins, as if they are one line, and the blank space is in the middle of the line, which is called closed, as above. The second form is where he finished the parashah at the end of a line, and on the line below it he left at its beginning a blank space the measure of nine letters, and afterward began another parashah. According to Rambam this is called closed, since it does not begin at the start of the line; but according to the Rosh, in such a case it is called open. Beis Yosef wrote in Yoreh Deah, siman 275, that initially one who fears Heaven should satisfy everyone and write in a form that is open according to all where it must be open, and likewise where it must be closed he should make a form that according to all is closed. Rama wrote: however, if he cannot align it in this way, he should not depart from the words of Rambam, whose words are primary in this matter. In tefillin one must make the parshiyos as they are in the Torah: the first three parshiyos are open in the Torah, and the fourth parashah, “Vehayah im shamoa,” is closed in the Torah. Therefore, the parashah of “Kadesh,” which is first, must be open. Although it is not recognizable at its beginning whether it is open or closed, since there is no blank space before it at all, nevertheless this is sufficient for it to be called open, like the beginnings of the five books of the Torah, which Rambam did not count among the open and closed parshiyos, for Bereishis and the other four books have a sufficient indication that they are the beginning of a book; and the same applies to the beginning of “Kadesh,” which is the beginning of the tefillin. However, to indicate that the parashah of “Vehayah ki yeviacha” is open, one must leave at the end of “Kadesh” a blank space of nine letters. When he then begins “Vehayah ki yeviacha” at the top of the parchment, with no blank space before it, it is as if he began on the line below the end of “Kadesh,” which is open according to all, if he began at the start of the line as above. The same should be done at the end of “Vehayah ki yeviacha,” so that there will be an indication that the parashah of “Shema” is open according to all. But to make “Vehayah im shamoa” in a form that is closed according to all is impossible, for then on the last line of “Shema” he would have to leave a blank space of nine letters and begin “Vehayah im shamoa” on that same line, as above; and this is impossible, not only with the head-tefillin, where each parashah is on its own parchment, but even with the hand-tefillin, since in any event each parashah must begin at the top of the parchment. Since Rambam’s words are primary in this matter, Beis Yosef wrote that he should not leave at the end of “Shema” a blank space of eight letters, but at the top of the parchment where he begins “Vehayah im shamoa” he should leave a blank space at the beginning of the line of nine letters, and it is closed according to Rambam but open according to the Rosh, as above. However, regarding this, Beis Yosef in the name of Rambam disagrees with those who validate. Rambam’s view is that if he made “Vehayah im shamoa” open, it is invalid, since in the Torah it is closed. Although in the Torah the reason it is closed is because it relates to the parashah before it, whereas in tefillin “Vehayah im shamoa” does not relate to the preceding parashah of “Shema,” since “Shema” is written in Parashas Vaeschanan and “Vehayah im shamoa” is written in Parashas Eikev, and if so, although initially it is proper to write it in the form in which it is written in the Torah, why should it be invalid if he did not do so? Beis Yosef answered that Rambam holds that just as with the three preceding parshiyos, if he changed from the way they are written in the Torah they are invalid, the same law applies to “Vehayah im shamoa.” Those who validate do not accept this reasoning, and therefore they validate it at least after the fact if he made “Vehayah im shamoa” open.

<b>Semak wrote, etc.</b> Not like Beis Yosef, etc., who understood it as referring to writing out of order. For in Semak it is written that regarding head-tefillin, since they are on four parchments, one need not be particular about the order in which they are written. **** ENGLISH ****
<b>(s.k. 51) Where possible, etc.</b> Without a blessing. This is because the Rosh holds that the leather of the housings also requires processing for the sake of the mitzvah. But Rambam holds that the leather of the housings does not require processing at all. If so, the same law applies when they were processed not for their sake, for that is no worse than if they had not been processed at all. Therefore, where it is not possible to obtain housings processed for their sake, he should put on housings that were processed not for their sake, or not processed at all, for in this matter one may rely on Rambam’s view so as not to be nullified from the mitzvah of tefillin. Nevertheless he should not recite a blessing, since blessings do not prevent fulfillment. And since the primary ruling follows the Rosh, who requires processing for its sake, one should not bless over them. Rashi also holds this way. For in Sanhedrin 46, Abaye and Rava disagree: Abaye holds that designation is significant, and Rava holds that designation is not significant. The Gemara concludes that this is a dispute of Tannaim: the first Tanna holds that tefillin housings do not require processing for their sake, while Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel holds that they do require processing for their sake. Rashi explained that processing is not called an act, and is only like designation. The first Tanna holds that designation is not significant; therefore he holds that processing for its sake is unnecessary, for the processing neither raises nor lowers the status, since it is only designation, and designation is not significant. Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel holds that designation is significant; therefore he requires processing for its sake, for although the processing is only designation, nevertheless the processing is effective because he holds designation is significant. Tosafos asked: if so, since we rule like Rava against Abaye, that designation is not significant, then according to Rashi’s explanation processing for its sake should not be required. Yet in Gittin, a Sefer Torah is invalid if the parchment was not processed for its sake; and it is difficult to distinguish between the leather of the housings and parchment, since on the leather of the housings the shin is written, and it is a halachah transmitted to Moshe from Sinai, so it is fitting that they have the law of parchment. Therefore Tosafos explained the opposite: the first Tanna holds that processing for its sake is unnecessary because he holds designation is significant, and therefore processing for its sake is not needed, for designation suffices. But Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel holds that designation is not significant, and designation is ineffective; therefore one must perform an act for its sake, namely the processing. The Rosh likewise holds this way. If so, since we rule like Rava, that designation is not significant, even the leather of the housings requires processing for its sake. Rambam explains like Rashi. To resolve Tosafos’ difficulty, one must necessarily say as Tosafos wrote, that there is a distinction between the leather of the housings, which does not require processing for its sake, and the parchment, which does require processing for its sake. Although Tosafos wrote that this is forced, Rashi and Rambam hold that it is not forced. Therefore Rambam holds that the leather of the housings does not require processing for its sake, since we rule that designation is not significant, like Rava, for according to him even if it was not processed at all it is valid.
