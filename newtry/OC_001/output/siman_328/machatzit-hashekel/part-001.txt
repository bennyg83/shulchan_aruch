**** ENGLISH ****
<b>(s.k. 3) And is in pain, etc.</b> It should read: that part, etc.; and extracting a tooth according to one opinion, etc. In this he gives the reason that it necessarily discusses where his whole body became ill from it. For if extracting a tooth were only a D'Rabbanan prohibition, one could say that it discusses even where his whole body did not become ill and it is only a partial illness, and even so it was permitted, because for a partial illness we permit a double shevus, meaning telling a non-Jew to perform a shevus, as the Magen Avraham wrote below in s.k. 12 and above in siman 307. Therefore he wrote that it is a melacha according to the Torah, and telling a non-Jew to perform a melacha according to the Torah was permitted only when his whole body became ill, as below in seif 17. The Taz in s.k. 1 disagrees and writes: Granted, Ameimar in tractate Beitzah, folio 22, permitted painting the eye, which is a shevus, through a non-Jew, and Rav Ashi said to him, does the master not assist by closing and opening the eye, and he answered that assisting has no substance. Nevertheless, we say at the end of Makkos that both the one whose hair is rounded and the one who rounds it are liable. The Gemara asks, but the one whose hair is rounded does nothing, and it is a prohibition without an act; Rava answers that it is where he assists by positioning the hairs. Thus assisting does have substance. Only in the case of Ameimar, where initially every person's eyes are open and he does not assist at the beginning but only afterward when the eye is being painted, in such a case assisting has no substance. But in the case of one whose hair is rounded, before the one rounding begins, the one being rounded must position the hairs, and it is as though the one being rounded begins the melacha; assisting in such a case has substance. If so, the same applies to extracting a tooth, for a person's mouth is normally closed, and when the non-Jew wants to extract the tooth the Jew must open his mouth, which is like the one whose hair is rounded, where the assistance has substance. He also brought the words of Maharshal that even to extract it because of danger is forbidden, for the extraction itself is also dangerous. These are his words. The Shach in Yoreh De'ah siman 138 rejected the words of the Taz, saying that there is no distinction between whether the Jew begins the assistance or not; in every case assisting has no substance. Rather, the case of the one whose hair is rounded is different, for the Torah explicitly included him for liability, as Rashi wrote there, since it is written, do not round, in the plural, to include also the one whose hair is rounded. The Gemara asks only that even so he should not receive lashes, since it is a prohibition without an act; to this it answers that it is because he assists, meaning that through this it is considered a prohibition that has an act. But in general, where the Torah did not include the assistant for liability, we do not make him liable, and we say that because of this slight assistance he is not fit to be made liable. See there, and see the responsum of Chacham Tzvi, siman 22, regarding this.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>(s.k. 6) A doctor, etc.</b> If the doctor says, etc., that it will harm him, we listen to the doctor, Radbaz. Although in siman 618, s.k. 3, he wrote in the name of Radbaz as follows: If the sick person says, I need it, even if the doctors say that the food will harm him, we listen to the sick person. That case is different, for there the sick person says that he needs it not as a medicine but as food, because weakness has overcome him, and if he does not eat he will be endangered because of weakness. Even if the doctor says that he will not become endangered from weakness if he does not eat, and on the contrary, if he eats the food will harm him, in this we listen to the sick person. For if the doctor had not said that the food would harm him, but only said that even if he does not eat he will not be endangered because of weakness, we would feed him based on the words of the sick person, for the heart knows the bitterness of its soul, and we believe the sick person more than the doctor. If so, even when the doctor says that the food will harm him, nevertheless we feed him, since we believe the sick person's statement that if he does not eat he will be endangered because of weakness. Why should we be concerned for the future danger caused by eating according to the doctor's words and not be concerned for the danger that is before us because of the weakness? This is unlike here, where the sick person says he needs a certain medicine that will heal him and the doctor says it will harm him. The words of the sick person and the words of the doctor are both about the future, and in this we listen to the doctor. We do not say that the heart knows the bitterness of its soul, because this depends on medical wisdom, and the doctor's words are also in the form of refraining from action, and both matters concern the future.

The Beit Yosef wrote that this proves that according to the Rambam one should be more lenient with an illness that includes the whole body than with danger to a limb, which is the opposite of what the Tur wrote in the name of the Rambam. But the Beit Yosef himself also challenged the approach of the Maggid Mishneh and explained that according to the Rambam the distinction is between something attached to a melacha according to the Torah, and this is the third opinion brought in Shulchan Aruch. Therefore the Magen Avraham wrote that this was not the intent of the Maggid Mishneh, and the Maggid Mishneh also holds that regarding danger to a limb one should be more lenient than regarding an illness that includes the whole body. What the Maggid Mishneh wrote, but with one limb, since there is no danger, means that there is also no danger to the limb. But if there is danger to a limb, a Jew is permitted to perform a shevus. The proof is that the Maggid Mishneh wrote afterward that for danger to a limb a full melacha was not permitted, which implies that a shevus is permitted. And if there is no danger to a limb and it is also not an illness affecting the whole body, this is the case of Ameimar, who permitted painting the eye through a non-Jew, for there was no danger to a limb and also no illness affecting the whole body. Nevertheless, since painting is only a shevus, through a non-Jew it is a double shevus and is permitted even for partial illness.
