**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And so it is explicit in Megillah 22a.</b> The Gemara discusses Rosh Chodesh, when four people must read. There are three sections: Vayedaber has eight verses, Uveyom HaShabbos has two, and Uverashei Chodsheichem has five. If the kohen and levi each read three verses from Vayedaber, two verses remain, and one may not leave fewer than three verses in a section. If they each read four, the third reader begins Uveyom HaShabbos, which has only two verses, and must add one or two verses from Uverashei Chodsheichem while leaving three for the fourth reader. But one may not begin a section with fewer than three verses. The Gemara therefore concludes that the kohen and levi together read five verses. The third reads the three remaining verses of Vayedaber and also Uveyom HaShabbos, and the fourth reads Uverashei Chodsheichem. How are the five verses divided between the kohen and levi, since each must read three? Rav says that the levi repeats: the kohen reads three verses, and the levi goes back and repeats the third verse, then adds two more. Shmuel says that they divide a verse: the kohen reads the first half of the third verse, for a total of two and a half verses, and the levi reads the latter half of the third verse plus two additional verses. The Gemara asks why Rav does not agree to dividing a verse, and answers that any verse not divided by Moshe may not be divided. Why does Shmuel not agree to repeating? Because of concern for those entering. If the levi repeats one verse, he begins two verses after the section break, and those entering may think that the kohen read only those two verses from the beginning of the section until the place where the levi began. Rav must hold that where no alternative exists we are not concerned for those entering, because one who enters will ask those present what the kohen read and they will tell him. The Gemara then cites a Baraisa: a section of only five verses is read entirely by one reader. If the first reader read only three, the second reads the remaining two and one verse from the next section; others say he reads the remaining two and three from the next section, because one may not begin a section with fewer than three verses. The Gemara asks: according to the view that one repeats, why not repeat instead of reading from the next section? It answers: here it is different because another solution is possible. This disproves Agudah’s concern. Even though another solution is possible, the second reader should have repeated because of those leaving during his reading, who may think he will read only two verses until the section break. One cannot answer that if he repeated there would remain only two verses after the section break, causing concern for those entering, because the first opinion is not concerned about that, as explained there. Necessarily, one does not repeat merely because of those leaving, for it is forbidden to leave during the reading, as stated below in siman 146.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Regarding Rashi’s reading of the Gemara’s question, “According to the one who says repeat, let him repeat,” and not, “According to the one who says divide, let him divide.”</b> Tosafos ask that the questioner should also have asked according to the view that divides: let him divide and repeat. Shmuel, who holds that one divides, would also agree that repetition is valid in principle, but rejects it only because of concern for those entering. Here, where the first reader read three verses and left two, the second could repeat half of the third verse. Tosafos explain that this is not done “lest those entering err, since this reader began two verses before the section break.” Magen Avraham explains that they may think the Sages’ enactment has been uprooted, because those entering, unaware that an error occurred, will conclude that one may initially begin two verses before a section break, meaning that the preceding reader may leave two verses before the section break. Tosafos answer that according to the view that divides, there is no difficulty, because where it is possible not to divide, one certainly should not divide. But according to the view that repeats, the question remains. This means that even the questioner understood that where another option exists one should not both divide and repeat, despite the concern that those entering may think the Sages’ enactment was uprooted. According to the view that repeats alone, however, the questioner thought repetition would still be proper because of that concern. This may also be Agudah’s reason for requiring the second reader to begin earlier, lest those entering think the enactment was uprooted.

Ran answers that if this were done, not only those entering but even those present would mistakenly think that one may initially begin a section with fewer than three verses, thereby nullifying the Sages’ enactment, because they would not know that this unusual arrangement was adopted only from necessity.
