Beit Yosef concludes: “He is not presently about to perform them. When he later performs them, he will recite the particular blessing instituted for that mitzvah.”

(ס"ק ו) אם למד עסי' סמ"ך בא ליישב קושית הרב"י דמאי ספק יש אי סגי בקור' ק"ש סמוך לה כמ"ש בסעיף שאח"ז דע"כ צ"ל דק"ש לא נחשב ללימוד לענין זה ודינה בזה כתפלה ותחנונים דאל"כ למה התנה בירוש' דאין אהבה רבה פוטרת אלא בשלמד מיד כמבו' פה בש"ע ת"ל הא כל פעם צ"ל ק"ש אחר ברכת אהבה רבה וע"ש מ"ש ולזה רמז מ"א לעמ"ש סי' ס' דכ' שם אם קרא ק"ש בלי ברכות צ"ל אח"ז הברכות ומצד הדין א"צ שוב לקרות ק"ש ע"ש. וא"כ משכחת לה כה"ג. דאמר אח"ז אהבה רבה ולא קרא אז ק"ש. כגון זה אמר הירוש' ודוקא כשלמד מיד אבל לעולם י"ל דק"ש נחשב כד"ת. ואז פוטרת לימוד של כל היום. ר"ל י"מ הובא בהרב"י דהתנאי אם למד דבירוש' ר"ל שפוטר רק מה שלומד מיד לא מה שלמד אח"כ אם הפסיק בין לימוד ראשון ללימוד שני קמ"ל דאינו כן ודין ברכת אהבה רבה אם למד מיד כדין ברכת התורה ממש:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>(Seif katan 6) If he studied, see siman 60.</b> Magen Avraham comes to resolve Beit Yosef's question: what doubt is there whether reciting Krias Shema immediately afterward is sufficient, as stated in the next seif?

One must apparently say that Krias Shema does not count as Torah study for this purpose, but has the status of prayer and supplication. Otherwise, why does the Yerushalmi require one to study immediately after Ahavah Rabbah? One always recites Krias Shema after Ahavah Rabbah.

Magen Avraham alludes to what he wrote in siman 60: one who recited Krias Shema without its blessings must afterward recite the blessings, but by strict law need not repeat Krias Shema. See there.

Thus, one can have a case in which he afterward recites Ahavah Rabbah without then reciting Krias Shema. The Yerushalmi says that in such a case he must study immediately.

It therefore remains possible that Krias Shema itself counts as Torah study and, when recited then, exempts the day's later study.

The explanation cited by Beit Yosef, that “if he studied” means Ahavah Rabbah exempts only the study performed immediately and not later study after an interruption, is rejected. Rather, once he studied immediately, Ahavah Rabbah has the same law as the regular blessing over Torah.

<b>(Seif katan 9) Since, etc.</b> The wording implies, etc.

Taz writes that one who has a fixed daily Torah-study period and spends the rest of the day in business need not recite a new blessing if, after finishing his fixed period and going to work, no business opportunity arises and he returns to study.

The obligation to study rests upon him at all times. There is no lapse of attention. His business burdens merely exempt him temporarily. Once no business arises, the original obligation again applies, and the first blessing continues to exempt him. See Taz's extended discussion.

Magen Avraham may agree in this case. Magen Avraham discusses a person who has no regular study practice and once decides to begin learning. He had no prior intention that his blessing should exempt that study, and therefore he must recite a blessing.

Taz may likewise agree in that case.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>You can know this, for according to Rabbeinu Tam, etc.</b> Beit Yosef cites Agur, who writes that his father would not recite a new blessing after a fixed daytime sleep because the poskim dispute whether one must do so.

Beit Yosef objects that no such dispute is found and that everyone requires a blessing. He then suggests that Agur's father relied upon Rabbeinu Tam, who holds that even an entire night's sleep is not an interruption.

Although we do not rule like Rabbeinu Tam regarding nighttime sleep, perhaps his view should still be considered regarding daytime sleep. This is the alternative opinion cited in seif 11.

Magen Avraham explains that according to Rabbeinu Tam, since even nighttime sleep is not an interruption, the reason Birchas HaTorah is recited each day must be that the Sages instituted it anew every day, like the morning blessings.

According to him, one who rises before dawn would not recite it until dawn.

It is therefore possible that Agur's father and the alternative opinion in seif 11, which take Rabbeinu Tam's view into account, accept his basic principle that neither daytime nor nighttime sleep is an interruption, and that the Sages instituted Birchas HaTorah anew each day like the morning blessings.

We disagree with Rabbeinu Tam only in this detail: one who rises before dawn must recite it immediately, as stated in seif 13. The prior blessing exempts only one day. Once he rises, even before dawn, it is considered a new day for this purpose.

Accordingly, one who remained awake all night must at least recite the blessing when dawn arrives, because the prior blessing exempted only one day, like the other morning blessings.

One might alternatively say that the alternative opinion does not fully accept Rabbeinu Tam. Perhaps it holds that a fixed daytime sleep is not an interruption and wakefulness at night is not an interruption, but nighttime sleep is an interruption. If so, one who remained awake all night would not recite a new blessing.

Magen Avraham therefore says: “You can know this, for according to Rabbeinu Tam,” etc. Since Rabbeinu Tam certainly requires a new blessing because of the new day, it is preferable not to multiply the dispute between our view and his. We should adopt the first understanding: we agree with his general principle and disagree only regarding one who rises before dawn.

Accordingly, one who remained awake all night must recite the blessing.

In siman 494, Magen Avraham himself rules that one who remained awake all night should recite the blessing when day begins. If someone present slept during the night, he should listen to that person's Birchas HaTorah, with both intending that the listener fulfill his obligation.

Eliyah Rabbah writes likewise.

In siman 46, seif katan 12, Eliyah Rabbah writes that one who did not sleep all night should not recite Elokai Neshamah or HaMaavir Sheinah.
