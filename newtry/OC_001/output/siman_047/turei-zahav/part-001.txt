<b>But it is better, etc.</b> In this way one satisfies all opinions.

If one says Haarev Na without a vav, it appears to be a separate blessing and creates an interruption according to the view that it is one blessing.

Rashba writes that no blessing is recited after Torah study because Torah study is a mitzvah, and no blessing is recited after mitzvos. Although the congregation recites a blessing after public Torah reading, that is an enactment of Moshe and Ezra, and blessings are recited over enactments, as with Hallel and Megillah.

Ri explains that Asher Bachar Banu begins with Baruch because it is often recited independently when blessing over public Torah reading.

Rif's text includes, “All of us who know Your Name and study Your Torah for its own sake.” This does not appear in the Gemara, Rosh, or Tur.

Nevertheless, when saying Haarev Na one should intend that Torah be sweet to us so that we engage in it for its own sake and not for any ulterior purpose, Heaven forbid.

Tur writes that in this blessing one should direct his mind toward the revelation at Sinai: Hashem chose us from all nations, drew us near before Mount Sinai, caused us to hear His words from the fire, and gave us His holy Torah, His cherished instrument with which He delights every day.

The concluding formula is “Who gives the Torah,” in the present tense, rather than “Who gave.” Hashem continually gives us His Torah each day as we engage in it and grants us new explanations. This is like the teaching on “May her breasts satisfy you at all times,” comparing Torah to an infant who constantly finds new nourishment.

<b>If he studied.</b> Tosafos, Rosh, and Mordechai cite the Yerushalmi: this applies only if he studied immediately.

Ri rules that this requirement applies specifically to Ahavah Rabbah. Since it was not instituted primarily as a blessing over Torah but as a blessing of Krias Shema, it exempts Torah study only if one studies immediately.

The blessings Asher Bachar Banu and LaAsok BaTorah were instituted specifically as blessings over Torah and exempt the entire day.

The reason ordinary interruptions do not require a new blessing is that a person never abandons his intention toward Torah. Every hour he remains obligated to learn, as the verse states, “You shall contemplate it day and night.” It is therefore as though he studies all day.

This differs from sukkah, where eating occurs at fixed times and a new blessing is required for each meal.

These words show that after the ordinary blessing over Torah, one need not begin learning immediately. This differs from other mitzvah blessings, where the mitzvah must follow at once. Torah study extends throughout the day, and only practical burdens temporarily exempt him.

The same reasoning that prevents an interruption during the day from canceling the blessing also prevents a delay between the blessing and the beginning of study from doing so.

I am surprised why the poskim needed to justify our failure to study immediately after Ahavah Rabbah by distinguishing that blessing from the regular Torah blessings.

We actually do study immediately after the morning blessings, for we recite the passage of the daily offering, the Mishnah Eizehu Mekoman, and the baraisa of Rabbi Yishmael's thirteen principles. These are Torah study.

One cannot say that the passage of the daily offering and the sacrificial Mishnayos count only as substitutes for offerings and not as Torah. Tur and Shulchan Aruch in siman 50 state that these passages are recited in place of study, and the baraisa of Rabbi Yishmael is certainly Gemara.

This is exactly immediate study. Even after Ahavah Rabbah, once one studies immediately, a later interruption does not matter. All the more so after the regular blessing over Torah.

Otherwise, what of those who do no other learning during the day? Could their morning blessing be in vain, Heaven forbid? Clearly, they fulfill their learning through reciting the passage of the daily offering.

The poskim apparently wrote their distinction only for added clarity, to show that even where the passage of the daily offering cannot be recited immediately, the later interruption still does not invalidate the regular blessing over Torah.

<b>כיון שדעתו כו'. </b> לכאורה משמע מזה באין דעתו לחזור כגון שיש לו עת קבוע ושאר היום יוצא לעסקיו ואזדמין ליה באיזה יום שאינו יכול להתעסק בעסקיו ומתוך כך הוא חוזר ללמודו צריך לברך שנית דהוה כמו נמלך אבל אין האמת כן דכבר נתבאר לעיל הטעם שאין הפסק מפסיד כלום מטעם שחיוב הלימוד תמיד רובץ עליו אלא שהטרד' פוטרתו וכל שעה שאינו טרוד חל עליו החיוב ע"כ א"צ לחזור ולברך ולאו בדעתו תליא מילתא ותו אדרב' אמרי' דמסתמא הוה דעתו אם יזדמן שיוכל ללמוד אפ' בשעה שאינו רגיל ללמוד שילמוד וכן מורה לשון הש"ע שאומר כיון שדעתו לחזור ולא אמר אם דעתו לחזור אלא מסתמא אמרינן דעתו כן:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Since he intends, etc.</b> At first glance, this implies that one who does not intend to return to learning, such as one with a fixed study period who spends the rest of the day in business, would need a new blessing if circumstances unexpectedly allow him to return to study.

This is not correct. As explained above, an interruption does not invalidate the blessing because the obligation to learn constantly rests upon him, and only practical burdens temporarily exempt him.

Whenever he is no longer occupied, the obligation returns. He therefore need not recite another blessing, and the matter does not depend upon his actual intention.

Moreover, we presume that if an opportunity to study arises even during a time when he does not ordinarily learn, he intends to take advantage of it.

This is also indicated by Shulchan Aruch's wording: “Since he intends to return,” not, “If he intends to return.” The presumption is that this is his intention.
