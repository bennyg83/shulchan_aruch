<small>Magen Avraham, seif katan 12.</small> “This requires study, for according to the view, etc.”

In my humble opinion, there is no difficulty.

Beit Yosef explicitly explains the origin of the custom not to recite a new blessing after daytime sleep. It comes from Agur, who writes that his father instituted this practice and that one loses nothing by being lenient regarding blessings.

Beit Yosef explains that according to Rabbeinu Tam, even nighttime sleep is not an interruption. Although the halachah does not follow him because all other poskim disagree, his view should still be considered regarding daytime sleep.

Thus, because of the concern for an unnecessary blessing, the custom is not to recite a new blessing after daytime sleep and to take Rabbeinu Tam's view into account.

One who remains awake all night, however, should not recite the blessing according to the ordinary reading of Shulchan Aruch, because the primary halachah is unlike Rabbeinu Tam.

If one sleeps at night, he recites the blessing when daylight comes under either possibility: according to Rabbeinu Tam, the new day creates the obligation; according to Rosh, the sleep creates the obligation.

The same logic applies to one who slept during the day and remained awake the entire following night. He recites the blessing in the morning under either possibility. According to Rabbeinu Tam, the blessing extends only for a twenty-four-hour period. According to Rosh, daytime sleep itself is an interruption.

One might argue that in such a case no blessing is required because Ahavas Olam before the evening Krias Shema exempted him. It contains the substance of a blessing over Torah and resembles Ahavah Rabbah in the morning.

Nevertheless, the primary ruling appears to require the blessing.

According to Rabbeinu Tam he certainly must recite it. According to Raavad and those who agree with him, he must likewise recite it, because Ahavah Rabbah exempts only Torah study performed immediately afterward. Peri Chadash agrees.

According to many other poskim, Krias Shema itself does not count as Torah study, as the Mechaber's wording implies: “There is room for doubt whether reciting Krias Shema is sufficient.”

Had the principal ruling been that Krias Shema counts as study, the Mechaber should have written the greater novelty that one who had not recited the blessing beforehand should not recite it afterward. His wording instead implies that Krias Shema does not count as Torah study according to the straightforward Yerushalmi, and one should recite the blessing beforehand merely as a precaution.

Rama at the end of siman 52 likewise writes that one should nevertheless recite all the blessings as explained in simanim 46 and 47. His reference to siman 47 indicates that the blessing over Torah should be recited after prayer, because Krias Shema is not considered study.

Accordingly, if one did not study immediately after Maariv, Ahavas Olam did not exempt him.

Magen Avraham's basic inference from Shulchan Aruch, that one who remains awake all night need not recite the blessing in the morning, is also surprising. On the contrary, the rule that the night follows the day implies that the blessing exempts only one day.

Although I am unworthy to disagree with Magen Avraham, he himself rules in siman 494 that one who remains awake all night should recite the blessing.

It is therefore proper to combine this reasoning and say that Shulchan Aruch agrees.

According to all opinions, one who slept during the day and then remained awake the entire following night must certainly recite the blessing based upon the double reasoning above. There is no need to fear that Ahavas Olam exempted him, because many poskim hold that it exempts only immediate study and many hold that Krias Shema is not considered study. This appears correct.
