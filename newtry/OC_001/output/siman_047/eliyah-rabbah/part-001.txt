<b>(4) With a vav, etc.</b> Levush explains that VeHaarev Na is one blessing together with LaAsok BeDivrei Torah. In my opinion, this is not conclusive. Perhaps it does not begin with Baruch merely because it is adjacent to the preceding blessing.

This is difficult, because Beit Yosef writes that short blessings begin with Baruch even when adjacent. If Levush follows Rambam and Baal HaMaor, who disagree, he should have explained this, especially since Beit Yosef explicitly records their dispute.

It is still more difficult because in siman 46, seif 4, regarding the morning blessings, Levush himself explains Rabbeinu Tam's view on the basis that they are short blessings.

Beit Yosef also does not conclusively rule that it is one blessing. He cites Abudraham, who agrees with Rambam and Tosafos that they are two blessings and that the custom is to say Haarev without a vav.

Beit Yosef nevertheless concludes that practically one should say VeHaarev with a vav. According to those who hold it is one blessing, omitting the vav would interrupt in the middle. According to those who hold it is separate, adding the vav causes no problem.

This indicates that Beit Yosef leans more toward Rambam and those with him, and recommends the vav only as the safer formulation.

The opposite may be argued. Even according to those who hold it is one blessing, omitting the vav should not count as an interruption. Bach writes similarly below in siman 207 concerning Borei Nefashos, that the clause may be understood with or without a vav.

Furthermore, Haarev is a request, whereas LaAsok BeDivrei Torah is thanksgiving for the commandment to engage in Torah, as Tzeidah LaDerech writes.

Many blessings likewise shift from thanksgiving to request without a vav. An example is the blessing beginning “You existed before the world was created,” whose conclusion is “Sanctify Your Name,” without a vav.

Conversely, according to those who hold they are two blessings, saying VeHaarev with a vav makes it appear to be merely the conclusion of the first blessing, leaving one blessing missing.

There is also proof from Rosh and the other poskim who cite the Gemara's wording, “Rabbi Yochanan said: Haarev Na,” without a vav.

Even according to our text in Berachos 11a, “Rabbi Yochanan concluded with Haarev Na,” the wording shows that the conclusion of LaAsok has already occurred, yet Haarev still appears without a vav. Rashi also has this wording.

Even Rabbeinu Tam, who said VeHaarev with a vav, may have done so not because he was certain it was one blessing, but because many poskim treated it as two and the wording sounds like a new future-oriented request. Listeners might therefore mistakenly answer amen before Haarev Na. He added the vav to prevent that error.

This also explains his use of a vav before Yehi Ratzon in siman 46, where the wording sounds like a new blessing because the conclusion does not resemble the opening, as Tosafos writes in Berachos 46a and Rokeach likewise holds.

Without the concern of an erroneous amen, he too might have omitted the vav despite regarding it as one blessing.

Moreover, Sefer Tanya in the name of Rav Natronai Gaon, Shibolei HaLeket, Rokeach, siman 302, and Tzeidah LaDerech, page 19, rule like Rambam that they are two blessings.

Abudraham explains that the three blessings correspond to Scripture, Mishnah, and Gemara, and Shulchan Aruch records that Mahari Luria practiced accordingly.

Therefore, the practical halachah appears to be to say Haarev without a vav. Nevertheless, one should not be lenient regarding answering amen.

Rashba asks why no blessing is recited after Torah study like the blessing after public Torah reading. He answers that Torah study is a mitzvah, and blessings are not recited after mitzvos. Public Torah reading is a single enactment, and blessings are recited over enactments, as with Hallel and Megillah.

Beit Yosef answers that the mitzvah to meditate upon Torah applies day and night, so no final blessing is appropriate. I found the same in Shibolei HaLeket, siman 2.

Me'adanei Melech asks from Berachos 21a, where a final blessing over Torah is derived by an a fortiori argument from food. One could reject the argument because food provides physical benefit.

He notes that Berachos 48b instead argues: if one blesses for temporal life, all the more so for eternal life. His answer is forced.

In my humble opinion, the objection from benefit could not be raised there because eternal life is certainly a greater benefit. On page 21, however, the proposed inference is specifically from the fact that food requires a final blessing though not an initial Torah-level blessing. There the answer is appropriate: food is different because of direct physical benefit.

My father explained that the Gemara means: food is physically enjoyed, and the Sages derive that a person may not benefit without a blessing from “The earth and its fullness belong to Hashem.” Thus, food also requires an initial blessing, and the a fortiori argument collapses.

Levush writes that the two morning blessings over Torah are arranged so that one serves as a final blessing upon the previous day's learning.

Nachalas Tzvi disagrees and explains that the two blessings correspond to Written Torah and Oral Torah. Bircas Avraham writes likewise.

Nachalas Tzvi mistakenly attributes to Levush the position that LaAsok is the first blessing and Asher Bachar Banu the final blessing for yesterday's study. Levush explains the reverse, as stated explicitly at the end of his gloss.

Nachalas Tzvi also challenges Rashi in Bava Metzia 85b, who says that “Who gave us the Torah of truth” was recited before words of Torah, apparently against Berachos 11 and all the poskim who say Asher Bachar Banu is recited before study.

My father wished to answer that Rashi only meant that one blesses before Torah study in thanksgiving that Hashem gave us the Torah of truth, not that the specific formula Asher Nasan was recited first.

Some explain in a gloss to Levush that one blessing is a blessing over the mitzvah and the other a blessing over enjoyment, because Torah study is pleasurable. This is difficult from Berachos 21a, which challenges the comparison to food precisely because food provides enjoyment.
