[א] <b>בני חורין וכו'.</b> לאפוקי עבדים גמורים ובחצי עבד וחצי בן חורין יש להסתפק, ונראה כיון דקיימא לן דכופין את רבו ועושה בן חורין הרי חייב וכן משמע בריש חגיגה, עד כאן לשון עולת תמיד. וקשה דלקמן סימן קצ"ט בזימון כתב זה לשונו מדכתב עבדים ולא כתב בן חורין משמע דחצי עבד מזמנים עליו, עד כאן, ואם כן הכא דנקט בן חורין הוא לאפוקי חצי עבד, גם מהא דחגיגה שכתב אין ראיה כלל דשם הטעם מי שיש לו אדון אחד וכו' וכיון דכופין רבו הוי כאילו אין לו רבו אלא אדון אחד וחייב בראייה כמו שכתבו תוס' שם דף ד' ד"ה לא וכו', ובפרט למה שכתבתי בחידושי רשב"א גיטין דף ס"ו ולא הקפידה תורה בעבד משום שיש לו אדון אחד אלא משום שרבו מעכבו משום ביטול מעשה ידיו, ובזה שכופין לרבו לשחררו ואין לו מעשה ידיו חייב בראייה עיין שם, ואם כן בשאר מצוות נשאר בו חצי עבדות עד שיכתוב לו רבו שטר שיחרור, וכן הדין בשופר סימן תקפ"ט וסימן תרפ"ט במגילה ואפילו לעצמו אינו מוציא:
**** ENGLISH ****
[1] <b>Free men, etc.</b> This excludes full slaves. Regarding one who is half slave and half free, there is room for doubt. Olas Tamid writes that since the halachah is that we compel his master to free him, he is obligated, as is also implied at the beginning of Chagigah. This is difficult, because below, siman 199, regarding zimun, Olas Tamid writes: “Since the text says slaves and does not say free men, it implies that one who is half slave may be included in a zimun.” Here, however, where the text specifically says free men, this excludes one who is half slave. Nor is there any proof from Chagigah. There, the reason is that the mitzvah applies to one who has only one master. Since we compel his master to free him, it is as though he has only one master, and he is obligated in appearing at the Temple, as Tosafos write there, 4a, s.v. lo. This is especially so according to what I wrote in my novellae to Rashba, Gittin 66a, that the Torah did not exempt a slave because he has another master, but because his master prevents him on account of the loss of his labor. Since we compel the master to free him and the master no longer has rights to his labor, he is obligated in appearing. See there. Regarding other mitzvos, however, his half-slave status remains until his master gives him a bill of emancipation. The same law applies to shofar in siman 589 and to Megillah in siman 689: he cannot discharge even his own obligation.

[3] <b>Even when rolled like a Sefer Torah, etc.</b> It appears that in pressing circumstances this is permitted, since the prohibition is only because of the honor of the congregation. Even according to Beit Yosef, who implies from Raavyah that it is forbidden even if the congregation waives its honor, so that they will be distressed and purchase a Sefer Torah, this applies only to reading regularly in this manner. If it occurred occasionally and they have no other Sefer Torah, it is permitted. Moreover, most poskim disagree with Raavyah. It further appears to me that Raavyah discusses our ordinary Chumashim. With one Chumash prepared in the manner of a Sefer Torah, this rationale does not apply, for they will necessarily purchase more, since they possess only one Chumash. This requires study. [5] <b>They bring another Sefer Torah, etc.</b> Malbushei Yom Tov writes that our master, the Gaon Rabbi Loew, of blessed memory, instituted here in Prague not to bring another Sefer Torah. He ruled according to the opinion that only when an entire sheet is missing may one not read from it. Initially, one should not bring out a Sefer Torah missing even one word, but where letters affect the reading, because written Torah may not be recited by heart, the missing text should be read from a Chumash. In the case of an extra letter, however, one may initially bring it out. I likewise saw this in Tiferes Yisrael, and Lechem Chamudos, page 87, rules this way. They conclude that the missing text should in any event be read from a Chumash. This somewhat implies that when reading from our Chumashim there is no prohibition against reciting written Torah by heart. Responsa Rashba cited above in seif katan 3 also somewhat implies this. Below, siman 690, seif 4, concerning Megillah, the implication is otherwise, and this requires study. They further write that the prohibition against reciting by heart applies only to a complete subject or passage, but a word that must be joined to the surrounding text is not called written Torah recited by heart. In Eliyah Zuta I wrote that the practice here in Prague follows the unqualified ruling of the Shulchan Aruch and Levush. My grandfather, the Gaon of blessed memory, likewise practiced this way. Taz and Shach in Yoreh Deah, siman 279, also rule like the Shulchan Aruch and do not mention those great authorities. The same is implied by Responsa Maharam of Lublin, Nachalas Tzvi, and the other later authorities. Shelah, page 135, is stricter and holds that even where the subject does not change but only the wording changes, such as keves and kesev, another Sefer Torah must be brought. He challenges Levush, who rules that another is not brought in such a case. In my humble opinion, his challenge is unfounded, because Levush also holds this view. Levush writes at the end of this seif that when neither the subject nor the wording changes, another is not brought, implying that if the wording changes even though the subject does not, another is brought. It further appears to me from Baal HaTurim on Parashas Vayikra that even the subject changes, for he writes that kesev denotes one grown among the flock. Thus keves may mean an ordinary sheep. My brother, Rabbi Michel, said that since Yalkut on Parashas Pinchas states that Beis Shammai interpret kevasim as subduing the sins of Israel, while Beis Hillel interpret it as laundering the sins of Israel, this cannot be derived from kesev, and it therefore constitutes a change. An incident occurred at the end of Parashas Masei where “and enclosures for your flocks” was written vetzonam instead of vetzonachem. I ruled that another Sefer Torah should be brought. First, the wording changes, as with keves and kesev according to Shelah. Second, Gur Aryeh writes that tzonachem denotes an abundance of flocks, like “sheep and thousands, all of them,” so the meaning also changes. Another incident occurred at the end of Parashas Vayeira, in the passage concerning Hagar, where al tiri was written without a yud. It appeared to me that another Sefer Torah should be brought, because this changes the meaning: without the yud it is not an expression of fear but of seeing. In Parashas Mikeitz the word is written with two yuds and Targum renders it as fear. Some were uncertain because the word may be pronounced tiri without a yud as with a yud, although without a yud there is no long vowel. It appears that whenever there is even some doubt whether the meaning changes, one may rely on the great authorities cited above and not bring another Sefer Torah. Magen Avraham writes that if one letter is attached to another, one may rely on Rashba, who validates it, and need not bring another. Bach, siman 32. Rashba, however, validated only an attachment at the end of the letter. If it is attached in the middle, and certainly at its head, he agrees it is invalid, as he writes in responsum 611. In my humble opinion, Bach implies that in every case another Sefer Torah need not be brought. The reason appears to be that Rashba invalidates attachment at the head or middle because he considers it equivalent to hollowed-out writing and holds that scraping does not repair it. According to our ruling in siman 32 that scraping does repair it, one may rely upon the principle that anything fit for mixing is not invalidated by failure to mix. The great authorities I cited are also sufficient to rely upon not to bring another. If, however, the attachment altered the form of the letter so that it is no longer recognizable, another Sefer Torah must be brought. Bach writes similarly in Even HaEzer, siman 121. Not only for one extra word where the meaning changes, as implied at the end of this siman, but even where an entire word was duplicated, the practice is to bring another Sefer Torah. The reason appears to be that duplication is subject to exposition and therefore changes the meaning, and an addition is equivalent to a deletion. See Yoreh Deah, beginning of siman 283, concerning joining Torah with Neviim, Taz at the end of Yoreh Deah, siman 274, and above, siman 32, seif katan 37. From this Taz concludes there that if an error is found during the final aliyah before Kaddish or during maftir, another Sefer Torah is not brought at all. His reason is the explicit Gemara in Yoma, which states that the passage “On the tenth” is recited by heart so as not to roll a Sefer Torah in public. He writes at length that there is no distinction between the era of the Gemara and the present. I am astonished that he did not mention Bach at the end of siman 144, who concludes that there is a distinction. Moreover, the Yerushalmi in the chapter Ba Lo states that “On the tenth” is recited by heart because it is part of the order of the day. One may also distinguish that the passage “On the tenth of the month” is not read by virtue of Ezra’s enactment, but as an independent obligation, as Rashi explains there. It is therefore recited by heart. All our Torah readings, however, derive from Ezra’s enactment, and Ezra instituted reading specifically from a Sefer Torah, as written at the beginning of siman 144. Even if only one word is not read from a Sefer Torah, it is possible that the enactment has not been fulfilled. This also appears to explain why the practice does not follow Maharil and Lechem Chamudos. See also the end of siman 49, where the ruling disagrees with Taz. Although the objections to Taz can be answered, for his reason that the passage “On the tenth” is familiar and may therefore be recited by heart appears from Mordechai cited by Beit Yosef in siman 49 not to apply there, nevertheless the reason he gives, that the passage is not being recited to discharge others, would according to Bach apply only where one is familiar with it, in which case it may be said for oneself but not to discharge others, and that explanation is impossible. What he writes in the name of Rabbeinu Yonah, that the passage of offerings is different, is also difficult, for Beit Yosef and Shulchan Aruch there imply that all poskim disagree with Rabbeinu Yonah. Nevertheless, Taz’s practical ruling is correct, and this is the practice even during the final aliyah and the ordinary maftir of Shabbos. Responsa Beer Eisek rules likewise, and concludes that on one occasion several Sifrei Torah were found successively to contain errors after the day’s obligatory reading had already been completed. Because replacing them imposed great burden upon the congregation, he ruled that they should complete the passage from that Sefer Torah, unlike Magen Avraham. A newly printed edition of Taz on Orach Chayim now states: “In Yoreh Deah, siman 279, I concluded that if an error is found where it is impossible to stop, such as where fewer than three verses would remain, or during the final aliyah, he should recite the erroneous word by heart, complete the reading from the invalid Sefer Torah, and afterward recite the concluding blessing.” This is even more astonishing, because in Yoreh Deah it is explicit that this applies specifically to the final aliyah. If the error is found where interruption is impossible, another Sefer Torah is brought, they stop, and the concluding blessing is recited after the reading from the other Sefer Torah. Taz also challenges Rama: according to Rabbi Yosef Ibn Lev, even if three verses were read, the reader should not recite the concluding blessing, but should bring another Sefer Torah. In my humble opinion, Rama holds that Rabbi Yosef Ibn Lev agrees where interruption is possible. If another Sefer Torah is brought, there is complete diversion of attention and a new opening blessing is required for what is read from the second. This differs from a case where interruption is impossible. This rejects all that Magen Avraham wrote on this matter.
