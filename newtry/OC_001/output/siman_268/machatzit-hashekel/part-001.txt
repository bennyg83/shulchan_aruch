<b>Paragraph 1: He completes, etc.; for by law, etc.</b> Berachos 21a says: Rav Yehudah in Shmuel's name rules that one who remembers during prayer that he already prayed stops even mid-blessing. This differs from students who mistakenly mention weekday blessings on Shabbos and complete that blessing. There the person is fundamentally obligated, but the Sages spared him exertion for Shabbos honor; here he already prayed. The passage means as Magen Avraham explains.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>For in Musaf one need not pray eighteen, etc.</b> Even on weekday Musaf (Rosh Chodesh or Chol HaMoed) only seven blessings are said. The first view, which also requires completing a mistaken blessing in Musaf, holds Musaf is fundamentally fit for eighteen blessings. The Yerushalmi reports that Rosh Chodesh and Chol HaMoed Musaf once consisted of eighteen with an added request to perform the daily and Musaf offerings. Shabbos too fundamentally warranted eighteen with a Musaf addition, but the Sages did not burden worshippers.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Paragraph 2: In the middle, etc.; in Atah Chonen, unlike Shiltei HaGibborim.</b> Shiltei HaGibborim on Mordechai says one who began a weekday blessing completes it, except in Musaf: there he completes Atah Chonen but stops in other blessings. He gives no reason. Rosh of Lunel limits completion to Atah Chonen, the first blessing; Rosh disagrees and treats all equally, as Shulchan Aruch rules. Bach explains Rosh of Lunel: Atah Chonen properly belongs even on Shabbos, for the Yerushalmi says it was placed first because without understanding there is no prayer; it is therefore a prayer need, not forbidden personal request. Other blessings are ordinary requests. Rosh disagrees on reasoning, not necessarily another Talmudic text, since the Gemara says only that he is obligated. Shiltei HaGibborim agrees that in ordinary prayers all eighteen are included in this obligation, but Musaf fundamentally does not take eighteen. He nevertheless requires completing Atah Chonen because it belongs to every prayer. Magen Avraham appears to follow Knesses HaGedolah that under the alternative view one stops even in Atah Chonen during Musaf.

**** ENGLISH ****
Raavad comments on Berachos 12a's case of holding wine, beginning with intent for Shehakol, and ending Borei Pri HaGefen (valid because even Shehakol would suffice) and the reverse case of beer, beginning with intent for HaGefen but ending Shehakol. Rashi says the spoken opening was correct while its intended ending was wrong; the Gemara asks whether opening intention or correct conclusion controls. Raavad asks why incorrect intention should invalidate when the words were correct and mitzvos do not require intention. Rosh answers that no intention is preferable to deliberately redirecting intention to another blessing. Mordechai therefore says that on Yom Tov one who accidentally concludes for Shabbos corrects within toch kedei dibbur, as below in siman 487. Though the Gemara's question was unresolved and might be lenient, Tosafos rules stringently; see Magen Avraham 209:3. Alternatively, the beer case is lenient only because its correct conclusion may control. Here everything depends on the opening: once he says Atah Chonen he certainly completes it. Consequently, even saying only Atah, which could begin Atah Kidashta or Atah Echad, while intending a weekday blessing is as though he spoke that intention and began the weekday blessing, requiring completion.

<b>But according to what was written above, siman 209, etc.; therefore Beis Yosef omitted, etc.</b> Tosefes Shabbos objects that Shulchan Aruch in 209 follows Rambam: holding beer or water, intending Shehakol, but mistakenly concluding HaGefen is valid because intention was correct despite improper speech; the reverse likewise. The alternative Rashi view also does not follow Raavad. Why then omit Terumas HaDeshen here? Magen Avraham means his own conclusion at the beginning of siman 209: most poskim do not follow intention. Therefore, after saying Atah correctly, intention alone does not force a weekday ending. Beis Yosef omitted Terumas HaDeshen because this law depends on the siman 209 dispute. In 209 Shulchan Aruch first gives Rambam's view, then Rashi's as 'some say,' and finally Rif's distinct case. Magen Avraham explains that despite presenting Rambam primarily, Beis Yosef did not resolve the law absolutely but adopted each leniency because of doubtful blessings. Correct intention with wrong speech may rely on Rambam; correct speech with wrong intention may rely on Rashi; Rif addresses a different case of correcting speech within toch kedei dibbur. Likewise here, after only Atah in an Atah Kidashta or Atah Echad prayer, doubt whether intention controls means he should not complete a weekday blessing. Siman 479:6 similarly follows Rashi leniently: one who began intending to count four but remembered and correctly concluded five does not repeat.

<b>Paragraph 13: In a house, etc.; where they gather, etc.</b> Taz says that if a place is fixed for prayer for several days, Me'ein Sheva is recited there. Eliyah Rabbah remains uncertain whether a place without a Torah scroll counts as fixed.
