(1) <b>[Se'if 1] When he rises to pray, if he was standing in the Diaspora, etc.</b> Berachos 30a teaches: A blind person, or one unable to determine the directions, should direct his heart toward his Father in Heaven, as it says, 'They shall pray to Hashem.' One in the Diaspora directs himself toward Eretz Yisrael; in Eretz Yisrael, toward Jerusalem; in Jerusalem, toward the Temple; in the Temple, toward the Holy of Holies; in the Holy of Holies, toward the place of the Kapores; and behind the Kapores, he should envision himself as standing before it. Thus all Israel direct their hearts to one place, 'the mound toward which all mouths turn.' Tosafos, Tur, and Beis Yosef rule accordingly. Rabbeinu Yonah explains that one in the Diaspora intends not only toward Eretz Yisrael, but also toward Jerusalem, the Temple, and the Holy of Holies; one in Jerusalem still intends toward the Temple and Holy of Holies. This is because the gate of Heaven is there and all prayers ascend through it [Chayei Adam]. Sifrei derives the law from Moshe's viewing the land: just as his distant sight helped bring blessing into Eretz Yisrael, directing one's face and heart toward it helps draw the Divine flow, which descends first there and then spreads outward [Sifrei Devei Rav]. Minchas Aharon adds that facing the proper direction serves as a sign to maintain, 'I have set Hashem before me always.'

**** ENGLISH ****
(2) <b>There: If he was standing in the Diaspora, he should turn his face toward Eretz Yisrael and also intend toward Jerusalem, etc.</b> This wording implies that only his face must be toward Eretz Yisrael, while intention alone suffices for Jerusalem and the Temple [Prishah]. Kol Bo, however, implies that wherever one stands his face should be directed toward the Temple. Its statement that one in the Diaspora faces Jerusalem means Jerusalem together with the Temple, relying on its opening rule. The Gemara's phrase 'direct his heart toward Eretz Yisrael' thus means both face and heart, and Eretz Yisrael includes Jerusalem and the Temple, as Rabbeinu Yonah explains. Eliyah Rabbah understands Levush and Kol Bo likewise, and Chayei Adam also implies this.

**** ENGLISH ****
(5) <b>There: If he stood behind the Kapores, etc.</b> This means west of the Temple, outside the Western Wall [Magen Avraham; Yafeh LaLev]. Today it is forbidden to stand inside the Temple precinct, since all are impure through contact with the dead and we lack the ashes of the red heifer [Shulchan Aruch HaRav; Rambam, Hilchos Bi'as HaMikdash 3]. Now that the Temple is destroyed and we do not know the precise locations of the Cheil and the Azarah, one may not enter anywhere in the Temple area even if pure from every other impurity and bearing only corpse impurity. See Megillah 28a and Shemos Rabbah 2.

**** ENGLISH ****
(6) <b>[Se'if 2] If he prays facing another direction, he should angle his face, etc.</b> Magen Avraham explains that 'angle' rather than 'turn completely' concerns a place whose custom fixes that direction, or one riding a donkey; Shulchan Aruch HaRav agrees. Maamar Mordechai explains it as one facing north to become wealthy or south to become wise, who must nevertheless angle his face toward Eretz Yisrael. Eshel Avraham objects that this would not explain why the Rema states what the Mechaber already taught. Neveh Shalom therefore explains that some circumstance prevents him from facing Eretz Yisrael fully; for example, the congregation has begun and no suitable place remains. He should at least angle toward Eretz Yisrael, which is easy. Without such cause he must face Eretz Yisrael; if he wishes to face north or south, he should only angle his face that way.

**** ENGLISH ****
(7) <b>There: He should angle his face, etc.</b> Taz writes that this remedy helps only one facing north or south. One who mistakenly began facing west should not turn sideways, for then he would face north or south; neither need he interrupt and uproot his feet to face east, since one opinion holds the Divine Presence is everywhere and may be relied upon after the fact when others are not then facing east. Eliyah Rabbah says it is better to turn south, which also inclines toward Eretz Yisrael. Yad Eliyahu requires interrupting and moving to face east; later authorities dispute this. Maamar Mordechai argues that whenever correction is possible it should be made by turning and moving his feet slightly; walking during prayer is allowed when necessary, as in siman 104, and here he barely moves. Tikkunei Zohar forbids praying westward, the direction called 'behind.' Those east of Eretz Yisrael pray west because the Temple is in that direction and the Divine Presence never left its Western Wall. Those west of Eretz Yisrael may not face west. If one mistakenly did so, he should turn east; if north or south inclines toward Eretz Yisrael, he may turn there instead. This necessary turn is not an interruption. If he remembered only after completing the prayer, he need not repeat it, as implied by Rambam at the beginning of Hilchos Tefillah 5.

<b>ט) בה"כ  </b>שהעמידו בו את ארון הקודש לצד דרום של העולם וכולם מתפללים נגד הארון הקודש שהוא לצד דרום יכול להתפלל לצד מזרח אף שכל הקהל מתפללים נגד דרום ואין כאן משום יוהרא ולא משום איבה, יד אליהו סי' א' וגדול אחד שם חלק עליו וכתב דלאו שפיר עביד אם מתפלל עם הצבור ויהפוך לצד אחר רק יתפלל לצד שהצבור מתפללים יעו"ש, והביאו י"א בהגב"י, ש"ץ שם, עמה"ש על קיצור הש"ע סי' י"ח אות ט"ז, חס"ל אות ד' וכתב שם החס"ל וז"ל הביטה וראה כמה שגו אותם העושים הארון לצד אחר משאר רוחות משום נוי בהכ"נ ואין זה נוי אלא גנאי להכנס בתגר זה והרוצה להתנאות במצוה יעשה הארון וכותל מזרחי נגד קרן מזרתית דרומית של העולם אם יוכל וזהו נויו וזהו כבודו של מקום ולא למראה עיני אדם ישפוט, וירצה לפני ה' ע"פ תה"ק זה כל האדם עכ"ד:
**** ENGLISH ****
(9) In a synagogue where the Aron Kodesh was placed on the southern side and the entire congregation faces it, Yad Eliyahu permits an individual to face east without concern for arrogance or hostility. A great authority there disagrees: when praying with the congregation, he should face their direction. Chesed LaAlafim sharply criticizes those who place the Aron on another side merely for beauty. This is not beauty but disgrace. One wishing to beautify the mitzvah should, where possible, place the Aron and eastern wall toward the southeastern direction; that is beauty and honor to the Omnipresent, judging according to the holy Torah rather than human appearance.
