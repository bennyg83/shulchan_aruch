**** ENGLISH ****
[1] <b>A bare Sefer Torah, etc.</b> It is proper for the reader, the one who raises it, and the one who rolls it to be careful to hold even the poles of the Sefer Torah through the corner of a tallis. Bach. So too Responsa Maharit, siman 136. Taz is lenient and writes that even meticulous people follow the lenient practice. Magen Avraham, however, brings proof from the fact that the poles impart impurity to the hands like the parchment sheets. In my humble opinion, this may be because the Sages did not distinguish, and it is comparable to tefillin, which impart impurity to the hands although they are touched bare. The same applies even to sacred writings according to Rama. Tosafos in Megillah 7a are also only uncertain whether the prohibition applies to sacred writings. Magen Avraham writes that it applies specifically when they are written in Ashuris script, on parchment, and with ink, like Megillas Esther, but our printed sefarim are permitted. This implies even before washing one’s hands. Rama’s statement that the custom is not to be stringent regarding sacred writings may be understood as referring to a Megillah, since other printed sefarim are always permitted. This applies, however, only to ordinary hands. If they are known to be dirty, one should be stringent.

[2] <b>Among those, etc.</b> “And they are accustomed to purchase it,” etc. This means that nowadays it is customary to purchase the privilege. In the time of the Gemara, it belonged to the greatest person without payment even if another offered money. Magen Avraham understood the statement as referring to the time of the Gemara and was therefore forced into a strained explanation. This is not precise. The customs of purchasing the privileges of taking out and returning the Sefer Torah are based on “In a multitude of people is the King’s glory.” Maharil. Knesses HaGedolah, siman 153, cites Mahari Bruna regarding two people who jointly purchased mitzvos and stipulated that whenever one was absent from the synagogue, the other would take his privilege in his place. If one died, the condition is void. It appears that the privilege passes to his heirs. Similarly, Shiyarei Knesses HaGedolah writes here that when one purchased a privilege and died, even where there is a holder of deficiencies, meaning the local practice of purchasing the right to fill vacancies, the privilege belongs to the heirs. Where there is an established custom, however, everything follows the custom. He concludes that the heirs must attend to the mitzvah and pay the congregation the amount their ancestor pledged, or sell the privilege and pay the congregation any shortfall. If they sell it for more, the profit belongs to the heirs. Olas Tamid, end of siman 144, objects that one may not profit from mitzvos funded by charity, and therefore the profit belongs to the congregation. In my humble opinion, the principle that one may not profit does not apply here. There, as stated in Kiddushin, one does not profit from consecrated property because “there is no poverty in a place of wealth,” and from charity funds because poor people may arrive and there may be nothing available to give them. None of those reasons applies here. Since he purchased the privilege for himself and paid the price to charity, it became his. Indeed, this occurs every day: even the owner of a privilege sometimes sells it to another when necessary and profits from it. This is also implied by Magen Avraham, siman 306, seif katan 15. Responsa Nachalas Shivah, siman 4, writes that money raised from the sale of mitzvos is fundamentally intended for the poor and not for sacred utensils, although the gabbaim may redirect it. See there also regarding using it for taxes. Emek Berachah, siman 5, states that one may not retain the mitzvah money for himself, but must give it to the gabbaim. Responsa Tzemach David, siman 72, writes that if one wished to purchase a privilege for twenty and another bid thirty, but the second bidder is not financially reliable, the gabbai may compel the first to take it for twenty. Magen Avraham, end of siman 155, copied his words concerning a case where the second bidder later withdrew, and wrote that this is not the accepted practice. I am astonished, for the responsum itself clearly states that when the second bidder withdraws the practice is to compel him to pay the entire amount. Only where he is financially unreliable and the gabbaim refuse to allow him to purchase it must the first bidder take it for twenty, for the second is treated as nonexistent. It is possible to follow this ruling. Yam Shel Shlomo, Beitzah chapter 5, writes that the pious of Austria practiced that whatever a person uttered as a bid, even if another then increased the price and retained the privilege, he nevertheless gave to charity the amount he had already pronounced. If one purchased a privilege for a year and during that year the king expelled the Jews, if he knew of the danger at the time of purchase he should have stipulated. See Responsa Menahem Azariah, siman 64, and Magen Avraham there. Shiyarei Knesses HaGedolah writes that where the custom is to purchase the right to fill vacancies, or if the purchaser himself travels to another city or becomes ill, he may not appoint an agent in his place. See above, siman 133, seif katan 12, regarding Kaddish.

**** ENGLISH ****
[3] <b>Some say that if the mantle, etc.</b> This is from Mordechai, who gives the reason that it resembles the incense altar. This requires study, for on the contrary, the incense altar was covered with gold on the outside. Sefer Chassidim, siman 535, compares it to the boards of the Mishkan, which were covered with gold on the inside. Magen Avraham writes that there appears to be a scribal error in Mordechai, and it should read, “like the Ark.” Agudah at the end of Megillah likewise writes that with respect to the mantles, meaning the wrapping cloth, the silk should face the Sefer Torah, while on the outer mantle the silk should face outward, like the Ark, which was gold inside and outside with wood in the middle. This is strained. I am also astonished that he altered the wording of Agudah, which states: “When they cover the Sefer Torah with a cloth, and there are two or three cloths, the lower cloth should be turned toward the Sefer Torah, and the beautiful upper cloth should face outward.” This discusses a cloth whose two sides are alike, not one that itself has different materials on its two sides. In the latter case, perhaps the outer mantle does not help. I would explain Mordechai as referring to the golden altar being inside and the copper altar outside. Still, this is not a complete proof concerning a cloth whose silk is wrapped around the Sefer Torah as well. Perhaps this is why Rama writes that the practice is not to be stringent. Magen Avraham nevertheless writes that one should follow this rule. In any event, a scrupulous person should make the cloth identical on both sides, even if he places linen in the middle. Agudah writes there that one should be careful not to reverse the cloths so that the sides normally facing inward face outward, just as the boards of the Mishkan were marked to preserve their positions.

[4] <b>From old materials, etc.</b> This applies specifically when their form was not changed. Olas Tamid, end of siman 153. Magen Avraham likewise writes that if one changed their form and made a different utensil from them, it is permitted, for the laver was made from the mirrors of the women who assembled. This proof is difficult, because there the reason, as Rashi explains at the end of Vayakhel, was that those mirrors were more beloved than all the other donations, since through them the women established many hosts of Israel. Ramban, Gur Aryeh, and Divrei David further explain that they were polished copper, and even after being made into the laver their appearance did not change. See there. Magen Avraham would have done better to prove the point from the nose-rings and earrings mentioned earlier. Shiyarei Knesses HaGedolah writes that the restriction applies specifically to accessories of sanctity, but accessories of a mitzvah, such as a bag for a tallis, are permitted. Beis Hillel, siman 139, writes that even the tallis itself is permitted, as is also implied by Responsa Bach, siman 17. Taz in Yoreh Deah, siman 282, cites Responsa Maharil as ruling that accessories of a Sefer Torah should not be made from a garment used by an ordinary person, deriving this from the exposition concerning the altar, and that those who do so should be protested. I examined the responsum itself, siman 122, and it does not say that one should protest. Rather, it states: “Although people commonly make them from old garments, Mar bar Ravina did not sign his approval of them. When asked, I tell them to purchase new garments for this purpose; therefore, new is preferable.” This implies that one should not protest. When asked, one tells them not to purchase old garments, but if they already purchased them perhaps they are permitted. His conclusion, “new is preferable,” likewise implies that old garments are not forbidden. It appears that for this reason Levush included the case in his first clause together with a torn cloth, where there is no prohibition. He was also precise in writing that the rule was supported by a verse, rather than using Maharil’s expression that it was derived from the verse, since it is only an asmachta. This will be explained further at the end of siman 153. Taz there writes that the restriction applies only to the accessory of sanctity itself, while a secondary accessory mentioned in siman 154 may be made from old materials. Magen Avraham objects that our paroches is a secondary accessory, yet Maharil forbids making it from old materials. Perhaps in Maharil’s time they would sometimes place the Sefer Torah on the paroches, as Shiyarei Knesses HaGedolah writes, making it a direct accessory. However, Mordechai’s explanation in the name of Ri is difficult. He writes that although regarding a tallis we would not forbid a garment that an ordinary person had used, and so forth. This implies that in matters of sanctity even a secondary accessory is forbidden. Perhaps he mentioned a tallis only to teach the greater novelty in the latter clause, that one made by non-Jews is forbidden. If an accessory of sanctity was already made from such material, it is forbidden afterward to make from it a secondary accessory. Although we are stringent not to make from it a direct accessory of sanctity, we do not therefore say leniently that no sanctity ever took effect upon it. See Responsa Bach there. Taz there, seif katan 8, writes that if one says, “This money is for an accessory of a Sefer Torah,” the rule that a declaration to sacred use is binding does not apply, since he may sell the item. That rule applies specifically to charity. I found the same in Responsa Maharil there: even if one purchased fabric for a wrapping cloth, he may retract, although he originally vowed to provide a cloth for the Sefer Torah. Nevertheless, he must fulfill his vow by purchasing another mantle. See below, siman 153, seif katan 21.
