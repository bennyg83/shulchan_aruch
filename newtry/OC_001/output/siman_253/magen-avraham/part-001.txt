**** ENGLISH ****
<b>It is permitted for a Jew to remove it.</b> The Darkei Moshe writes: "See the end of siman 311 in the Beit Yosef, where it is stated that it is permitted to remove it." These are the words of the Beit Yosef there: "The Rambam writes regarding a cake placed upon coals. In my humble opinion, even if the coals have not gone out it is permitted, for since the cake is not insulated, removing it does not stoke the coals. Rather, he merely shakes them, which is indirect moving, and is permitted." Accordingly, the same applies to a pot specifically when it is standing upon the coals. The Mordechai at the beginning of the chapter Kirah writes: "It is also possible that the Jew himself may remove it, since he does not shake the coals, has no intention regarding them, and does not move them from their place." If, however, the coals are situated around the pot, the Mordechai in chapter 4 of Shabbat writes that a Jew may not remove it, because he thereby stokes and kindles the upper coals and extinguishes the lower coals, as stated in Keritot. This is also implied by Rashi at the end of the chapter Bameh Madlikin, for at the third shofar blast they removed the pots, which implies that it is forbidden to remove them on Shabbat. On Shabbat morning it is forbidden to remove them except through a non-Jew (Hagahot Maimoniyot, chapter 6). See the end of siman 259; see Tosafot, Yoma 34; and see what I wrote in siman 254, se'if 2.

By contrast, instructing a non-Jew is permitted, since the primary intention is to warm the house, which is permitted, as written at the end of siman 276. Although warming the food is an inevitable result, we are not so stringent regarding instruction to a non-Jew. See the end of se'if 1 and siman 314, end of se'if 1. From this I derive that if a non-Jew put water into a pot fixed in the oven and the water became hot, a Jew may use it to wash dishes. This follows a fortiori: when food is placed on top of the oven, the intention is to warm it, yet it is permitted; all the more so here, where the intention is not to warm the water but to prevent the pot from cracking. It is possible that this is permitted even if the non-Jew added the water after the oven was lit, for the reason just stated. This differs from siman 326, se'ifim 3 and 11, where the intention is to heat the water. See what I wrote in siman 254, unlike the ruling in Tal Orot, which forbids it. A Jew, however, may not put in the water even before the oven is lit, because warming it is an inevitable result. Where the dishes are soiled with fat, it appears to me that one may not pour boiling water over them, as written in siman 320, se'if 14. I found it written in the name of Maharam of Rothenburg that it is permitted because one's intention is only to clean them. It nevertheless appears to me that one should place the utensil into the hot water rather than pour the water over it.

<b>Near the oven.</b> Placing them on the oven itself is forbidden because it is neither swept nor covered. The Rama's wording implies that it is forbidden even to place the food on the oven before it is lit, since fire will later be inside it, and we decree lest one stoke the coals. The common practice, however, is lenient in this regard. See what I wrote in se'if 3, for there is a basis on which they may rely. Keep this general rule in mind: all the permissions mentioned in this siman apply only to hot food. Regarding cold cooked food or other cold items, see siman 318 for which cases are prohibited because of cooking by Torah law.
