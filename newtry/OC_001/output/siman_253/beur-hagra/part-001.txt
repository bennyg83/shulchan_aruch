**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And some say that anything.</b> This follows Chananya there on 37b. The anonymous discussion at the end of the first chapter proceeds according to his view. Furthermore, the Gemara modifies and presses the Mishnah in order to establish it according to him. See Tosafot there, s.v. 'Actually,' and so forth. Moreover, Rabbi Shimon said in the name of Rabbi Yochanan, and Rava supported him, and the Gemara says, 'Both were taught in our Mishnah.' It then answers that he teaches us an inference from the Mishnah. Thus, the Mishnah is precisely consistent with his view. Rav Shmuel bar Yehudah likewise holds this view, except that he teaches the additional point that food whose continued reduction improves it is permitted. Rav Hai explained that this is the novelty: although there is reason to decree because even a slight stoking helps, and one may therefore pay attention and stoke the coals, it is nevertheless permitted. A proof is that Rav forbids food whose continued reduction improves it, yet at the end of the first chapter Rav permits it once it has reached ma'achal ben Derusai. Although Rav and Shmuel disagree with Rabbi Yochanan, the halachah follows Rabbi Yochanan against them, as stated at the beginning of Yom Tov and in chapter 4 of Eruvin. Furthermore, Mar Ukva of Mishan said to Rabbi Elazar, 'You who are near,' and so forth, 'but we,' and so forth. This shows that the halachah follows Rabbi Yochanan, except that those near him had to observe the stringency because of matters that are permitted but which others had treated as forbidden. In Syria they would leave food and eat it. Rav Nachman bar Yitzchak, a master of practical rulings, also acted accordingly, and an incident is authoritative, as stated at the end of chapter 17 of Shabbat. The first opinion maintains that since the Gemara concludes with Rav Nachman's statement, 'The general rule is,' and so forth, this shows that the halachah follows it, certainly where the food was not fully cooked. This is also the force of the Rashba's question, for Chananya states without qualification that anything cooked to the degree of ma'achal ben Derusai is permitted, which implies from that point onward. The discussion at the end of the first chapter is no proof, because there all agree, as the Ran writes there. The proof is as stated above: Rav forbids food whose continued reduction improves it, yet there he permits food cooked to ma'achal ben Derusai. Rava's support for Rav Sheshet is likewise no proof; see the Rosh. Nor is there proof from Rabbi Yochanan, since the halachah does not follow him against Rav and Shmuel, as the Rif writes in several places and as stated at the end of chapter 2 of Avodah Zarah. The words of one do not stand against two.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Se'if 5, it is permitted to place, etc., because this is not the usual manner of cooking, etc.</b> This follows what is stated in the gloss to siman 318, se'if 15: 'There are those who are lenient,' etc. This is the view of the Rashba, who writes in his novellae that placing food opposite a fire or in a first vessel is not forbidden merely because it is cold and uncooked. Rather, warming is prohibited in a place capable of cooking only as a decree lest one cook. Food that was already cooked, however, may be warmed even in a first vessel or opposite a fire, and even in a place where the heat reaches yad soledes bo, provided it is not placed directly upon the fire or directly upon a stove. This is evident from the statement, 'Anything that has already come into hot water,' etc. Although an object heated by fire has the same status as fire itself, and one who cooks with it is liable to bring a sin-offering, as stated in chapter 3 concerning rolling an egg, the Gemara nevertheless says that oil may be heated even to yad soledes bo, because that authority holds that oil is not subject to cooking. Salt likewise may be placed even in a first vessel, because it does not cook in a first vessel. It follows that even an entirely uncooked food, if it is difficult to cook, such as meat, may be placed in a first vessel or opposite a fire, even where it will reach yad soledes bo. The general principle is that wherever the item will not become cooked, whether because the prohibition of cooking does not apply to it or because it does not cook under those conditions, it is permitted in a first vessel or opposite a fire. Although the Sages forbade returning a pot to a stove that was not swept, and forbade initially placing it even upon a swept stove, notwithstanding that the food is hot and fully cooked, the reason there is that it appears like cooking, because that is the normal manner of cooking. The same applies to placing it directly upon a fire. A first vessel or a location opposite a fire, however, is not normally used by people for cooking in most instances, so the act does not appear like cooking but merely like removing the chill. It is forbidden only where the food will actually become cooked. From all this follows the ruling that one may place it upon another pot, etc., because this is not the usual manner of cooking and is no different from placing it beside a hot-water urn or opposite a fire. The first opinion in the Shulchan Aruch there, which forbids reheating a food containing liquid once it has cooled, is the view of the Rosh. He explains the Mishnah's statement, 'Anything that has already come into hot water,' etc., as referring specifically to dry food, and he derives this from the phrase, 'a decree lest it boil,' following Rashi's explanation. This presents no difficulty for the Rashba, for it may refer to bringing the food to a boil directly over a fire or upon a stove. The ruling there in se'if 4 concerning a cooked dish is likewise stated according to the Rosh, as explained above, but the proof is not conclusive.

<b>ומבערת אח"כ התנור.</b> דאין מתכוין בשביל הקדירה ואע"ג דפסיק רישא הוא מ"מ אינו אסור אלא מדרבנן כמ"ש תוס' בכריתות כ"ב וביומא ל"ד ב' והוי שבות דשבות ואע"ג דאף דבדרבנן אסור פסיק רישא כמ"ש בביצה ל"ו ותסברא כו' ואף ע"ג דאין במינו ניצוד ובשבת ק"כ ב' בהא אפי' ר"ש מודה כו' וכן אמירה אסור אף באיסור דרבנן כנ"ל ואפי' עשאו מעצמו כנ"ל מהא דביצה וכן מנכרי שבא לכבות שם קכ"א א' דכותה גבי נכרי כו' מ"מ אמירה בפסיק רישא מותר ששניהם איסורין קלין. תה"ד. וצ"ע דפסיק רישא דוקא בדלא ניחא אבל בדניחא אסור מדאורייתא מדאמרינן ק"ג א' והא אביי ורבא כו' ופריך שתהא חייב ושם קל"ג רבא אמר אפי' תימא כו' וגם מה שחילק האיסורין דוחק גדול. ונראה שגם רמ"א לא סמך עצמו ע"ז ואין מתיר רק בכה"ג שלא נצטנן לגמרי וז"ש לכן נוהגין כו' ר"ל בכה"ג:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And she subsequently kindles the oven.</b> Her intention in kindling the oven is not for the sake of the pot. Although the resulting heating is inevitable, it is only rabbinically forbidden, as Tosafot write in Kerisus 22b and Yoma 34b, and it therefore constitutes a double rabbinic restriction. It is true that an inevitable result is forbidden even in a rabbinic prohibition, as indicated in Beitzah 36b, and even though trapping that species is not ordinarily intended, and likewise in Shabbos 120b, where the Gemara states that even Rabbi Shimon concedes in such a case. Instructing a non-Jew is also forbidden with respect to a rabbinic prohibition, as explained above, and even when the non-Jew acts on his own, as shown by the passage in Beitzah and by the case of a non-Jew who comes to extinguish a fire in Shabbos 121a. Nevertheless, instructing a non-Jew to perform an act whose prohibited result is merely inevitable is permitted, because both elements of the prohibition are lenient. Terumas HaDeshen. This requires study, for the category of an inevitable result applies specifically where the result is not desired, whereas if the result is desired the act is forbidden by Torah law, as follows from Shabbos 103a, where the Gemara invokes the principle of Abaye and Rava and asks that he should be liable, and from Shabbos 133b, where Rava says, 'Even if you say,' etc. The distinction drawn between these prohibitions is also highly strained. It appears that the Rama did not rely upon this rationale alone, and permitted only where the food had not cooled completely. This is the meaning of his words, 'Therefore the custom is,' etc., namely, in such a case.
