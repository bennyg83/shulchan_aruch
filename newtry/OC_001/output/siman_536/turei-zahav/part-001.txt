**** ENGLISH ****
<b>One may not set, etc.</b> In the Mishnah we say: one may set dovecotes for chickens on the fourteenth of Nisan; and a hen that fled, one returns her to her place; and if she died, one sets another in her place. And we ask: now that we permit setting, must one ask about returning? Abaye said: the latter clause brings us to Chol HaMoed. Rashi explained: the latter clause, which teaches "one returns," is on Chol HaMoed because of loss of eggs, for a matter of impending loss is permitted. And the Tur did not mention that if she died one sets another in her place, only the law of one that fled. And Beis Yosef answered that he explains Abaye's words "the latter clause" as referring to the fled case alone, which concerns Chol HaMoed; but the end of the latter clause, namely "and if she died," applies to the fourteenth of Nisan, for the whole chapter concerns the fourteenth of Nisan, and whatever we can interpret regarding the fourteenth we so interpret. And if so, this is the meaning of the Mishnah: one may set dovecotes on the fourteenth of Nisan, and on Chol HaMoed one may not set ab initio, but one may return; and on the fourteenth of Nisan, since one may set, it goes without saying that if she died one returns; but on Chol HaMoed, no; and even though it is a matter of impending loss, since the eggs are ruined, nevertheless since the exertion is great it is forbidden, like after three days of her rebelling, when it is forbidden to return them because her heat has fled from her and there is exertion in returning her; and all the more so if she died one may not set another. Therefore our master the Tur did not write here that if she died one sets another, because that is not taught regarding the moed but regarding the fourteenth of Nisan; and he did not write it regarding the fourteenth because he wrote there that one may set ab initio, all the more so if she died one sets another. And if you say: still there is a difficulty regarding the fourteenth: now that we permit setting ab initio, if she died one sets another, is that not obvious? One may answer that initially this was not difficult to him in the sense of "this, and it goes without saying that," for in every place we say that it teaches "this, and it goes without saying that"; rather, because these three clauses are not equal: the first and middle are taught with "it goes without saying," while the "if she died" clause is "not only this, but even that"; but after we established the middle clause on Chol HaMoed, the first clause and the end of the latter regarding the fourteenth follow the pattern of "this, and it goes without saying that." So far is Beis Yosef's explanation. And it is very puzzling to me: for if so, the difficulty against the Tur is even greater: why did he not write regarding Chol HaMoed that if she died it is forbidden with another, lest we err and permit as the plain reading of the Mishnah? And in the place where the Tur wrote "one may not set a hen, etc.," which is the more stringent case, he should have written the novelty that if she died one may not set another in her place; or at least he should have written "and not only that, but even if she died one sets another in her place." And further it is difficult: since the Tanna of the Mishnah needed to say regarding the fourteenth "and it goes without saying that if she died one sets another," why did the Tur not also write that "it goes without saying" regarding the fourteenth? For certainly the Tanna is more precise and careful about surplus language than the Tur. And further it is difficult against the Tanna: he should have stated "if she died, one sets," which concerns the fourteenth, before the law of fleeing, which concerns Chol HaMoed, and then the pattern of "it goes without saying" regarding the fourteenth would be in order; why did he interrupt between them with the Chol HaMoed law? Therefore it appears to me that the Tur explains that the "if she died" clause also concerns Chol HaMoed, and he holds that the fled case is more reasonably forbidden than the died case: for when she fled we see that she was there and it was not pleasing to her, so whenever you return her it is against her will, and there is more exertion in returning her than when she died and one sets another, where it is not clear to us that it is not pleasing to her. And there are three levels here: setting ab initio is the most stringent, for there is great exertion and no loss at all; lighter than that is when she fled and one returns her, for the exertion is not so great, since the eggs have already warmed somewhat and it is pleasing to her to return upon them, and there is also some loss; lighter still is when she died and one sets another, for here too the exertion is not so great since they have already warmed, and we do not know that it is not pleasing to her, and there is loss of eggs. And if so, the Tanna well said regarding Chol HaMoed "this, and it goes without saying that": not only when she fled is it permitted, but even if she died one sets another, even though ab initio one may not set. And since the Tur wrote regarding Chol HaMoed that it is permitted when she fled, a fortiori regarding when she died. And one need not ask why the Tanna employed that "it goes without saying": the Tanna was forced to say it so that you not err and say that the fled clause also applies to the fourteenth and is taught in the pattern of "this, and it goes without saying," namely that one may set, and all the more so when she fled one returns; and then the question "now that we permit setting, etc." would not arise, for the Tanna sometimes teaches in that pattern. Therefore the Tanna also stated the "if she died" clause: if you interpret everything regarding the fourteenth, you would have before you two "this, and it goes without saying" teachings, one for fled and one for died, and it would be difficult why he did not state only the second, with the first included; rather, from this we learn that the latter clause applies to Chol HaMoed, and there he said "this, and it goes without saying that" regarding the fled clause and the died clause, and there is only one "it goes without saying" once; so the Tanna taught the died clause only to indicate that the fled clause applies to Chol HaMoed. But the Tur divided them explicitly and wrote the fled law regarding Chol HaMoed, and separated it from the fourteenth law above, and therefore he did not need at all to write the permit that if she died one sets, etc. All this appears correct according to the Tur's view. And if so, what the Rama wrote, that if she died it is forbidden to set another, is not according to the Tur's view, but according to the Rambam, who ruled that on Chol HaMoed all setting is forbidden, whether ab initio or if she died and one would set another, and he permits only when she fled. And one need not ask against his view that every matter of impending loss is permitted on Chol HaMoed: one may answer that with excessive exertion there is no permit, as written in the following siman, even regarding a matter of impending loss.

<b>בתוך ג"י לבריחה. </b> דאז אכתי לא פרח החמימות מהבצים ואין טירחא בחזרה:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Within three days of fleeing.</b> For then the heat has not yet fled from the eggs, and there is no exertion in the return.
