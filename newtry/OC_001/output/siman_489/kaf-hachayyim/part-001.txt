<b>א) [סעיף א'] בליל שני וכו'  </b>ואומרים המעריבים השייכים לליל שני אף אם חל יום ראשון בשבת דבשאר חגים אם חל יום ראשון בשבת אומרים בליל שניה המעריבים של יום ראשון והשניה נדחית מ"מ ביום ב' של פסח אומרים לעולם המעריבים השייכים ליום שני משום בכורו לספירה. לבוש. מ"א ריש הסי' ח"י או' א' חק יוסף א' א' וה"ה שמחת תורה במו"ש אומרים מעריבים השייכים לש"ע אעפ"י שנזכר בהם גשם. והא דאין אומרים מעריבים בשבת משום שאין בקי יבוא להטות. א"א ריש הסי' וכ"ז למנהג אשכנזים והספרדים אין נוהגים בזה.

<b>1) [Se'if 1] On the second night, etc. </b>The Ma'arivos associated with the second night are recited even if the first day fell on Shabbos. On other festivals, if the first day falls on Shabbos, the Ma'arivos for the first day are recited on the second night and those of the second day are displaced. On the second day of Pesach, however, the Ma'arivos associated with the second day are always recited because of the first counting of the omer. Levush; Magen Avraham, at the beginning of the siman; Chok Yaakov, subsection 1; Chok Yosef, subsection 1. The same applies when Simchas Torah falls on the night after Shabbos: the Ma'arivos associated with Shemini Atzeres are recited, although rain is mentioned in them. Ma'arivos are not recited on Shabbos lest an unskilled reader tilt a lamp. Eshel Avraham, at the beginning of the siman. All this follows the Ashkenazic custom. Sephardim do not have this practice.

<b>ב) שם. מתחילין לספור העומר.  </b>הקשה הכ"מ פ"ו דסוכה היאך מברכין על הספירה ביו"ט שני ולא חיישינן לזילותא דיו"ט דהא מה"ט לא מברכין על הסוכה בשמיני ותירץ דהתם אומר על הכוס את יום ש"ע הזה ואם יברך על הסוכה סתרי אהדרי משא"כ כאן. א"נ כיון. דאינן בקיאין בקיבועא דירחא א"כ מדינא א"צ לישב בשמיני בסוכה לכן אין מברכין אבל הכא הוי איפכא דמדינא צריך לספור היום יעו"ש. והב"ד מ"א סק"א. וכ"כ הר"ן בסוף מס' סוכה. ועיין ח"י או' ב' שכתב עוד ישובים אחרים יעו"ש.

<b>2) There. The counting of the omer begins. </b>Kesef Mishneh, chapter 6 of Hilchos Sukkah, asks how a blessing can be recited over counting on the second day of Yom Tov without concern for diminishing the sanctity of Yom Tov. For this reason, no blessing is recited over sitting in the sukkah on Shemini Atzeres. He answers that there one says over the cup, 'This day of Shemini Atzeres,' and reciting a blessing over the sukkah would be self-contradictory, unlike counting. Alternatively, since people outside Eretz Yisrael are not expert in determining the new month, by strict law they need not sit in the sukkah on the eighth day, and therefore no blessing is recited. Here the reverse is true: by strict law, that day must be counted. See there. Magen Avraham, subsection 1, cites this. Ran at the end of Sukkah writes likewise. See Chok Yaakov, subsection 2, who gives additional resolutions.

<b>3) There. The counting begins, etc. </b>Shehecheyanu is not recited over counting the omer because nowadays it is only a remembrance of the Beis HaMikdash. Furthermore, Shehecheyanu is found only with a mitzvah that provides some benefit, such as taking the lulav, which brings joy; sounding the shofar, which brings remembrance before our Father in Heaven; reading the Megillah, because the Merciful One had compassion upon us and redeemed us; and redeeming the firstborn son, where the father recites Shehecheyanu because the child has passed beyond doubtful viability, since a person who survives thirty days is not considered nonviable. Counting the omer, however, recalls no benefit, but only the sorrow of the destruction of the House we desire. Ran at the end of Pesachim in the name of Baal HaMaor. See there. Responsa Rashba, siman 126, writes likewise. Levush explains that the counting is for the sake of Yom Tov, so the Shehecheyanu recited for the festival includes it. See there for another explanation. The later authorities give additional reasons, but there is no need to elaborate.

<b>5) There. The counting begins, etc. </b>Most commentators maintain that counting the omer nowadays is only rabbinic, a remembrance of the Beis HaMikdash, where the omer meal-offering was brought on the sixteenth of Nissan and the count began on the day it was offered. This follows Ameimar in Menachos, chapter Rabbi Yishmael, who says it is merely a remembrance of the Beis HaMikdash and therefore counts days but not weeks. Although other Amoraim there counted both days and weeks, the early authorities explain that they did not disagree with Ameimar about the essential point or maintain that counting nowadays is required under Torah law. Rather, the Jewish people count days and weeks as a mitzvah practice. Rambam, chapter 7 of Hilchos Temidin, and Sefer HaChinuch, however, maintain that counting the omer is required under Torah law even nowadays. According to them, the scholars of Rav Ashi's academy who counted days and weeks disagree with Ameimar: it is not merely a remembrance of the Beis HaMikdash but a Torah obligation, and the law follows them. The language of the Talmud somewhat supports this position. It says, 'Ameimar counted days and did not count weeks; he said it is a remembrance of the Beis HaMikdash.' If all agreed that it was a remembrance, it should have said, 'He did not count weeks because it is a remembrance of the Beis HaMikdash.' By saying, 'He said it is a remembrance,' the Talmud implies this was Ameimar's own view and not an uncontested rule. The authorities make similar inferences elsewhere. Birkei Yosef, subsection 1. Shulchan Aruch HaRav, subsection 2, writes that the primary ruling follows those who maintain that counting nowadays is rabbinic. Chayei Adam, rule 131, subsection 1, writes likewise.

<b>6) There. The counting begins, etc. </b>I have seen meticulous people who, on the second night of Yom Tov, conduct the Seder and recite the blessing over counting the omer only after completing the entire Seder. They give a sound mystical reason, and their words appear correct. Yet the law forbids eating before counting, and throughout the long Seder there is a strong possibility of forgetting to count. Birkei Yosef, subsection 5. Nahar Shalom of Rashash, page 25b in the new edition, however, writes that according to the mystical teaching as well, those outside Eretz Yisrael must recite the blessing over counting immediately after Maariv on the second night of Yom Tov. Counting the omer is a Torah positive commandment, whereas conducting the Seder on this night outside Eretz Yisrael is rabbinic. Each has its own distinct spiritual aspect. Therefore, the blessing over the omer must be recited immediately after Maariv of the second Yom Tov and not delayed until after the Seder, contrary to some mekubalim. Delaying it would give the external forces a hold. See there. Machazik Berachah, subsection 2, cites this. See there.

<b>7) May it </b>be Your will, Havayah, our Elokim and Elokim of our ancestors, that through this counting of ours there be rectified whatever we have damaged in the supernal attributes: greatness, might, beauty, eternity, splendor, 'for all that is in heaven and on earth,' and 'Yours, Havayah, is the kingdom,' each one individually. (This too is not said on the forty-ninth night.) [During these seven complete weeks,] 'upon one stone are seven eyes.' (On the night of Shabbos and Yom Tov, one should not say from 'Pardon' onward.) Pardon and forgive all our iniquities and sins through which we have caused damage from our youth until this very day. Just as You purified the souls of Your people Israel from the defilement of the serpent, the impurity of Egypt, when Israel left Egypt through the power of the mitzvah of counting the omer, so too, in Your abundant mercy and kindness, purify our souls of every impurity and contamination that has attached itself to them, and may we be purified and sanctified with the supernal holiness. Just as the mitzvah of the omer served Israel as a shield and buckler in the days of Gideon (see Midrash Rabbah, parashas Emor, section 28), in the days of Hezekiah, in the days of Ezekiel, and in the days of Mordechai and Esther, to subdue all their enemies, and the waving fought against them, so may it stand for us to subdue all the enemies of our souls and those who seek our harm. <b>8) After </b>the blessing, one should say, 'May the Merciful One restore the service of the Beis HaMikdash to its place, speedily in our days.' Tosafos, Megillah 20b, s.v. kol. One should intend that through the blessing he draws down a surrounding light, and through saying 'May the Merciful One, etc.,' which corresponds to the actual service performed in the Beis HaMikdash, he draws down an inner light. He should then recite the entire Ana BeKoach prayer each night, followed by the entire chapter of Tehillim beginning 'May Elokim be gracious to us and bless us,' while standing each night. When reciting the chapter, he should direct his intention each night to one word of the chapter: on the first night to the word 'Elokim,' on the second night to 'be gracious to us,' and so forth through the completion of forty-nine days. Likewise, each night he should direct his intention to one letter of the verse 'Nations will rejoice and sing joyously, etc.': on the first night to the yud of 'will rejoice,' on the second night to the shin of 'will rejoice,' and so forth through the completion of forty-nine days. Similarly, when reciting Ana BeKoach, etc., each night he should direct his intention to the initial of one word: on the first night to the alef of Ana, on the second night to the beis of BeKoach, and so forth through the completion of six days. On the seventh night, he should direct his intention to the initials of the entire verse, namely Abag Yatatz. The same pattern applies in the second week: on the first night of the second week, he should direct his intention to the kuf, the initial of Kabel; on the second night, to the reish of Rinas; and so forth. On the seventh night, he should direct his intention to the initials of the entire verse, namely Kera Satan. This pattern continues through the completion of the seven weeks. All this is explained in Shaar HaKavanos, discourse 11 on counting the omer, and Pri Etz Chaim, gate 22, chapters 3 and 5. See there. See also below, subsection 106; above, siman 1, subsection 22; and below, siman 494, subsection 8. See there.

<b>9) There. The counting begins, etc. </b>Women are exempt from counting because it is a time-bound positive commandment. Rambam, chapter 7 of Hilchos Temidin UMusafin, law 24. The sacred Zohar, parashas Tetzaveh, page 183a, writes likewise, as does Shaar HaKavanos, page 85c. Magen Avraham, subsection 1. Magen Avraham writes there, however, that women have already accepted it upon themselves as an obligation. Chok Yaakov, subsection 3; Eliyah Rabbah, subsection 2; Chok Yosef, subsection 3; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, subsection 2, write likewise. Nevertheless, Maharshal Shaar Aryeh in manuscript and Mahari Lingo in manuscript objected to women counting. Birkei Yosef, subsection 22, cites them and writes that if women follow Rabbeinu Tam by reciting a blessing over the lulav and similar mitzvos, they should follow their usual practice with counting as well. Shulchan Aruch HaRav writes there that there is no concern of a prohibition when they recite the blessing, although they are exempt. Sefer Shulchan Shlomo, however, writes that in any event they should not recite the blessing, because they will certainly make a mistake on one day, and most of them do not understand the meaning of the words. See there. Mishnah Berurah, subsection 3, cites this. All this follows the Ashkenazic custom, under which women recite blessings over time-bound positive commandments, as stated below, siman 589, se'if 6, in the gloss. See there. Sephardic women, however, may not recite the blessing in any event if they wish to fulfill the mitzvah of counting the omer. If possible, they should hear the blessing from men, with both parties intending that the women fulfill their obligation, and then the women should count.
