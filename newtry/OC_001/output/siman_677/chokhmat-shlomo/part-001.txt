<b>There, section 2.</b> A minor who has reached the age of education must kindle. Note: I was uncertain regarding a minor who became an adult during the days of Chanukah, and a convert who converted during the eight days of Chanukah, whether he is obligated in the remaining lamps or not. And if you say that he is obligated, then if he converted at night after feet have ceased from the marketplace, for we hold that after the fact immersion at night is effective, see Yoreh Deah, siman 268, or in the case of a minor who became an adult then according to those who require exact hours in the years, is he obligated in the lamps of that night or not? Now Magen Avraham wrote in the name of Hagahos Maimoniyos in siman 686 that a convert who converted before sunrise is exempt from the nighttime Megillah reading, because its main mitzvah is by day. It appears that what he wrote, that its main mitzvah is by day, is not needed to exempt him from the nighttime reading, for regarding that, why mention that its main mitzvah is by day? Even if its main mitzvah were by night, or they were equal, he would also be exempt from the nighttime reading in this case, since before sunrise it is day, and the nighttime reading applies then only by way of compensation. Since he was exempt at the time of the obligation, he remains exempt afterward as well, as the authorities wrote regarding an onen and Havdalah. Why then did he need the reason that its main mitzvah is by day? Rather, the intent is for the inference: if its main mitzvah were not by day, they would be exempt by day, since the entire time was not in a state of obligation. But since its main mitzvah is by day, the whole daytime obligation still remains in a state of obligation. His intent is therefore as follows: a convert who converted before sunrise is exempt from the nighttime reading, which implies that from the daytime reading he is obligated, because its main mitzvah is by day. If so, it implies that if its main mitzvah were not by day, he would be exempt even from that of the day. Accordingly, regarding Chanukah, where all the days are equal, it would seem that he is entirely exempt, since the whole time was not in a state of obligation, and at the beginning of the obligation he was exempt. However, it seems that this is not comparable. There, regarding Purim, the miracle occurred before the fourteenth, and the fourteenth was already a day of rest; it is only a remembrance of the miracle of yesterday. Therefore, if its main mitzvah were not by day, and the whole day were not in a state of obligation, they would be exempt from the day. But since we hold that it diminishes the time but does not diminish the miracle, as the Talmud explains, because the miracle exists on every day, here too, in this case, the miracle of each day is a new miracle, and he is obligated to bless over what is to come. Now with this one can understand the dispute of the amoraim concerning the reason of Beis Shammai and Beis Hillel. One opinion holds that the reason of Beis Shammai is corresponding to the days that are coming in, and the reason of Beis Hillel is corresponding to the days that have gone out. Another opinion holds that the reason of Beis Shammai is corresponding to the bulls of the Festival, and the reason of Beis Hillel is because we ascend in holiness and do not descend. At first glance, what practical legal difference is there in this? But according to the above, one may say that there is a practical difference regarding a convert who converted, or a minor who became an adult, in the middle of the days of Chanukah. According to the reason that Beis Shammai corresponds to the days that are coming in, the convert too is obligated to kindle according to the number of Chanukah days that are coming in, like everyone else. But according to Beis Hillel, where it corresponds to those that have gone out, he was not under obligation during the days that have gone out, and therefore the concept of beautification is not applicable to him, and he kindles only one lamp. Or perhaps, since it is not possible to kindle corresponding to those that have gone out, Beis Hillel agrees that he kindles corresponding to those coming in. Specifically where it is possible to kindle corresponding to those gone out does he kindle that way; but where it is not possible, Beis Hillel agrees that he kindles corresponding to those coming in. With this, there will be a distinction between the reasons. According to the other reason, that Beis Shammai corresponds to the bulls of the Festival, this is understandable if he began while obligated, for then he resembles the bulls of the Festival, since he kindled the entire number of the obligation. But if he began in the middle and did not kindle the whole obligation at all, he does not resemble the bulls of the Festival, and then Beis Shammai would agree that there is no beautification for him at all. Beis Hillel holds that we ascend in holiness and do not descend, and therefore he too kindles corresponding to those that have gone out, even though he was not then under obligation, in order to be ascending in holiness. There will be a major practical difference between the reasons. According to the reason of corresponding to those that have gone out or those coming in, then for Beis Shammai a convert who converted and a minor who became an adult kindle like everyone else, corresponding to those coming in. For Beis Hillel, where it corresponds to those gone out, this is not applicable to a convert; according to this he holds that there is no beautification for him at all, or that he kindles corresponding to those coming in. But according to the other reason, corresponding to the bulls of the Festival and ascending in holiness, the matter is reversed: according to Beis Shammai, a convert or minor who became obligated in the middle has no beautification at all, since he does not resemble the bulls of the Festival; but according to Beis Hillel, even in this case he kindles like everyone else, in order to ascend in holiness. This seems to me in my humble opinion. With this it is well understood that Rif and Rosh quoted the dispute about the reasons of Beis Shammai and Beis Hillel. At first glance, there is no practical legal difference. According to the above, there is indeed a practical legal difference. However, since it is so that a convert who converted in the middle and a minor who became an adult in the middle are obligated, one must examine if he converted, or became an adult according to those who require exact hours in the years, after feet have ceased from the marketplace, whether he is obligated that night or not. At first glance it would seem that since the main time is only until feet cease from the marketplace, and afterward it is by way of compensation, if at the time of his main obligation he was exempt, he is then obligated to kindle afterward? Rather, it would be like the case there of Megillah, where he is exempt from the nighttime reading, and like an onen regarding Havdalah. But according to this, we are left with one difficulty: in chapter 2 of Shabbos, the Talmud asks, if it went out he is not required to tend to it, but did the master not say that its mitzvah is until feet cease from the marketplace? It answers: this means that if he did not yet kindle, he kindles; alternatively, it refers to the measure. Why did it not answer that the practical difference is a convert who converted after the time when feet ceased, etc., who is exempt from that night's lighting because it is only compensation? But any other person who did not kindle does kindle afterward as compensation. Likewise, for the measure too we do not need it, and the only practical difference is that afterward it is only compensation, and a convert who converted or a minor who became adult then is exempt from that night, according to those who require exact hours. This requires examination.

<b>There, section 3.</b> In the gloss: and even if he lives among Jews and sees the lamps, if he wants to be stringent upon himself, etc. Note: see Turei Zahav, who wrote at length on this. In my humble opinion, it now seems that there is a clear proof for this according to those who hold that it is forbidden to be stringent, from what we read in the Talmud in Berachos 33: this itself is difficult, etc., until: rather, say as follows, if he recited Havdalah in this and in that, blessings should rest on his head. What is the question? Let us say that certainly, to recite Havdalah over the cup and not recite Havdalah in prayer at all, prayer is certainly preferable to the cup. But if he recites Havdalah in prayer and does not intend to fulfill his obligation with that, but only with Havdalah over the cup, then although rabbinic commandments do not require intent, nevertheless if he intends not to fulfill, he certainly has not fulfilled. So it truly is regarding counting in siman 489, where one counts with the congregation without a blessing and intends not to fulfill, and blesses afterward at night. It is proven there in Magen Avraham that even if mitzvos do not require intent, if he intended not to fulfill, he did not fulfill, even though he counted then. If so, here it is proper to say that blessings should rest on his head, since in truth he recited Havdalah in prayer and over the cup, and it is not a blessing in vain, since he intended not to fulfill with the Havdalah in the prayer. Nevertheless he did not alter the text of the prayer, since he mentioned it in the prayer as well, and he is among those who beautify the mitzvah. But if he did not recite Havdalah in prayer at all, one who recites Havdalah over the cup is worse than one who recites Havdalah in prayer and not over the cup. What then is the question? Perforce, this proves like the view of Terumas HaDeshen: since in any event, if he wanted, he could have fulfilled his obligation with the Havdalah in prayer, once he recited Havdalah it is considered a blessing in vain. This is unlike counting, where he cannot fulfill at that time because it is daytime. Examine this, for in my humble opinion this is a clear proof. Now while there, I found this very difficult: what is the question? Perhaps the meaning is that one who recited Havdalah in this and in that means where he first recited Havdalah over the cup and afterward in prayer; in that case it is not a blessing in vain. What then is the question? Perforce, the intent must be that if so, it is an interruption in the prayer. This creates difficulty for the view of Rabbeinu Yonah, brought by Beis Yosef in Orach Chayim, siman 108, that mentioning the event of other days in prayer is not an interruption. He learned this from what we say, that if he recited Havdalah in both, he fulfilled. But here it is compelled that Havdalah is an interruption. Now, if not for the words of Rabbeinu Yonah, it would seem that there is no proof at all from Havdalah. Certainly, where he mentions something from whose very content it is recognizable that it does not belong to the day, it is an interruption, since from within it itself it is recognizable that it is an interruption. But regarding Havdalah on Motzaei Shabbos, if he had not prayed at all, it would be relevant to recite Havdalah in this prayer. If so, from the body of the prayer it is not recognizable that it is an interruption, for it is not known whether he prayed or not. Therefore, whenever it is not recognizable from the thing itself, it is not an interruption. This follows the approach of what Turei Zahav challenged there, though I explained the reason more fully. Yet even according to this, our question remains difficult, for even if he first recited Havdalah over the cup, it is also not recognizable whether he already recited Havdalah over the cup or not. Therefore it seems that the main distinction is that there, prayer draws prayer after it. If it were not the time for prayer, there would be no place to pray as compensation; only because it is a time for prayer does he also pray for compensation. If so, the obligatory prayer of the current time causes the second prayer, and therefore its law is like it, and they are considered like one prayer. There is support for this from what we say elsewhere: once permission has been granted, it has been granted. Therefore it is not an interruption. But if we say that after reciting Havdalah over the cup one need not recite Havdalah in prayer, then he has no Havdalah in prayer at all, and if he recited Havdalah it is indeed an interruption, and the question is proper. But if we say like the view of Rabbeinu Yonah, who holds that anywhere one mentions something related to the event it is not an interruption, even where this reason does not apply, the question is indeed difficult: let it say that the meaning is where he first recited Havdalah over the cup. This requires very great examination, and it proves like Turei Zahav and not like Rabbeinu Yonah; examine it. Regarding the dispute about where one begins kindling, see what I wrote about this in my work on Yoreh Deah, fourth edition, siman 180, at the end, page 162, in explaining the language of the Talmud, so that the mezuzah should be on the right; see there and examine well.

<b>There, section 4.</b> The oil remaining on the eighth day from the oil needed for the measure of lighting, etc. Note: regarding what everyone asked as a contradiction in the words of Shulchan Aruch, see the Chanukah discourse of the year 5601, where I wrote to resolve this by distinguishing between a matter of prohibition and a matter of mitzvah; see there, with Hashem's help. Regarding what everyone asked as a contradiction between the words of Shulchan Aruch here and above, siman 672, Turei Zahav brought in the name of Mahari to answer that here it is speaking where he placed the oil without specification, and above it is speaking where he stipulated that it was only for the measure of lighting. Turei Zahav wondered from the Talmud in chapter Nigmar Hadin, that what is not fit for him is not seized by it; and just as this is so for Abaye regarding designation, the same applies for Rava regarding an act. We already wrote above about this. Now it seems as follows, based on what we already proved in our novellae to the laws of Sukkah, which are still in manuscript, to prove against Hagahos Ashri brought by Magen Avraham, who holds that the sukkah is not forbidden until one sits in it, because designation is not significant. I proved from what is said: if one set aside seven esrogim for seven days, he fulfills with each one and eats it the next day. If we say that designation is not significant in this case, then at twilight it was not muktzeh, and if so there is no muktzeh for half of Shabbos; why should it not be permitted immediately? Perforce, in this case designation is significant. The reason appears to be that since the sukkah is forbidden for benefit by force of the verse, the festival of Sukkos, just as the festival offering is to Hashem, etc., and regarding designation and inherent sanctity, designation is significant, as Magen Avraham wrote in siman 42, so too with the sukkah. The same applies to lulav, since we derive lulav from sukkah; see there. If so, according to this, the same applies to the Chanukah lamp, since it is like the Menorah, as Rashi wrote, beginning with lighting performs the mitzvah: it is like the Menorah. Therefore here too designation is significant, as with inherent sanctity. Therefore it is well understood. Certainly according to Abaye, regarding designation alone, since it is only speech and not an act, we say that what is not fit for him is not seized by it. The same applies to an act according to Rava in cases where designation is not significant: since there is only an act here, we say that for what is fit for him it is considered an act, but for what is not fit for him the act is not considered an act. What is extra is as though it is not there, and in the whole of it no act was done in the extra portion at all. The act on the part that was fit does not work to forbid the part that was not fit. But here with oil, since designation alone also forbids it, then at the time of the act the designation takes effect on all of it, for we did not know whether he would need it or not, and on which part the name leftover would fall. If so, designation takes effect on all of it. Then, if afterward he also performs an act, since here there is both an act and designation, all of it is forbidden. In this case, the act on part of it is effective for all of it, because in any event he designated all of it for this, and it becomes like one body; the act on part of it is effective for all of it because it became one through designation. Designation is effective at least to make it one matter and not a separate matter. Then, since he performed an act with part of it that was fit for him, and that was a strong act, this is effective for all of it and all of it becomes forbidden. In this everyone agrees, and the words of the Mechaber are well understood. According to this, there is no need to say that there it is speaking where he stipulated. Rather, even without stipulation, there it is speaking where he did not designate it first, and it has only an act; therefore it follows the reasoning of Turei Zahav, that what is not fit for him is not seized by it. But here it is speaking where he first designated all of it and afterward kindled it. With designation together with the act, all of it is forbidden. Therefore the She'iltos, which is the source of this law, and all the authorities, were precise and concluded, because it was set aside for its mitzvah. Why do I need all this? This is already known, that for this reason it was forbidden initially. Rather, the intent is to teach that he set it aside initially for this, so it was also designation. Therefore it concluded, because he set it aside for its mitzvah from the beginning, and this is designation together with the act; and it is well understood. Examine this, for so my view now inclines. There is support for the above distinction from what we hold in Choshen Mishpat, siman 275, section 7, and in Sema there, that when one takes possession of the property of a convert who died without heirs, he acquires only as far as a team of oxen goes and returns. But if it is called by the name of the convert, he acquires all of it; see there. If so, here too, if designation is significant and all of it is called the Chanukah lamp, the subsequent act with part of the oil that burned is effective, and it is as if an act was done with all the oil, so there is a strong act here.
