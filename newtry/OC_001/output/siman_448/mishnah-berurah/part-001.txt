(ב) של א"י - וה"ה של הפקר דכיון שלא נעשה איסור בשהייתו לא הטילו חכמים איסור על אכילתו וע"כ גר שמת קודם פסח והניח חמץ כל הקודם וזוכה בו אחר פסח מותר לו החמץ אף באכילה. ישראל שיש לו שותפות עם הנכרי בחמץ ועבר עליו הפסח וחלק עצמו עם הנכרי אחר הפסח חלקו של הנכרי אח"פ מותר בהנאה דבדרבנן אמרינן יש ברירה והוברר הדבר למפרע שהוא של עכו"ם ועיין במקור חיים דמצדד דוקא כשהחמץ עמד בבית נכרי (ואם עמד בבית ישראל תליא בפלוגתא ע"ש) ואפשר דבזה אפילו באכילה מותר ע"ש וחלקו של ישראל אסור בהנאה וה"ה שני ישראלים שיש להם שותפות ומכר אחד מהן חלקו לעכו"ם מותר החלק שנפל לאותו ישראל שמכר חלקו וחלק של ישראל אסור:
**** ENGLISH ****

<b>(2) Belonging to a non Jew.</b> The same applies to ownerless chametz. Since no prohibition was violated by retaining it, the Sages did not prohibit eating it. Therefore, if a convert died before Pesach and left chametz, whoever first acquires it after Pesach may even eat it. If a Jew and a non Jew jointly owned chametz through Pesach and divided it after Pesach, the portion assigned to the non Jew is permitted for benefit, because in a rabbinic matter we apply bereirah and regard it as clarified retroactively that this portion belonged to the non Jew. Mekor Chayim inclines to limit this to chametz that remained in the non Jew’s house; if it remained in the Jew’s house, the matter depends upon a dispute. It is possible that in the permitted case it may even be eaten. The Jew’s share is forbidden for benefit. The same law applies when two Jews jointly owned chametz and one sold his share to a non Jew. The portion that falls to the Jew who sold his share is permitted, while the other Jew’s portion is prohibited.

**** ENGLISH ****
[3] (9) <b>Or under unavoidable circumstances.</b> This means that he knew about it but was under unavoidable circumstances and could not destroy it. Even though in this case he did not transgress bal yera'eh, nevertheless we penalize permitted cases because of prohibited ones, for if we permit it to him, he may come to leave it intentionally ab initio and transgress regarding it. See Biur Halacha. Beis Hillel in Yoreh De'ah, siman 132, wrote: I was asked about a Jew who before Pesach had grain at a non-Jew's mill to grind, and the grain was delayed at the mill until Chol HaMoed of Pesach. When the seventh day of Pesach came, the non-Jew went and ground the Jew's grain, made bread from the flour, baked the bread, and brought the bread to the Jew immediately after Pesach. I answered that it is permitted to take payment for that bread from the non-Jew, and that non-Jew should eat it or sell it to a non-Jew; see there. Chok Yaakov agreed that it is permitted even for eating, because perhaps the non-Jew exchanged the grain, so it is only doubtful chametz that remained over Pesach (see below, siman 449). Beis Meir inclines to permit eating it for another reason: although the Mechaber ruled that whether inadvertent or unavoidable it is forbidden, not all unavoidable circumstances are equal. In such an unavoidable case, where he could not at all have considered that the non-Jew would bake his flour into chametz, the sages certainly did not penalize him; see there. See in Biur Halacha what I wrote in the name of Radbaz. If a Jew left wheat in a mill and a non-Jew came and told the Jew that he had soaked and ground it, see Chok Yaakov in the name of Chinuch Beis Yehudah, that there are many doubts here for leniency. Pri Megadim questions this where it is the practice of the local people to soak wheat before grinding, and he inclines that the non-Jew should exchange it for other wheat.

**** ENGLISH ****
[3] (12) <b>Outside the house.</b> The later authorities wrote that there is no concern about the non-Jew himself, whether he lives in his house or not. Rather, the main concern is that the non-Jew remove the chametz from the Jew's house. As for the non-Jew himself, even if he is the Jew's servant and lives in his house, we have no concern (unless he is permanently acquired by him, for then his hand is like his master's hand, and selling to him is ineffective, since the item has not left his domain through this). As to why they required here that the chametz be removed from his domain, although he has already sold it and it is merely with him as a deposit without responsibility, and in general in such a case we require only a partition, as above in siman 440, Chok Yaakov wrote that here too the law is so. They required here that he remove it from his domain only so that the non-Jew acquire the chametz by pulling; and indeed, if the non-Jew already removed the chametz and acquired it by pulling to his house or to a side alley according to law, he may afterward bring the chametz into the Jew's house, and a partition of ten handbreadths alone is enough. But several later authorities wrote that here it is worse, for since the non-Jew knows that the main chametz belongs to the Jew and will not touch it, even though he acquired it, if it remains in the Jew's house it appears like the Jew's chametz. There is also concern that he may come to eat it, because since it was originally his, he does not keep away from it. According to this view, even if he designated a separate corner in his house or made a partition of ten handbreadths, it also does not help. <small>All this, however, is only ab initio. After the fact, it is obvious that one should not forbid it at all, and even if he did not designate a place for it, as long as the sale was according to law.</small> The later authorities wrote that if he has much chametz and it is impossible to remove it from his house, he should also sell him the room, and it is like selling it to him outside his house. Acquisition of the room is by money together with a document. <small>If he fears giving him a document lest he decide to take possession of the chametz, and there is great loss, he should stipulate with him that he acquire it with money alone without a document, and such a stipulation is effective according to several poskim; one may rely on this leniently in such a case.</small> He must hand him the key to the room in which the chametz is located so that he can enter whenever he wants and take his chametz that was sold or given to him. Or at least the Jew should say to him: whenever you want, you can take the key to your room and enter it to take your chametz. It is an absolute prohibition to leave any seal or lock on the room or on the chametz so that the non-Jew cannot take the chametz, for this proves that the whole sale was merely evasion. If he did so from the outset, at the time he sold it, the chametz is forbidden even after the fact, because the non-Jew did not rely on the sale. Nevertheless, if at first he sold it according to law and afterward placed a lock on the room or a seal on the chametz, although it is forbidden to do so, the chametz is not forbidden because of what he did improperly after the sale. See Biur Halacha, where we wrote in the name of Pri Megadim and Chayei Adam distinctions in this law.

[3] (19) <b>For a small amount.</b> There is no concern for voiding the sale because of underpricing, for he himself knows that it is worth more and agrees to transfer it to him so that it not remain in his domain and become forbidden for benefit. It is better for him to sell it to his acquaintances, because in that case presumably the non-Jew will return it to him after Pesach. See Biur Halacha regarding our present custom. The later authorities wrote that although ab initio one should sell with money and pulling, nevertheless, if there is much chametz and it is impossible to pull all the chametz, and likewise if the chametz is not here so that the non-Jew can pull it, but is in another city or on the road, he should transfer it to the non-Jew through other acquisitions, such as through a mark, where in some places the custom is that the buyer marks the acquired item so that he has a known sign on it, and through this he acquires it, or through a handshake, called "tzu shlag," meaning that they strike their palms against one another, or through giving a perutah in places where the custom is that when the buyer gives a perutah to the seller the sale is completed, or in a place where the custom is that handing over the key completes the sale. <small>In any case, however, he must say, "I hereby sell you such-and-such chametz for such-and-such sum," unlike what the masses say, "I hereby sell you the key," for that is not considered anything.</small> So too, anything like this, each place according to the merchants' custom, is considered an acquisition according to Torah law, and one may properly transfer the chametz through such an acquisition as well. Or he may sell the chametz through agav, meaning that he should sell the non-Jew land or a room, or rent it to him, and together with it say to him, "Acquire the chametz that I have in the place of the land, or wherever it is," for through agav he acquires even if the items are not piled there. The land is acquired by the non-Jew with money and a document, and for rental, money alone is enough. Even if the non-Jew does not give him all the money, but only a few coins, called "auf gab," that is enough, and the Jew should stipulate with him that with this auf gab he acquires the room and the chametz, while the rest of the money is recorded as a loan. They wrote further that if the house where the chametz is located is not his, but is rented by him, then his sale is ineffective, for how can he sell something that is not his? Rather, he should rent that room to the non-Jew, and rent it to him without specification, so that he may keep his vessels and movable goods there, and he should not explicitly say that he is renting it to place chametz inside it, as below in siman 450. A Jew who bought chametz from a non-Jew and gave money but has not yet pulled it: although ab initio he certainly should sell it before Pesach, for according to several poskim money acquires between a Jew and a non-Jew, nevertheless, if he did not sell it and Pesach passed over it, it is permitted, for according to most poskim he acquires only by pulling. But if he pulled it, even if he did not give money, and Pesach passed over it, one should forbid it. However, if according to the merchants' custom acquisition is made with money, Pri Megadim inclines to be stringent even in the first case; see Sha'arei Teshuvah, se'if katan 9.

[4] (24) <b>On condition.</b> This refers to where he said it in the language of "if," meaning: if you do such-and-such during Pesach or after Pesach, the chametz is given to you or sold to you. As long as the condition has not been fulfilled, the chametz is not yet sold or given, and therefore he transgresses bal yera'eh. But if he said to him, "I hereby give it to you from now, on condition that you do such-and-such," the chametz belongs to the non-Jew immediately if the condition is ultimately fulfilled, and it is forbidden only because of the stringency of chametz, as above at the end of se'if 3. **** ENGLISH ****
[5] (25) <b>Forbidden even though he nullified it.</b> Or made it ownerless, and its prohibition is even for benefit. Even though when he nullified it he does not transgress bal yera'eh, nevertheless the sages were concerned that if we permit it when he nullified it, every person may leave his chametz until after Pesach and say that he made it ownerless before Pesach so that we would permit it to him. Know that several later authorities wrote that even if he also checked according to our custom and chametz was found after Pesach, it is also forbidden for benefit, for they did not distinguish in the matter. Some later authorities are lenient where he checked and nullified it and it was found afterward, for what should he have done? He did everything according to the law. Their view is that at least for benefit one should not forbid it, and in a case of great loss one may rely on them. See Biur Halacha, se'if 3, s.v. "even," and what I wrote there. One who was on a ship or on the road and had chametz with him, and had no non-Jew with whom he could sell it, and he stood and made it ownerless before witnesses: whether he may acquire it again after Pesach and benefit from it is disputed by the later authorities, and most incline to forbid. Nevertheless, in a case of great loss one may rely on the lenient view and permit him to sell it to a non-Jew or exchange it with him for another item. If he was on the road and remembered that he had chametz in his house and had no one to whom he could sell it on the eve of Pesach in the proper manner, and he stood and made the chametz in his house ownerless before witnesses, it appears that he may rely on the lenient view and acquire it again after Pesach and benefit from it. Since the chametz was not in his hand, he could not sell or destroy it according to the sages' enactment; he was therefore obligated by law to make it ownerless so that he not transgress bal yera'eh. If so, we have no reason to penalize him by forbidding his chametz when he acquires it again.

[7] (33) <b>That he feeds it, etc.</b> If he cannot do otherwise, the custom in our country is to sell the animal to the non-Jew according to law before Pesach, using the acquisitions explained in Yoreh De'ah, siman 320, and the animal should be with the non-Jew on Pesach, or he should transfer the animal to him together with the cowshed, and the non-Jew should feed it, and the Jew should have no involvement with them on Pesach, provided that he does not tell him to feed it chametz. All this is where the non-Jew has food of his own. But if he has no food and the Jew sells him, together with his animal, also its food, which is chametz, some are stringent about this because it appears like evasion, and as though the whole sale was only so that the non-Jew would feed it chametz. They wrote that he should do it in this manner: he should sell the food to another non-Jew, and it is permitted to broker for the non-Jew who buys his animals that he can buy malt from such-and-such non-Jew. The custom in our country is that even if he does not feed it actual chametz, but only wants to give his animal grain on Pesach, he also sells it to a non-Jew with the cowshed, and the non-Jew feeds it. For if he does not sell it, he will have to give it a little at a time in a dry place and stand over it while it eats, to destroy what remains so that it not become chametz from its saliva, as explained in siman 466, se'if 2. Regarding milk from an animal that eats chametz, even if it belongs to a non-Jew, the later authorities dispute this. Pri Megadim's view is to permit milk milked after a full twenty-four hours from when it ate chametz, and some are lenient even on the same day if it eats permitted foods in the morning and evening.
