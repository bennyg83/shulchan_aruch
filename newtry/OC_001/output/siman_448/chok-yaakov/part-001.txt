<b>של עכו"ם. </b> ואיתא בירושלמי פרק כל שעה גר שמת והניח חמץ ועבר עליו פסח כל הקודם אחר פסח וזכה בה מותרת לו לד"ה ע"כ ואל תטעה לומר שזה סותר לתלמודא דידן בב"ק דף ק"י בגוזל חמץ הגר ועבר עליו פסח דאסור בהנאה התם מיירי שלא מת הגר אלא שחל עליו איסור חמץ או אפילו מת הגר קודם פסח מ"מ היתה ביד גזלן בשעת איסור חמץ משא"כ במת הגר החמץ נשאר מופקר ועומד דנמצא שאין מי שעובר על בל יראה דשלך אי אתה רואה אבל אתה רואה של הפקר:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Belonging to a non-Jew.</b> The Yerushalmi in the chapter Kol Shaah states: If a convert died and left chametz, and Pesach passed over it, whoever first takes possession of it after Pesach may use it according to all opinions. Do not mistakenly think this contradicts our Talmud in Bava Kamma 110b concerning one who stole a convert’s chametz and Pesach passed over it, where benefit is forbidden. There the convert had not died before the prohibition took effect, or even if he died before Pesach, the chametz was in the thief’s possession when the prohibition began. Here, by contrast, when the convert died, the chametz remained ownerless. No one therefore violated bal yera’eh, for the Torah forbids seeing what is yours, but permits seeing ownerless chametz.

<b>עכו"ם </b> ונ"ל דמי שיש לו שותפות עם עכו"ם בחמץ ועבר עליו פסח אף דחצייה שהוא של ישראל נאסר בהנאה מ"מ כשחולק אחר פסח עם העכו"ם חלקו של עכו"ם מותר בהנא' כדין חמצו ולא אמרי' אין בריר' כיון דחמץ שעבר עליו הפסח הוא רק דרבנן אסור משום קנסא ואין כאן עיקר דאורייתא אמרי' יש ברירה (ובאכילה צ"ע ע"ל ר"ס תס"ז) כדאיתא בפרק משילין (ביצה דף לח ע"א) ועיין בים ש"ש בב"ק פ"ה סי' ל"ב מדיני ברירה אבל חלק ישראל ודאי דאסור דאף חמץ של שותפין אסור בהנאה כמ"ש לעיל ס"ס תמ"ה וע"ש:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>A non-Jew.</b> It appears to me that where a Jew and a non-Jew jointly own chametz that remained over Pesach, the Jew’s half is forbidden in benefit, but after they divide it the non-Jew’s share is permitted in benefit like any chametz belonging to a non-Jew. We do not say that there is no bereirah. Since chametz that remained over Pesach is forbidden only D’Rabbanan as a penalty and has no underlying Torah prohibition at that stage, we apply bereirah. Whether it is permitted for eating requires study; see the beginning of siman 467. This follows Beitzah 38a. See Yam Shel Shlomo, Bava Kamma, chapter 5, siman 32, concerning bereirah. The Jew’s share is certainly forbidden, because even chametz owned jointly is forbidden in benefit, as stated above at the end of siman 445.

<b>אפי' באכילה. </b> כן הוא מוסכם מש"ס וכל הפוסקים אך בטור הביא בשם העיטור שדעתו להחמיר והיינו משום גדר וסייג בעלמא כמו שסיים הטור בעצמו וכ"כ בס' תניא ושבולי לקט כלל ז' סי' ס"ב בשם בעל הדברות להדיא ג"כ כלשון העיטור ומשום גדר בעלמא ונראה שבעל העיטור הוא המחבר בעל הדברות כמ"ש בש"ק דף מ"ט וכן הוא בש"י ששניהם חיבר הריב"א ואף דלדינא מוסכם מכל הפוסקים דאין להחמיר כלל בזה וכ"כ בש"ל עצמו שם מ"מ כתבתי כל זה לפי שראיתי בב"ח שהניח דברי העיטור בתימא מכח כמה סוגיות שבש"ס והט"ז מדחיק ליישב דבריו ובאמת מעיקרא לא קשה מידי:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Even for eating.</b> This is agreed upon by the Gemara and all the authorities. Tur, however, cites Baal HaIttur as stringent. His stringency is merely a protective fence, as Tur himself concludes. Sefer Tanya and Shibbolei HaLeket, klal 7, siman 62, likewise cite Baal HaDibros explicitly in the language of Baal HaIttur and explain it as a mere fence. It appears that Baal HaIttur is the author called Baal HaDibros, as stated in Shabbos Kallah, page 49, and in She’eilos Yaavetz, that Riva authored both. Although all authorities agree in practice that no stringency is required, and Shibbolei HaLeket itself says so there, I have written this because Bach found Baal HaIttur’s words difficult in light of several sugyos, and Taz strained to reconcile them. In truth, there was never a difficulty.

באכילה מטעם דקי"ל חמץ שעבר עליו הפסח אינו אלא מדרבנן משום קנסא אפילו כשהוא ודאי חמץ אבל כשהוא ספק חמץ קי"ל ספיקא דרבנן לקולא. וכאן יש ס"ס דמתהפך חדא שמא לא לתתו ואת"ל דלתתו עדיין יש ספק אם לתתו כ"כ עד שנתחמץ ואת"ל שנתחמץ יש ספק שמא לתתו וטחנו אחר הפסח ומאחר שיש כמה וכמה ספיקות והוא איסור דרבנן אין מקום להחמיר אפילו באכילה. ומשום מסל"ת לא שייך כאן די"ל דכוונתו הוה שיחזיק לו הישראל טובה ע"ז ובכונה **** ENGLISH ****
<b>If a non-Jew brings.</b> Beis Hillel, Yoreh Deah 132:1, relates a case in Hamburg. Before Pesach a Jew left grain at a non-Jew’s mill to be ground. It remained there until Chol HaMoed. On the seventh day of Pesach the non-Jew ground the Jew’s grain, baked bread from the flour, and brought the bread to the Jew immediately after Pesach, believing he had done him a favor. Beis Hillel permitted the Jew to collect the bread’s value from that non-Jew, who could eat it himself or sell it to other non-Jews, because the non-Jew had destroyed the Jew’s property. Although at first glance one might hesitate, since unlike wine, bread may be sold to Jews in places where non-Jewish bread is eaten, and bread baked by a non-Jew from Jewish dough has an independent prohibition under Yoreh Deah 112, the bread could be cut into small pieces as required there. In this case there are additional grounds to permit even eating. According to Rambam, who forbids receiving the value of wine when the non-Jew caused the prohibition unintentionally, that is because the damage is not externally recognizable and a Jew would be exempt. Here, however, the non-Jew took the grain without permission, ground it, and baked it. This is like an artisan who spoiled an entrusted item, who must pay; it is direct and recognizable damage. Before fermentation, the non-Jew acquired the grain through his unauthorized change and damage, even without intending to acquire it, so it ceased to belong to the Jew. Even if the damage and acquisition occurred only after fermentation, the Jew may at least collect damages from this non-Jew, and there is no concern that the Jew is selling chametz to another Jew. There is also support from Demai 3:4: produce deposited with a non-Jew is treated as certainly exchanged for the non-Jew’s own produce. Although Rav Ovadiah of Bartenura distinguishes a non-Jewish miller, where one suspects exchange only with another Jew’s grain, that applies when Jews commonly owned fields and brought grain to non-Jewish millers. In our time, especially on Yom Tov of Pesach itself, this is uncommon, and all the more so where it is known that the miller had no other Jewish grain. Raavad distinguishes between produce in a basket and in sacks, while Rambam treats the matter as doubtful. I wrote in siman 449 that doubtful chametz that remained over Pesach is permitted even for eating. The same therefore applies here if there is at least a doubt whether the non-Jew exchanged the grain. These grounds may also permit a case where a non-Jew stole a Jew’s chametz and Pesach passed over it, though this requires study. Chinuch Beis Yehudah, responsum 11, records a similar incident and permits even eating because chametz after Pesach is only a D’Rabbanan penalty and the case contains several doubts: perhaps the grain was not moistened, perhaps not enough to ferment, and perhaps it was ground after Pesach. Since the prohibition is D’Rabbanan, there is no basis for stringency. The non-Jew’s statement is not accepted merely as innocent conversation, because he may have intended the Jew to appreciate his service.

<b>דורן חמץ. </b> אם עכו"ם נתן או מכר לישראל חטים וכה"ג וקיבלם הישראל ממנו ואחר פסח נודע לו שהיה בתוכו חמץ לכאורה נראה דהחמץ מותר לאחר פסח כדין חמצו של עכו"ם וראיה לזה מהא דאיתא בנדרים דף ל"ה באומר קונם ככרי עליך מי מעל למעול מקבל יכול לומר היתירא בעיתי איסורא לא בעיתי ופירש שם רש"י ור"ן והרא"ש דמעילה בשוגג הוא ואלו הייתי יודע שהיא אסורה לא הייתי מקבלה. אבל המוציא מעות של הקדש בשוגג מעל אף ע"פ שאילו היה יודע שהי' של הקדש לא היה רוצה לזכות בהם כיון שהוא עצמו לקחם כדי לזכות בהם. אבל מה שנתנו לו על ידי אחר יכול לומר אלו הייתי וודע שהוא אסור לא הייתי מקבלה הימנו כי איסורא לא בעינא למקני ונמצא זכיה בטעות ואינו זכייה עכ"ל ואם כן ה"ה בנידון דידן ואע"ג דמסיק הש"ס שם בקונמות שהמקבל מעל לכשיוציא היינו משום דעשה מעשה אח"כ באיסור שמעל בשוגג כשהוציא מה שא"כ כאן שלא עשה שום איסור אחר שקיבלם מידו וע"ש בר"ן וצ"ע:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>A gift of chametz.</b> If a non-Jew gave or sold a Jew wheat or similar produce and the Jew accepted it, and after Pesach it became known that chametz was mixed into it, the chametz appears permitted after Pesach as chametz belonging to a non-Jew. Nedarim 35a states that where one says, “My loaf is forbidden to you by konam,” the recipient can say, “I wanted a permitted item, not a prohibited one.” Rashi, Ran, and Rosh explain that when another person gives him a prohibited object unknowingly, his acquisition is mistaken, because had he known it was prohibited he would not have accepted it. This differs from one who himself spends consecrated money unknowingly, since he actively took it to acquire it. Although the Gemara concludes that the recipient commits me’ilah when he later spends the loaf, that is because he then performs an additional prohibited act unknowingly. Here he did nothing further after receiving the produce. See Ran there. This requires study.

<b>ביום אחרון של פסח. </b> לרבותא נקט יום אחרון דאפילו הכי לא יקבלנו החמץ ממנו וכ"כ בתשו' הרשב"א סי' קע"א אבל באמת ה"ה בימים הראשונים וטוב שדבר בהוה שדרך להביא דורן ביום אחרון מן המנחה ולמעלה כמ"ש הטור בשם העיטור ועיין בתשובת נחלת שבעה שכת' לרבותא נקט יום אחרון דבימים הראשונים כיון דאיסור דאורייתא עליו פשיטא דאמרינן דאיסורא לא ניחא ליה דתקניה עכ"ל ואם הוא בי"ט אחרון אז אין צריך לא כפיית כלי ומחיצה יו"ד כי לא חששו שמא יבא לאכול ממנו בזמן מועט כזו אבל תוך הפסח צריך כפיית כלי או מחיצה יו"ד כמבואר לעיל ונראה דה"ה אם שכח עכו"ם חמצו אצל ישראל ג"כ דינו כמו בדורן דהכא:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>On the final day of Pesach.</b> The ruling mentions the last day to teach that even then the Jew should not accept the chametz. Rashba, responsum 171, writes likewise. In truth, the same applies on the earlier days; the common case was stated, because gifts were ordinarily brought on the last day after Minchah, as Tur writes in the name of Baal HaIttur. Nachalas Shivah explains that the last day is mentioned as a novelty, whereas on the earlier days, when the prohibition is according to the Torah, it is obvious that the Jew does not wish to acquire the prohibited item. On the final Yom Tov one need not cover it with a vessel or erect a ten-handbreadth partition, because there is no concern that he will eat it during such a short interval. During Pesach, however, covering or a partition is required, as explained above. The same law applies where a non-Jew forgot his chametz in the Jew’s home.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Outside the house.</b> Terumas HaDeshen, simanim 119 and 120, means that the chametz should be placed outside the Jew’s house. The later authorities explain that otherwise it resembles a deposit left with the Jew. In my view, the primary reason is simpler: how does the non-Jew acquire the gift without removing the chametz from the Jew’s domain? In a sale as well, according to most authorities a non-Jew acquires movable property through meshichah, not money alone. Therefore, initially the non-Jew should give money and also perform meshichah into an alley or his domain, or the Jew should transfer ownership of a room and, together with the land, the chametz within it. The room should not function merely as the non-Jew’s courtyard, because courtyard acquisition derives from agency and a non-Jew lacks halachic agency. Although acquisition of land ordinarily requires a document, the parties may stipulate that money or chazakah alone will acquire it. After the fact, meshichah alone is effective without money, as in a gift, and money alone may also suffice regarding chametz because any minimally valid acquisition is adequate once the Jew has revealed that he does not wish to retain rights in it. Commercial customs, such as marking the goods, clasping hands, giving a coin, or delivering the key, also acquire where merchants customarily rely upon them, as explained in Choshen Mishpat 201. Merely saying, “I sell you the key,” is ineffective if the chametz itself is not sold. The chametz should be kept where the non-Jew can enter, and the key should be in his possession. If, at the time of sale, the Jew locked it away so that the non-Jew could not reach it, the sale is a mere subterfuge and even after the fact the chametz should be forbidden. If, however, the sale was initially complete and the Jew only afterward added his own lock or seal, this is improper and should be protested, but after the fact the chametz is not forbidden. The non-Jew may remove the lock whenever he wishes because he lawfully purchased the chametz. If he refuses to return it after Pesach, the Jew may not sue him in their courts, though the Jew may repurchase it from him after Pesach. A Jew who sells chametz to another Jew who openly transgresses may, in a case of substantial loss, instruct the transgressor to exchange it with or sell it to a non-Jew, and the money is permitted. One should therefore avoid purchasing beer or spirits immediately after Pesach from a Jewish public transgressor, because it may be chametz that remained over Pesach. The presumption that transgressors exchange prohibited chametz for permitted chametz applies only to one who sins for appetite, not one who sins defiantly. See Magen Avraham regarding agency from one non-Jew to another, and Beis Shmuel at the end of siman 5 and Yoreh Deah, siman 164, concerning acquisition by one non-Jew for another. The matter requires study.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>It is forbidden even though he nullified it.</b> We are concerned for subterfuge, that he will claim to have nullified it although in fact he did not, since he acted contrary to the enactment of the Sages by neither eliminating the chametz nor selling it to a non-Jew. Although everyone now performs bittul, we nevertheless remain concerned for subterfuge. The same applies even to complete hefker, and even witnesses to the hefker and bittul are ineffective if he knowingly left the chametz in his home and possession throughout Pesach. In such a case, bittul or hefker certainly does not help. If, however, he was unaware of it throughout Pesach and witnesses attest that he nullified or declared it ownerless before Pesach, or there was a complete and known coercive circumstance in the sale, so that throughout Pesach he believed it had been sold and only afterward learned that the sale was ineffective, then if he states that he nullified it we do not suspect subterfuge even without witnesses. At least benefit after Pesach may be permitted. I discussed and established all of this at length in Toras HaShelamim, question 6, contrary to several later authorities who adopted excessive stringencies. See also what I wrote in Soles LaMinchah concerning the glosses to Shaar Ephraim. <b>If</b> a Jew’s chametz was delayed on the road, see Responsa Rabbi Shlomo HaLevi, Orach Chaim, siman 12, who ruled that it should be declared ownerless before a beis din, but remained uncertain in practice whether benefit after Pesach could be permitted. <b>Where</b> the chametz is due to arrive in a city containing Jews and customs charges, carriage fees, or similar expenses must be paid during Pesach, what should be done? Declaring it ownerless is possible, but a sale to a non-Jew for money alone is, as I wrote above, initially ineffective according to most authorities, while meshichah is impossible because the chametz is not present. It appears that he should sell it to the non-Jew for money, stipulating that if it arrives during Pesach the non-Jew will pay the customs and carriage charges and take possession. After the sale he should declare before two people that if the non-Jew does not acquire it through money, it is at least completely ownerless. In this manner, according to all views, it leaves his ownership even initially. See Magen Avraham, who writes that there are additional methods of acquisition even where the chametz is not present.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>That it is chametz.</b> Olas Shabbos writes that after the fact it is permitted, like a cow fattened with idolatrous vetch, as explained in Yoreh Deah 142. This comparison may be inexact. Here the Jew’s animal consumed during Pesach chametz belonging to a non-Jew, from which benefit is forbidden during Pesach, so the Jew benefited from chametz. He should therefore cast away the value of the benefit, meaning the amount he would otherwise have spent feeding the animal. A cow fattened with idolatrous vetch, by contrast, concerns an animal belonging to a non-Jew. Similarly, an animal of a non-Jew fattened on chametz during Pesach is certainly permitted. Here, however, at least the value of the Jew’s benefit should be cast into the sea, or the entire animal should be sold to a non-Jew except for the value of the prohibited benefit, following the law of one who benefits from an item forbidden in benefit. Nevertheless, one who studies the sugya at the end of the chapter Kol HaTzelamim will see that after the fact it is permitted in every respect because two causes produced the result. This requires study.
