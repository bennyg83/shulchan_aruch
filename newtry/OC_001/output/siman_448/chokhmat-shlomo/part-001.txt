**** ENGLISH ****
[1] <b>Se'if 1.</b> "Chametz of a non-Jew over which Pesach passed, etc." Note: I will copy here several novel points that are very necessary as a matter of law, with Hashem's help. First: an incident came before me involving someone who had chametz from which he had to pay the king's tax. Because he did not pay the tax from it according to the law, the matter became known to the king's officers, and they took the chametz from him to the king's customs house. The case came to trial and was uncertain: either all the chametz would be confiscated for the king, or the Jew would prove his righteousness and the judgment would be clarified that the chametz should be returned to him. The questioner then asked what, according to law, he should do on Pesach with this chametz standing in the king's house, and whether a sale to a non-Jew is effective for it. This requires analysis: perhaps a sale to a non-Jew is not effective at all, for we hold that something which is his but not in his possession cannot be sold by him. If so, here too, since it was taken by the king's officers, it is not in his possession and he cannot sell it; nevertheless it is his, and he transgresses bal yera'eh. One should not say that if so, this is like where a non-Jew stole the chametz from him, and where a non-Jew stole chametz from him the Jew does not transgress bal yera'eh, as the later books wrote. That is not so. With stolen chametz, at least responsibility rests on the non-Jew, and if it is lost and the Jew is able to force the non-Jew in court, the non-Jew must pay him. Therefore it is under the non-Jew's responsibility, and therefore the Jew does not transgress bal yera'eh. But here, responsibility does not rest on the king's officers. If it is ruined, even if it is later clarified that the Jew wins the case, they will not be obligated to pay him. If so, it is not under the non-Jew's responsibility, and perhaps in such a case he transgresses bal yera'eh. Nevertheless, perhaps one can be lenient here too through sale, for two reasons. First, what we hold, that something which is his but not in his possession cannot be sold by him, can be said to apply specifically where, for example, the item was stolen from him, because at present, in the robber's view, he wants to rob it from him entirely and does not intend to return it. Therefore it is presently not in his possession. But here, the king's officers do not intend to rob it outright, but to return it to him if he wins the case. If so, either way: if the king wins the chametz, then retroactively it belongs to the king and is not his at all, for the law of the kingdom is law, and he did not transgress bal yera'eh. If the ruling is issued that it belongs to the Jew, then from now their intention is to return it to him, and this is not included in something that is not in his possession. It is better than a pledge, for a pledge has a clear lien on it through the debt, and therefore he cannot sell it; but here there is no lien or clarified debt on it. Therefore it is proper to say this either-way argument. One can say even more. What we hold, that if one stole and the owners did not despair, neither can consecrate it, and the same applies to selling, is learned from the verse, "when a man consecrates his house as holy" - just as his house is in his possession, etc. And this matter, that a Jew's chametz in a non-Jew's domain is not transgressed by bal yera'eh, is also learned from the verse "in your houses" - just as your house is in your possession, etc., excluding a Jew's chametz in a non-Jew's domain, which is his but not in his possession, as is written explicitly in the Mechilta and brought in the early and later authorities. If so, both laws are learned from one word, from the language of "house." Therefore, either way: if this matter in our case is considered in his possession regarding bal yera'eh, it should likewise be considered in his possession regarding sale; and if it is not in his possession regarding sale, it is not in his possession to transgress bal yera'eh. Therefore, either way, it is permitted to sell it to a non-Jew in such a way that the non-Jew will stand in his place to acquire it if he wins it. For extra measure, I gave him a remedy: after the sale he should say before three people that he makes it ownerless and despairs of it completely, accepting the loss of money. Therefore, although from the angle of ownerless designation it would not help if it is not in his possession, for if it is not in his possession to sell it, it is not in his possession to make it ownerless, since their laws are equal, nevertheless it will help through the language of despair. Despair is certainly effective for something not in one's possession, for the essential law of despair is so regarding robbery or theft. Therefore, either way: if it is in his possession, he has already sold it and his sale works; and if it is not in his possession, despair is effective for it and it leaves his domain completely through despair. Therefore, from the angle of despair, two people would suffice, and three are not required; but to satisfy all concerns I instructed him also to make it actually ownerless, according to the law of ownerless designation, which requires three. By this he satisfies all views, with Hashem's help. Even with a pledge in such a case, although the pledger cannot generally sell it, here it is an either-way argument, for biblically he does not transgress bal yera'eh, only rabbinically, because it appears like his. If so, either way he can sell it in this case and it is effective. Examine this, for I have been brief. May Hashem save me from errors and teach us wonders in His Torah. See the later authorities regarding what they concluded in the laws of selling chametz, that ab initio pulling is required, etc.; see there. See Shach, Yoreh De'ah, siman 320, that the money must be given immediately at the time of sale, not afterward. In a responsum on the laws of firstborn written in 5593, I wrote that this means at the time of pulling. But this is specifically if the acquisition is not in the buyer's courtyard. If it was in his courtyard at the time the money was given, it is enough even if he gives the money afterward, at least while it is in his courtyard; see there and examine. Second: regarding the law of selling chametz to a non-Jew, it had been simple in my eyes that there is no need for it to be written in the law books of the king, may his glory be exalted, since it is only to remove a prohibition. But now, when I studied Eruvin 62, where it says that R. Chisda said we require a strong rental and R. Sheshes said a weak rental, etc., and the Talmud concludes: what is a strong rental? With seals and royal officers. Tosafos and the Rosh there explained that this means with the seal of kings and the agents of the officers; see there. If so, it is proven that although the rental is only to remove a prohibition, nevertheless we require a strong rental according to the law of the kingdom. If so, although R. Sheshes holds that a weak rental is enough, perhaps that is specifically regarding eruvin, where many leniencies were made, as the sages said in many places that many leniencies were made in eruvin more than in other rabbinic prohibitions. Also, according to many poskim, eruvin has no root in Torah law; not only according to those who hold that beyond twelve mil is also rabbinic, but even according to those who hold that beyond twelve mil is biblical, nevertheless it is not considered to have a Torah root, as I wrote elsewhere. See Hagahos Ashri at the end of the first chapter of Eruvin; see there. Therefore they were lenient there. But who says to be lenient regarding chametz, whose root is biblical, and where they imposed many stringencies because of the stringency of chametz? Perhaps everyone agrees that a strong rental and sale is required. It is preferable to minimize dispute. Who says to be lenient about this? Therefore it appears that the main view is that it should be written in the courts, and it requires examination why the custom is not so. Examine this. Third: whether an acquisition by admission is effective regarding prohibition, see Ketzos HaChoshen, siman 194, and what I wrote in my large work on the laws of Pesach, siman 448. Now it appears to me that there is a clear proof that admission is effective, from Gittin, chapter 3, page 30b, in the passage there: the sages taught, if a Jew said to a Levi, "I have ma'aser of yours in my possession, etc." Abaye said: this is what it means; see there. That passage is difficult: how can the Levi separate terumas ma'aser when he has not yet acquired it? Secondly, why should the homeowner's courtyard not acquire it for him? The early authorities already struggled with this, as brought in the later commentators and in Tiv Gittin on Gittin, recently printed. In my view, it is settled well. Since he said to him, "I have a kor of ma'aser of yours in my possession," this is an admission that he has ma'aser of his in his possession. Through this admission, it is acquired by him and becomes his, and he can separate it for another place. It is proven from here that admission is effective regarding prohibition and creates an acquisition. This also resolves the second question. Certainly according to Taz in Choshen Mishpat, siman 189, that the courtyard of a depositary does not acquire for him from the depositor, this is well understood: since the Jew admitted that he has ma'aser belonging to the Levi in his possession, it is as though the ma'aser is in the Levi's hand, and therefore the courtyard does not acquire for him. Even according to those who disagree with him, see Ketzos HaChoshen, siman 194, nevertheless here it is settled. One can say that although admission creates acquisition, nevertheless it is ineffective for priestly gifts, since the Talmud says at the beginning of Bava Metzia that exchange is a form of sale and does not work for priestly gifts, because the verse writes "giving" regarding them. But agav karka is merely giving; see there. It is also proven there that giving to his courtyard is also considered giving for priestly gifts. If so, here too, since every Jew is presumed fit, and we are witnesses that he wants to fulfill the mitzvah of giving, although he says to him, "I have a kor of ma'aser of yours in my possession," thereby making an acquisition for the Levi, nevertheless that is a form of sale and does not fulfill the mitzvah of giving. Therefore, we are witnesses that he admits to him in an effective manner, and his intent is that he transfers to him the courtyard where the kor of ma'aser rests. If so, it is as though he gave it to the Levi's courtyard, and through this he fulfills the mitzvah of giving, like the incident of Rabban Gamliel and the elders at the beginning of Bava Metzia. According to this, although generally a depositary's courtyard acquires for him, that is where he did not transfer the place to the depositor, so it remains the depositary's courtyard. But here, where we are witnesses that through this admission he also transferred the place to him, and conversely the Levi sold him only the ma'aser but did not sell him its place, since there is no mitzvah then there is no presumption to say that he went back and transferred the place. He sold him only what he specified, the body of the ma'aser and not the place. If so, the place of the ma'aser remains the Levi's, and therefore it does not acquire the produce for the depositary. This is a great proof, with Hashem's help, to the above law and to the resolution of the passage. Through this one can also resolve the language of the Yerushalmi brought in Sefer Tiv Gittin on Gittin in the above passage. This is the Yerushalmi's language in Terumos, chapter 1: R. Yochanan in the name of R. Yannai: if a Jew said to a Levi, "A kor of ma'aser, etc.," and the Levi went and made it terumas ma'aser for another place, and the Jew went back and gave it to another Levi, he has only a grievance against him. This applies where this one gave it before that one made it terumas ma'aser. But if that one made it terumas ma'aser before this one gave it, is this not like the Mishnah: if before he separated he canceled it, it is canceled; but if after he separated he canceled it, his terumah is terumah? "Is it all from him?" They explain this as an expression of "who gave him the power?" It resolves it according to R. Yosi, for R. Yosi says that if the homeowner separated the ma'aser, what he did is done. This is the end of the language; see there in Tiv Gittin. The commentators' main explanation there, that the Yerushalmi at first means it through the Jew's agency, is not, in my view, its intent. See what he asked, that anything one cannot do oneself one cannot appoint an agent for; and he himself explained that this is indeed the Talmud's challenge there, "is it in his power?" But in my view, the Yerushalmi at first means as follows. It is true that admission creates acquisition, but that is specifically when he admits before witnesses in a manner where there is no claim of jesting or avoiding appearing wealthy. But if he admitted not before witnesses, he can retract, because speech comes and cancels speech. This is the Yerushalmi's intent: if this one gave it before that one made it terumas ma'aser, then since it is only speech, that he admitted to him that he has a kor of ma'aser of his in his possession, he can later retract and say, "I was jesting with you," for speech comes and cancels speech, similar to what is taught regarding an agent: if he canceled before he separated, because speech comes and cancels speech. So too here. But if that one first made it ma'aser and only afterward he gave it away, then an act has already been done, and speech does not come and cancel an act. Therefore it asks: who gave the Levi power to make an act? It is not his, so how does his act help such that the homeowner cannot retract from his speech? It answers according to R. Yosi, that the homeowner can separate ma'aser. If so, here too, as long as the homeowner did not retract, the Levi is doing his agency, and it is as though the homeowner made him an agent, and speech no longer comes and cancels the act. This is well resolved. I also have another proof from what the Talmud says in Gittin, that a slave who married in the presence of his master, or whom he allowed to put on tefillin, goes free; see the Talmud there, 39. This is difficult: one who makes his slave ownerless still requires a bill of emancipation. Here, although it is like an admission, that would be only regarding money; why should it help regarding prohibition? Perforce, admission creates the acquisition even for prohibition and is like writing him a bill of emancipation. Examine this carefully.

[3] <b>There, se'if 3.</b> "Chametz of a Jew over which Pesach passed is forbidden for benefit, etc." Note: I will copy here several observations and novel rulings, with Hashem's help. [1] I was asked about someone who owed money to a woman and did not want to pay her. When the woman pressed him to pay what she was owed, because she needed to get married, he had mercy on her and gave her chametz as a pledge for her debt. But he placed the chametz with a third party on condition that if the match were completed as she said, the third party should give her the chametz, and if the match were not completed, the third party should return the chametz to him. Now the match was not completed and the borrower wants his chametz, but the woman refuses to give it to him, since he truly owes her the money, saying: what do I have to do with his condition? Meanwhile, the eve of Pesach arrived, and the woman wants to sell the chametz to a non-Jew according to our custom, either she or the third party selling it. The owner of the chametz protests against them selling it, saying that the chametz belongs to him. I was asked what, according to law, should be done. This is what I answered briefly. It appears clear that the woman is obligated to sell it, and that her sale is effective according to the law. Although it sounds from Taz in siman 443 that the obligation to destroy it falls on the borrower, that is only when he pledged it at the time of the loan. But when he pledged it not at the time of the loan, where the Talmud explicitly states that a creditor acquires the pledge and can betroth a woman with it and acquire through it all acquisitions, the same applies here: the creditor is obligated to sell it, and if she sells it, her sale is certainly effective. Therefore, if the pledge had not been conditional, her sale would certainly have been effective, and even if the borrower protested, it would not help. Now, although in our case the pledge was under the above condition, nevertheless, either way, the sale of the lender and the third party is effective. If his condition is ineffective because in truth it was hers, then she can seize it against the borrower's will, and it is obvious that her sale is effective. This is the main law, that a creditor acquires a pledge, as written in Shach, Choshen Mishpat, siman 72. If his condition is effective, then since they are obligated to return it, and when they do not want to return it their intent is to rob it, it is like one who sends his hand into a deposit, who also has the law of a robber. Since we hold that where a robber sold, the buyer acquires through despair and change of domain, and according to most poskim there is no difference whether despair came before the change of domain or afterward, here too, since he intends to rob it, and once the time of prohibition arrives the owner despairs, this is despair and change of domain, and therefore his sale to the non-Jew is effective. I later found in Sefer Mekor Chayim that he wrote similarly in our case regarding one who stole chametz and sold it to a non-Jew, that it is effective; see there. The same applies here, that her sale is effective either way, even though the borrower protests that it should not be sold. All this we wrote regarding ab initio. But regarding after the fact, if the borrower later objects that he did not sell it and challenges the woman's sale as unlawful, it appears certain that he is not believed to cause the woman to lose the chametz. Either way: if the woman's sale was lawful, then it is automatically permitted through her sale. If it is as the borrower says, that it was unlawful, then the borrower transgressed bal yera'eh and the obligation to sell it was upon him, as Taz wrote in siman 443; if so, presumably he acted lawfully and sold it. One should not say that his sale is ineffective because the borrower robbed it, and with stolen property where the owners have not despaired neither party can consecrate it. Here it is different. First, he does not intend to rob it outright, but as a pledge; and as a pledge the borrower can sell it, as Taz wrote in siman 443. See what I wrote in my novellae there to explain his words. Furthermore, here it is an either-way case: if we judge it as theft, then the robber's sale is effective because there is despair and change of domain; and if we do not judge it as theft, then the borrower can sell it, as implied by Taz's words. Since the borrower had to sell it, we are witnesses that presumably he sold it beforehand so as not to transgress bal yera'eh. Although he now says that he did not sell it, he is not believed to cause the lender loss, for a person does not render himself wicked, and he is not believed to cause loss to others, as we hold regarding "we were coerced," etc. Furthermore, since Beis Shmuel in Even HaEzer, siman 28, wrote that although with stolen property where the owners have not despaired neither party can consecrate it, nevertheless with the consent of both the consecration takes effect, the same applies to sale. If one does not agree to the other's sale, and his own sale alone would not help, he will transgress bal yera'eh. Therefore we are witnesses that each one consents in his heart to the other's sale so that he not transgress bal yera'eh, and it is as though each sold with the other's consent. Although at first he protests, we say that we are witnesses that afterward he agreed in his heart and afterward consented. One can also say that even at the time of his protest he consented, according to what Rashba wrote in his novellae to Kiddushin 23, brought in Avnei Milu'im, siman 1: where it is a complete benefit, even if he stands and protests explicitly, we do not pay attention to his protest; see there. If so, here too, being saved from transgressing the Torah prohibition of bal yera'eh is a complete benefit for him. Therefore, even though he stands and cries that he does not want to sell it, we do not pay attention to him, and we say that we are witnesses that he wants this. Therefore, in any event, this chametz is certainly permitted, and the borrower is not believed to render himself wicked and thereby cause the loss of his chametz that is in the lender's hands. Examine this; so it appears to me correct as a matter of law. [2] I was also asked regarding our times, where the law of the kingdom is to pay the king, may his glory be exalted, a certain tax on liquor. If one takes the liquor through the customs station outside the country, the king returns that payment. If on Pesach a Jew forgot to sell his liquor, so that the law requires him to destroy it and pour it out, may he keep it in order to take it outside the country so that he can receive back from the king the tax payment that he paid for the liquor? I answered that it appears forbidden. Although this is not considered deriving benefit from chametz, because he is not taking money for the chametz, but rather taking what is his, since the tax was due only if it was sold in this country, and if it is clarified that it was sold outside the country it is revealed retroactively that no payment was due from it and he is taking his own, nevertheless it appears forbidden for another reason: because he wants its continued existence. He wants it to remain in existence until he can bring it outside the country. There is an explicit proof to my words from Avodah Zarah 63: if one hired him to break a barrel of forbidden wine, what is the law? Do we say that since he wants its continued existence it is forbidden? Rashi explained: this Jew wants the barrels to remain in existence, so that they not break by themselves now, until he breaks them and receives his wages, and it is forbidden. Or perhaps, anything done to reduce impurity is proper. The Talmud concludes: anything done to reduce impurity is proper. So too we hold in Shulchan Aruch, Yoreh De'ah, siman 133, that it is permitted because reducing impurity is proper. This implies that if it were not reducing impurity, it would be considered that he wants its continued existence until he breaks it. If so, there it is understandable, for if it is not broken he will not reduce impurity. But here, in the end he will pour it out, and there is no reducing of impurity in this. It is certainly forbidden because he wants its continued existence until he takes it outside the country. This is clear and correct in my humble opinion: he is obligated to pour it out immediately. Examine carefully. [3] I was also asked by the Rav of Kozienice about someone who did not sell his wheat before Pesach, and there were sprouted kernels among them that were recognizable, and it was impossible to sort them out. What is their law? I answered him that I had already shown him, when he was here, the view of Levush, brought in Turei Zahav, siman 632, that even where the prohibition is recognizable, it is nullified biblically, and only rabbinically is it not nullified; Taz wondered greatly about this. But he did not see that R. Ovadiah of Bartenura in tractate Kilayim also explained this way, and Tosafos Yom Tov likewise wondered about him as Taz wondered about Levush. In any event, we have two pillars of the world who hold that even a recognizable prohibition is nullified, and I have already discussed this at length elsewhere, with Hashem's help. We also have the dispute of Rambam and Rashba brought in Beis Yosef, Yoreh De'ah, siman 115, regarding butter of non-Jews. It is proven there that according to Rambam, where the prohibition is recognizable but impossible to sort out, it is not nullified; but according to Rashba, even where the prohibition is recognizable, whenever it is impossible to sort out it is nullified. See Taz, Yoreh De'ah, siman 104, who wrote at length about this, and this is also explained there; see there. According to this, according to Rashba here, where it is impossible to sort out, it is nullified. Therefore, regarding chametz that remained over Pesach, all these sides mentioned above are enough for us to be lenient, that it is nullified in the majority like the law of chametz that remained over Pesach. However, if possible, it is better to sell it to a non-Jew, so that it will be permitted only for benefit and not for eating. If that is impossible, it is permitted to sell it even to a Jew. But during Pesach, one must not rely on this permission; rather, he must sort out whatever possible, throwing the chametz kernels into the sea, or else throwing everything into the sea. So it appears to me correct as a matter of law, and there is no need to elaborate further. Examine this. [4] Regarding chametz found in a room that was sold to a non-Jew together with chametz, where he forgot some chametz and did not sell it, whether it helps to say that the chametz is permitted because the non-Jew acquired it through his courtyard as a found object, see what I wrote incidentally in the omissions to my work Nidrei Zeruzin, part 2, page 107, in a responsum to the holy community of Tarnopol, that the chametz is forbidden for two reasons. First, it was not expected to come, for the non-Jew never considered such a thing. Also, since the sale to the non-Jew was before the time of prohibition, while despair occurs only at the time of prohibition, it entered his possession before despair, and he does not acquire it, as written in Shach, Choshen Mishpat, siman 268; see there. It is also despair without knowledge, which is not despair. The Talmud in Bava Kamma speaks where he knew about it then, and when the time of prohibition arrived, he despaired. We also wrote there that a stipulation that the non-Jew acquire with money is effective even according to those who hold that a stipulation does not help; that is only if the deficiency is in the body of the acquisition. But if the body of the acquisition is an acquisition and the deficiency is only on account of the person, a stipulation certainly helps; see there, with Hashem's help, and examine. [5] Regarding the law of selling chametz together with utensils, whether the utensils require immersion, see my novellae to Even HaEzer, fifth edition, page 38, siman 34, where I wrote about this incidentally in a responsum to the holy community of Nizhniv, that the utensils do not require immersion; see there and examine carefully. [6] If the chametz was digested by a person, we already wrote above in note 1 that the sale is effective. See also what I wrote to explain the matter and its reason in my responsum to Yoreh De'ah, Hilchos Bechor, chapter 9, fifth edition, siman 199, to the holy community of Nizhniv. Regarding whether acquisition by admission is effective, we also wrote there, with Hashem's help; see there and examine. [7] See Magen Avraham, se'if katan 4, who wrote: "And what he wrote, that we follow their laws, is somewhat difficult, for we say in Bechoros, etc." See my work on Yoreh De'ah, siman 320, fifth edition, siman 76, in a responsum to the holy community of Skala, where I wrote to resolve Magen Avraham's difficulty properly in several ways; see there, with Hashem's help, and examine.

[5] <b>There, se'if 5.</b> "Chametz that was found in a Jew's house after Pesach is forbidden, etc." Note: regarding the law if one stole chametz on the eve of Pesach after the sixth hour before nightfall, whether it is permitted for benefit after Pesach, see my work Avodas Avodah on tractate Avodah Zarah, page 72, in Tosafos s.v. "what, was it not given for return," where I wrote there a new side for permission that the earlier authorities did not estimate. There it is explained whether acquisition by pulling applies at all to chametz biblically for a Jew; see there, for its reasoning is good, and examine. Regarding whether it is permitted to sell chametz to a Jew suspected of transgressions, with the buyer then selling it to another, see what I wrote in a responsum to the holy community of Tarnopol in the laws of eruvin, siman 366, incidentally; see there and examine. See there also for discussion and some explanation of the passage at the beginning of Pesachim, regarding one who rents a house to his fellow; see there in the above responsum to the holy community of Tarnopol and examine. It is in my work on Orach Chayim from the year 5596, page 108b. I received the book Responsa Chemdas Shlomo, which the author, the true gaon R. Shlomo Zalman, Av Beis Din of the holy community of Warsaw, may his light shine, sent to me as a gift. I saw in his book, Orach Chayim, siman 1, that he wondered at the later authorities who were lenient several times regarding chametz that remained over Pesach when it was sold with money alone or pulling alone, saying that since there is a dispute whether money acquires for a non-Jew or pulling, it is a rabbinic doubt and lenient. He wondered about this, because in the first chapter of Bava Metzia, in the passage of "if the kohen seized it," it is explicitly proven that if the kohen seized it we remove it from him, for it is considered entirely his and entirely his property. If so, here too, since if the non-Jew seized it we would remove it from him, it is entirely the Jew's property and he transgressed bal yera'eh. He raised this difficulty also about the Talmud in Bava Basra regarding two trees, where he brings and does not recite; see there at length. In my humble opinion this is not difficult at all. Certainly if he nullified it, it bears no comparison to that case. There, if the kohen did not acquire it, it belongs to the Jew, the original owner, and it is proper to follow the prior owner's presumption and the presumption of the one in whose hand it was until seizure; therefore seizure does not help. But with chametz, he nullified it and it became ownerless. If so, at least it is certainly not the seller's. The sages only said that although he nullified it, it is forbidden for benefit as a decree because of one who did not nullify it, and it has no permission unless another acquired it before Pesach. In this, if the non-Jew seized it, he certainly would not need to return it, because at least it certainly does not belong to the owners. The only practical question is whether it is ownerless or whether the non-Jew acquired it. Against ownerless property, certainly if he seized it we do not remove it from him, and the non-Jew would not need to return it, for here there is no prior owner's presumption, and according to everyone, if the kohen seized it, we do not remove it from him. But even if he did not nullify it, it still appears settled. The Talmud states that general rule, that where if he seized we remove it from him it is considered certainly the Jew's, only where the opposing party has no acquisition based on the transferor's intent, for the transferor did not transfer it to him; rather, the Torah granted it to him. In such a case his acquisition is not considered an acquisition. This is similar to what Tosafos wrote at the beginning of Yoma and in Kesubos, that where the majority is Jewish one must return a found object to him, even though we do not follow majority in monetary law, because the finder does not have a strong presumption in it. Therefore, regarding damages, we follow the majority because it did not come into his hand with the owners' knowledge. It is proven that whenever it did not come into his hand with the owners' knowledge, it is not considered a strong presumption. Therefore, since even if the truth were with him he has no strong presumption, and the owners did not transfer it to him, only the Torah transferred it to him, here we say that since the Torah said that in such a case it belongs to the prior owner and that even if he seized it we remove it from him, it is considered certainly the prior owner's money. Either way, without the Torah's view, the other has no acquisition in it at all, only what the Torah granted him; and here the Torah did not grant it to him, but said that wherever it stands, let it stand, and even if he seized it we remove it from him. Therefore it is certainly considered the property of the first owners. But where the owners transferred it to him with their consent, and we are merely uncertain whether this is an acquisition or not, then certainly we do not consider the fact that the law leaves it in the hands of the first owners. Rather, since if it were revealed that this is an acquisition it would belong to the buyer, the buyer has a side of acquisition in it. Through this, all the difficulties of the above gaon, of blessed memory, are removed. Examine this. It also appears that Tosafos asked at the beginning of Bava Metzia: how does the kohen's seizure help? The owner can say, "I want to give it to another kohen." They answered that it refers to a kohen to whom he regularly gives. In my humble opinion, their words require examination. How does the Talmud bring proof generally from this case that if the kohen seized it we remove it from him? One can say that it is different there: with a kohen, were it not for regular priestly gifts, seizure would certainly not apply, for he can give it to another kohen. It is only because of regular priestly gifts. The reason regular priestly gifts treat what he did not yet acquire as though he acquired is that since the owner's way is to give to this kohen, we are witnesses that he resolved and transferred it to him. This is understandable where he knows that he certainly must give it to a kohen; then we say that even before giving it, he certainly transferred it to him. But here, since there is not certainly a kohen, he is not obligated, and the only point would be to say that if the kohen seized it we do not remove it from him. In this, it certainly does not apply to say that he resolved and transferred it to him, for who knows whether he will seize it or not? Especially here, there is certainly an opposite presumption, that he did not resolve and transfer it to him so that his seizure would not help. How then does regular priestly giving apply? Their words require examination. Therefore the main answer appears to be the second answer, that he must give him its value as priestly benefit, etc. According to this, since Tosafos at the beginning of Bava Metzia asked, when one says "half is mine" and one says "all is mine," we should say that doubt does not remove certainty, as with a doubtful heir and a yavam, and they answered that there the yavam is certainly an heir, but here, because this one certainly has it in this courtyard, why should he certainly have it in another courtyard? See there. According to this, it appears that if we had said that if the kohen seized it we do not remove it from him, it would also be considered the kohen's money, even though the owners at least have the benefit of deciding which kohen receives it and have some claim to the value of that benefit. Nevertheless, we would not say that doubt does not remove certainty, according to Tosafos' reasoning that because it is certainly in this one's courtyard, why should it be certainly in another courtyard? But this applies only if seizure is effective. Since, however, if the kohen seizes it we remove it from him, the kohen has no acquisition in it before us. Then that seizure is indeed effective, since at least the owners have some certain acquisition in it, namely the benefit of deciding whom to give it to. Therefore we say that since he has some acquisition in it, doubt does not remove certainty, and it is considered entirely his. This rule therefore applies only to priestly gifts. But elsewhere, such as chametz or one who buys two trees, where he has no acquisition at all in the chametz or the land if it belongs to the opposing party, then even if seizure would be removed from him, the buyer is still considered to have a side of acquisition, because if it were revealed retroactively that it was his, it would be his. This is clear and correct in my view as a matter of reasoning. Examine carefully.
