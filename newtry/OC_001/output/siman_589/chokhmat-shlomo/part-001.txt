**** ENGLISH ****
<b>(There, se'if 6)</b> Even though women are exempt, they may blow. Gloss: See the Magen Avraham, who was astonished at the Bach (for behold we hold that [regarding] a minor who eats neveilos, the court is not commanded to restrain him) see there. And in my humble opinion there is no difficulty: for there is a distinction whether the nevelah belongs to the minor or to the adult. That [ruling that] a minor who eats neveilos (the court is not commanded to restrain him) is when the nevelah is the minor's, or belongs to others who are not before us; in that case those who see him need not restrain him, for it is not like giving it into his hands. But if the nevelah belongs to the adult, and the adult sees him and does not protest against him, then it is like giving it into his hands. And so here too the case is that the shofar belongs to the adult; therefore regarding a woman and a minor one is not obligated to protest, but regarding a deaf person and a shoteh one is obligated to protest, so it appears to my humble opinion, correct and clear; and examine carefully.

And behold, see the source of this se'if's law in the Gemara and the Rishonim: and from this it is clear that they ruled like R' Yosi, that women's leaning [on a korban] is optional. And behold, this is evident from the language of the Rambam in chapter 3 of Maaseh HaKorbanos, halachah 5: "Olot of gentiles; they do not bring drink-offerings with them, etc., and they do not require leaning, for leaning applies only to Yisrael, to men and not to women" (end quote). Behold, from this language "they do not require leaning," it is implied that if he wishes he may lean; and it is evident that he rules like R' Yosi that it is optional.

And behold, thereby one may resolve the words of the Rambam in chapter 3 of Hilchos Chagigah, halachah 3, and this is his language: a tumtum and an androginos are exempt because they are a doubtful woman. And the Lechem Mishneh there already wondered that this contradicts the Gemara of Chagigah, folio 4, which says that this is derived from the verse "your males," and also objects there that a tumtum is a doubt (does a verse come to exclude a doubt?) and answers: when his testicles are outside; and the Rambam omitted this. He also wrote that an androginos is [exempt] because of a doubtful woman, whereas the Gemara says he is a distinct creature (see there, where he left it as requiring further study).

And what appeared to me to resolve it is as follows. From the outset I found difficult against the Rambam: what of it that he is a doubtful woman; nevertheless he is obligated to fulfill the mitzvah out of doubt, as is the law of every Torah doubt; and even according to the Rambam that every Torah doubt is [treated as] rabbinic, nevertheless by rabbinic law he would be obligated to fulfill. And it is resolved for me: since it is heads-or-tails, if he brings korbanos they are chullin in the [Temple] courtyard; and if he brings and stipulates on condition that if not [he is obligated] it should be a freewill offering (freewill vows and donations are not offered on Yom Tov; and to offer after Yom Tov) since it was not fit on the first day it is not fit on the second, since we hold that all are make-ups for the first. And so heads-or-tails: if he offers there is a prohibition as above, and without a korban there is a prohibition the other way, for "and they shall not appear before Me empty" is written; therefore Shev ve'al taaseh is preferable. And afterward I saw in Tosafos on that sugya that they wrote likewise.

But it is still difficult for me: for we say in Eruvin, folio 97, that Yonah's wife would go up for the pilgrimage festival and the Sages did not protest against her, and the Gemara there establishes it according to the view that women's leaning is optional; and Tosafos wrote there that according to R' Yehudah they needed to protest against her because of the korban, which would be chullin in the courtyard (see there); if so it is implied that according to R' Yosi she would also bring a korban. And so, since it is optional, all the more so we should say that one might have thought to be stringent: what should he do with the korban; even were he a woman, she too is permitted to offer, and there is no opposite concern of prohibition.

But it appears that, aside from what is written in Eruvin, folio 97, where it objects: but is an anonymous Sifra not [according to] R' Yehudah, etc., and so here too this beraisa of "your males" to exclude a tumtum and an androginos is likewise Sifra and is R' Yehudah's; and R' Yehudah holds that women are forbidden to lean. Aside from this as well, it is evident that it accords with R' Yehudah: for if it were according to R' Yosi, from where would we exclude a tumtum and an androginos from "your males" more than saying it comes to exclude women, as the language implies? And if from the language "male" that too would be implied (then why [say] "your males"? Perhaps it comes to exclude women [even] from the optional [ascent], that they are forbidden to go up) and from where that it comes to exclude a tumtum and an androginos from obligation? Of necessity it accords with R' Yehudah. And so, since throughout the Torah they are forbidden to go up, "your males" is not needed to exclude [women], and of necessity it comes to exclude a tumtum, etc.

And according to this it is well. For behold the Chavvos Daas wrote, and so other authors, that the Torah spoke only of a certainty; and this is an explicit Gemara in Sotah, folio 25, regarding Rabban Gamliel who posed: it is written "every pure person shall eat meat" a certain pure person shall eat, but a doubtful pure person shall not eat, etc. And so the Gemara's objection is thus: why do we need "your males" if only "male" were written we would also exclude a doubt, for "male" implies a certain male and not a doubt; and so why "your males"? And to this it objects: granted regarding an androginos there is no difficulty, for one may say that this beraisa accords with the view that he is a distinct creature and needs "your males"; but a tumtum is a doubt (and would a verse come to exclude a doubt?) in astonishment; for if only "male" were written, that too would imply "and not a doubt" from the language "male"; and even without any verse at all we would exempt him, for automatically, since a Torah doubt [is treated] stringently, there is the opposite concern: perhaps he is a woman, and one cannot offer, since it accords with R' Yehudah that women are forbidden to lean; certainly Shev ve'al taaseh is preferable. Therefore it is necessary to answer "when his testicles are outside." And behold this is according to R' Yehudah; but for us, since we hold like R' Yosi that women's leaning is optional, and behold although the Gemara brings a beraisa in Eruvin that regarding Yonah's wife the Sages did not protest against her, Tosafos brought the Pesikta that the Sages did protest against her, and according to this one may say that the Rambam rules like R' Yosi and rules according to the Pesikta that the Sages protested against her; that is, that even according to R' Yosi she is forbidden to lean, for "your males" excludes women even from the optional. And so "your males" comes to exclude women; and a tumtum and an androginos in truth need no verse, for they are a doubtful woman, and they cannot be stringent, for there is a prohibition the other way; that women are forbidden to go up. Automatically one may say that if his testicles are outside, so that it is known he is truly male, he is in fact obligated to go up. And that the Rambam did not clarify that women are forbidden to go up is because it is automatically implied: since he exempts a doubtful woman, [and] a Torah doubt [is] for stringency, of necessity it is impossible to "fix" [the matter], for there is a prohibition the other way. And what of it that she is a woman; [if] a woman is optional, of necessity he holds that a woman is forbidden to lean; and it is evident that he rules like the Pesikta. Also it is not his way to write what is not explicit in the Gemara, as is known of his way. So it appears to my humble opinion, correct, with the help of Hashem; and examine carefully.

<br><b>(There, se'if 6 in the gloss)</b> And the custom is that women recite the blessing over time-bound positive mitzvos; therefore here too they should recite the blessing for themselves, etc. Gloss: Behold I saw in Sefer Shemos BaAretz on Rosh Hashanah, folio 33, that he raised a difficulty that the Tur's words contradict one another: for here he wrote that they may recite the blessing if they wish, and above in siman 17 regarding tzitzis he wrote that it is better that they not recite the blessing (see there, where he was pressed in his resolution). And in my humble opinion one may say thus: for if women do not intend to "add," there is no prohibition that they recite the blessing, for to transgress "do not add" requires intent; and if they do not intend to add, there is no prohibition in their blessing, only that we hold that in its proper time one transgresses "do not add" without intent; therefore it is proper that they not recite the blessing, for then there is a distinguishing sign that they are not doing it for the sake of the mitzvah, as he wrote something similar in the name of the Mordechai: that therefore there is no concern of "do not add," since they do not recite the blessing; brought in the Shulchan Aruch of the Gaon, the pious Moreinu HaRav R' Eliyahu of Vilna, of blessed memory, siman 667. But one may say: regarding women today is not "its time," and so relative to them it is counted as not in its time, and then intent is required; and since they do not intend to add, why should they not recite the blessing? But one may say that this depends on whether women are counted as a people of their own or not; and see regarding this a dispute in tractate Shabbos, chapter 6, folio 62 (see there). And if we say women are a people of their own, relative to them it is counted as not in its time and intent is required; but if we say they are not a people of their own, then since it is its time for males, regarding women too one transgresses "do not add" without intent. And according to this one may say thus: regarding a time-bound positive mitzvah from which males are obligated and it is impossible to exempt oneself from it at all (and since women are exempt and men are truly obligated) women are counted as a people of their own, and for them it is not its time, and intent is required. But regarding a time-bound positive mitzvah from which males too have a side by which to exempt themselves, in that case women are not counted as a people of their own. Similar to what we say in Menachos regarding same-species [mixing]: that where it is possible to be like its fellow it is counted as same-species (see there); so one may say here. And therefore regarding shofar, where males are obligated and it is impossible to exempt oneself from it, and it is truly a personal obligation, therefore women are counted as a people of their own, and it is not its time, and "do not add" does not apply; therefore they are permitted to recite the blessing. But regarding tzitzis, which is an obligation of the garment and not a personal obligation, in that case, since even upon males there is no absolute obligation, and it is in his hand to exempt himself if he does not wear a four-cornered garment, therefore they are not counted as a people of their own, and it is counted as its time for women too, since it is its time for men; therefore intent is not required to transgress; therefore the Tur wrote that it is better that she not recite the blessing. So it appears to my humble opinion, correct in reason and as a practical ruling in this. <b>(There, se'if 8)</b> One who is occupied with shofar-blowing in order to practice has not fulfilled his obligation, etc. Gloss: Behold the Ran wrote at the end of Rosh Hashanah on the Mishnah that one who hears from one who is so occupied has not fulfilled, for even according to the view that mitzvos do not require intent, here it is worse, since he intends for those children it is "of its kind" and ruins it (see there). And it is very difficult for me from the Gemara there, which objects: "one who hears from one who is so occupied has not fulfilled" [implying] that from one who sounds for himself he has fulfilled; let this be a refutation of R' Zeira, who said to his attendant, "Intend and blow for me" and it answers "since [the earlier clause].." And it is difficult for me: what does it object according to the Ran's view above? For one may say that on the contrary it teaches us an additional novelty: that in truth mitzvos never require intent, and if he heard from a blower not for the sake of the mitzvah at all he has certainly fulfilled, and he need not intend that the other discharge him; only that R' Zeira's case is where his attendant intended to discharge himself; therefore, since it is "of its kind," it ruins it, and therefore he must intend also to discharge R' Zeira. And therefore the Mishnah teaches us an additional novelty: not [only] from one who sounds for himself, which is truly for the sake of the mitzvah (where it is obvious that it is truly "of its kind" and ruins it, and he has not fulfilled) but even if it is like one occupied [without mitzvah intent], one might have thought that since it is only for training and the hearer intends for the sake of the mitzvah proper, this is not called "of its kind" and does not ruin it; therefore it teaches us that this too is "of its kind" and ruins it and requires intent. And this is a great difficulty against the Ran's view, in my humble opinion, and it requires further study; and examine carefully.

Also it is difficult for me: what does it object, for one may say as we hold regarding one who seizes for a creditor in a place where he damages others, that he has not acquired, whereas one who lifts a find for his fellow has acquired by virtue of "since he acquired for himself," etc. say likewise here: where the blower has not yet fulfilled, he need not intend to discharge the hearer, by virtue of "since he acquired for himself"; but if the blower has already fulfilled, then we require intent; and R' Zeira's case is where the blower had already fulfilled. Even though it is forced to say that his attendant would fulfill before R' Zeira; nevertheless say that R' Zeira holds that l'chatchila intent is required in every case, and the Mishnah refers after the fact; and this requires further study; and examine carefully.

And behold, that which is explained in the Shulchan Aruch ("one who is occupied with shofar-blowing in order to practice has not fulfilled his obligation") seemingly the language of the Shulchan Aruch is not precise, for he took this out of order according to what the Ran wrote in tractate Rosh Hashanah in two places: that even according to the view that one who blew for a song has fulfilled, for mitzvos do not require intent; he concedes that one who is so occupied [for practice] has not fulfilled, for this is worse in that he intends for the sake of a mitzvah of training children, as is written in tractate Zevachim that "of its kind" ruins it, whereas what is not of its kind does not ruin it (see there). And according to this the Shulchan Aruch fits well: first he wrote that blowing in order to practice does not help, and afterward he wrote regarding one who blows for a song that he has not fulfilled; this is the manner of "not only this but even that": not only where he intended to practice, which is "of its kind," has he not fulfilled, but even one who blows for a song, which is not "of its kind," has not fulfilled. But that which he wrote afterward (where the hearer intended and the sounder did not intend to discharge the hearer, that he has not fulfilled) this is "this, and it goes without saying that": for if intending only to train the children, which is "of its kind" and ruins it, all the more so if he intended actually to fulfill his own obligation for the sake of the mitzvah, which is "of its kind" and ruins it, since he did not intend to discharge the hearer; and it is a fortiori that he has not fulfilled: just as the one occupied [for practice] has not fulfilled, all the more so in this case. And likewise it is a fortiori from one who blows for a song: just as one who blows for a song, which is not "of its kind," [still] ruins it, all the more so one who blows for the sake of the mitzvah and does not intend to discharge the hearer has not fulfilled; and this is "this, and it goes without saying that."

But in truth it is difficult for me against the Ran from the Gemara of Rosh Hashanah, folio 33, which objects there regarding one who hears from one occupied [for practice] that he has not fulfilled (implying that one who hears from one who sounds for himself has fulfilled) and a beraisa teaches, etc. And according to the Ran's words it is difficult: what does it object? Say that on the contrary it teaches us an additional novelty: that even one who hears from one occupied [for practice], who did not intend for a complete mitzvah but only to train children, and it is only rabbinic; nevertheless it is called "of its kind" and is effective that he has not fulfilled according to the Torah, even though according to the Torah there is no mitzvah at all and it is not "its kind" according to the Torah; nevertheless he has not fulfilled according to the Torah and is obligated to blow again according to the Torah; and there is a practical difference for several matters between whether he is obligated according to the Torah or only by rabbinic law, all the more so where he intended to fulfill for himself and did not intend to discharge the hearer, for in that case it is truly "its kind" and is a Torah mitzvah, which is "of its kind" and ruins it, and all agree that intent is required, and if he did not intend to discharge the hearer it does not help; and this is "this, and it goes without saying that" so what does it object?

And behold, thereby the language of the Shulchan Aruch would be well: for from that Gemara he proved that one occupied [for practice] is worse than one who intended for himself; and therefore he properly wrote in the manner of "not only this but even that." But even so, against the Ran it remains difficult: from where for the Gemara to raise a difficulty, for there is reason to say that intending for oneself is worse than being occupied [for practice], according to the Ran's reasoning above; so what does it object? And this requires further study.

And behold I examined Rashi's language, who wrote s.v. "One occupied (that is not [sufficient], etc.": "but one who blows for a song, who intends for blowing in general, has fulfilled") end quote. And this is puzzling: for the Gemara wishes to prove only that if he blows for the sake of the mitzvah but does not intend to discharge the hearer, he has fulfilled; and against this it objects from R' Zeira, so why mention here one who blows for a song? And one cannot say that Rashi explains that what the Gemara wrote "but one who hears from one who sounds for himself" means one who blows for a song who sounds for himself (that is, of his own mind, and not because of the day's obligation that rests upon him, but of his own mind) for this is not so: afterward Rashi wrote s.v. "The Tanna of the latter clause also [spoke of] one occupied," and this is his language: "and in truth, one who hears from one who sounds for himself, who did not intend to discharge the hearers, has not fulfilled" (end quote). If so, it is implied that he too explains "one who sounds for himself" as merely that he did not intend to discharge the hearers, but for himself he intended to fulfill, as is evident from R' Zeira's language. So why at first did he explain an intent for a song? And it is further difficult: according to his way, that from here it is evident that for a song too he has fulfilled (he should have raised a difficulty from this against the view that mitzvos require intent; why does it object specifically against R' Zeira) rather it should have said "derive from here that mitzvos do not require intent," as is written in Berachos and in several places "derive from here that mitzvos require intent" here it should have said the opposite: "derive from here that mitzvos do not require intent." And why mention R' Zeira's words? Of necessity it is evident according to the Ran's explanation that one who blows for a song is worse than one occupied [for practice]; therefore it objects only against R' Zeira. And this requires further study; and examine carefully.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>(There, se'if 9)</b> Therefore one who was walking on the way or sitting in his house and heard tekios from the agent of the congregation has fulfilled, etc. Gloss: See the Magen Avraham, who brought what the Ran wrote in the name of the Yerushalmi, and this is his language: "When is this said? Regarding one who is passing by; but regarding one who is standing, there is a chazakah that he intended" (end quote. And the Magen Avraham wondered: for does he not know whether he intended or not?) see there, where he was pressed on this. And in my humble opinion it appears that there is a great practical difference according to what is stated above in the Shulchan Aruch, siman 209, se'if 8 in the gloss: that if others were drinking wine, etc., and he recited the blessing "Who creates the fruit of the vine," thinking that in his cup there was wine, and it became known to him that it was water, when he afterward wishes to drink wine he need not recite the blessing, since his intent was to drink wine afterward, and he also discharged others with his blessing; therefore his blessing was a blessing (see there. And so here too: since we hold that one who heard nine tekios at nine hours of the day has fulfilled) and so there is a difference: if the one walking needed a teruah, for he had already heard a tekiah but had not yet heard also a tekiah, and he was obligated in a tekiah first, and he recited the blessing over the shofar and began to blow a teruah first, and since a teruah before a tekiah [means] he has not fulfilled, automatically there was an interruption between the blessing and the beginning of the tekiah, and he must recite the blessing again. But in this case, since the hearer needed a teruah, and so that one [who recited] discharged [others] with this blessing and this tekiah; therefore, since he discharged others, and his intent was also to blow further, this is not a blessing in vain, and he is permitted to blow afterward in order on the strength of the first blessing. And so this is fine if the hearer intended to fulfill; but if he did not intend, then he did not discharge others, and again his blessing was in vain, and he must recite the blessing anew. And so, since where an obligation or prohibition has been established, one witness is not believed to the contrary, for it is against the chazakah, therefore if there were no chazakah that he intended, but only that he said he intended, he would not be believed to exempt the blower from a second blessing, since having become obligated to recite the blessing, one witness is not believed to exempt him; but if we say there is a chazakah that he intended, then he discharged others with his blessing, and therefore he too is exempt from blessing.

And it appears further: since this very law that the Rama wrote in siman 209 is a novelty (that he learns that since they fulfilled, he too fulfills) and reason would suggest the opposite: since he recited a blessing in vain, they too have not fulfilled; and it is more [natural] to subordinate others to him than to subordinate him to others, as the Magen Avraham wrote there. And see siman 213, se'if 2, where the Magen Avraham raised a difficulty that the Shulchan Aruch's words contradict one another, and answered by distinguishing between inadvertent and intentional, and it is forced: what reason is there to distinguish between inadvertent and intentional? And one cannot say that for the intentional it is by way of a fine; first, we do not find that because of a fine they should again recite another blessing in vain; and further, even if they fined him, others did not sin; why should we fine them? And what reason is there to distinguish regarding others between whether the one who blessed was inadvertent or intentional?

But it appears that not for nothing did the Rama specify in siman 209 that he wrote that others were drinking wine and wine was before them; why tell us that wine was before them? But it appears: although in general we do not require a proven presumption and revelation of intent that the hearer intends to fulfill (mere intent in the heart that they intend to fulfill suffices) nevertheless this is specifically if the one who blessed fulfilled; then, since his blessing was according to law and he fulfilled, then [for them] mere thought alone does not help unless their thought is recognizable from their deeds; then, since it is evident in the Gemara in several places that thought recognizable from one's deeds is superior to mere thought and is like an act (see in chapter 2 of Beitzah and in the first chapter of Chullin) therefore if there is wine before them, so that their thought is recognizable from their deeds that they wish to fulfill, it is as if they themselves recited the blessing; and so what of it that he recited a blessing in vain; they fulfill, for it is as if they themselves recited, for in this it is like an actual act. But if their thought is not recognizable from their deeds, but only by intent of the heart alone, in that case it is not as if they themselves recited, but only that they heard; and hearing does not suffice, since he recited a blessing in vain. And therefore in siman 213 it refers to where there was no proven presumption from their deeds, but only that they said they intended (therefore it does not help, since he recited a blessing in vain; but if there is a proven presumption from their deeds, it helps) and this is like the law of siman 209 above. And so according to this the Yerushalmi is well resolved: the practical difference is if the blower recited the blessing and sounded a teruah first before the tekiah, so that there was an interruption between the blessing and the beginning of the tekiah and it was in vain, then if the hearer stood, there is a chazakah that he intended, and the hearer fulfilled even though the one who blessed recited in vain; and then on the contrary the one who blessed also fulfilled, as is written in siman 209; and this is clear and very correct in reason.

And if we say as I wrote at first (that in this the hearer is not believed against the other, since an obligation was established) one may say further a practical difference according to what is written in siman 585: that if he recited the blessing and could not blow, others should blow without a blessing (provided that the first blowers were [present] at the time of the blessing; implying that we require both) that the hearers be present at the time of the blessing and also that the blowers were then [present]; but if the blowers were not there, even though the hearers were there, it does not help. And so there is a difference: if there was no blower other than this one who stood on the way, then if he stood, since there is a chazakah that he intended, it is permitted afterward to blow without a blessing and he discharges the hearers; but if he walked, since it is a doubt whether he intended, he is not believed to say that he intended so as to exempt the hearers from the blessing (since they became obligated with certainty, one witness is not believed to exempt, for one witness is not believed against a chazakah) so it would appear to say. However, in truth this does not appear correct: for even without his testimony, since at any rate it is a doubt, behold we hold that a doubt regarding blessings is resolved leniently; and we do not find a distinction between whether the doubt is in the root of the obligation or whether he became obligated with certainty and there is a doubt whether he was exempted; rather in every case a doubt regarding blessings is resolved leniently; and so even without his testimony he is exempt out of doubt. Therefore it appears that the main [resolution] is the middle answer we wrote: that in this we require that his thought be recognizable from his deeds; and otherwise it is certainly a blessing in vain without any doubt; and it fits well and is correct, with the help of Hashem; and examine carefully.
