Bach writes that our Gemara has the reverse order: first HaMeichin Mitzadei Gaver and then SheAsah Li Kol Tzorki. Many poskim write likewise, and this was the text in all our siddurim. It appears to me that everything depends upon time and place. Nowadays everyone puts on shoes before walking.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>One hundred blessings.</b> Beit Yosef writes that we recite one hundred and five blessings each day. On a fast day eight are missing, leaving ninety-eight. This is a scribal error, because the total would be ninety-seven.

According to our practice of reciting HaNosen LaYaef Koach and two blessings upon tefillin, the total is ninety-nine. Beit Yosef also did not count the blessing Yir'u Eineinu, so the total reaches one hundred even on a fast day.

One who begins the fast before nightfall loses another eight blessings from one meal. Perhaps he can complete them by listening to the chazzan's repetition.

On Shabbos, thirty-six blessings are missing from the three prayers, two tefillin blessings, and Yir'u Eineinu.

Beit Yosef writes that we add the blessing MeEin Sheva. This requires study, because then every repetition of the Amidah should likewise be counted. One may answer that MeEin Sheva differs because the individual did not recite it, though this is forced because the individual still says the core themes of the blessing.

Beit Yosef also counts Kiddush as one blessing, while one who uses wine actually gains three: two at night and one by day.

It therefore appears that on Shabbos we count three Kiddush blessings, seven of Musaf, and sixteen from the two daytime meals. Since according to our calculation even a fast day contains one hundred blessings, these twenty-six leave a deficit of thirteen, which he completes with fruit as explained in siman 290.

The statement in siman 284, seif 3, that listening to the Torah and Haftarah blessings suffices applies only to one who has no fruit. Otherwise, why would Rabbi Abbahu complete the total with spices and delicacies when Torah reading and Haftarah were certainly available?

He must also intend to hear the entire blessing, as stated in siman 284. Since this resembles one personally obligated in the blessing, he must hear it, as explained in siman 124, unlike Beit Yosef here.

I later found in Knesses HaGedolah, citing Maharit, part 1, simanim 117 and 180, that one fulfills the total through listening only where no fruit is available. Maharit nevertheless writes that on Yom Kippur it is preferable to rely upon answering amen rather than fragrance.

On Yom Kippur, thirty-nine blessings are missing as on Shabbos, plus one meal, totaling forty-seven. Against these, there are Shehecheyanu, Musaf, and Neilah, totaling fifteen; seven people including the maftir at Shacharis and three at Minchah, totaling twenty blessings; and the Haftarah blessings at Shacharis and Minchah, totaling ten. This gives forty-five, leaving two.

Knesses HaGedolah suggests reciting Mekadesh Shimcha BeRabim with Hashem's Name and sovereignty, and likewise Modim DeRabbanan with Hashem's Name and sovereignty.

It appears to me that one should not enter a possible blessing in vain merely to complete one hundred blessings.

His further suggestion to recite a separate blessing upon the tallis katan likewise appears improper, because one should not multiply blessings. See siman 215.

According to our custom of omitting Al HaTorah at the Minchah Haftarah, another blessing is missing. If Yom Kippur falls on Shabbos, seven people are called apart from the maftir, adding two blessings.

Shelah writes not to use fragrance, while Seder HaYom permits it.

Yoreh Deah, siman 265, explicitly indicates that fragrance is permitted on Yom Kippur, because it says that myrtle is not brought on Tisha B'Av, implying that it is brought on Yom Kippur. One cannot, however, recite a new blessing each time without a lapse of attention. See siman 217.

<b>שלא עשני גוי</b>. כצ"ל וגירסת שעשני יהודי שינוי מן המדפיסים וכתב ב"ח יש מקשי' אמאי לא מברך שעשני ישראל כמו שאר ברכות שמברכין על הטובה פוקח עורים וכדומה ומתרצים משום דנוח לאדם שלא נברא משנברא ולכן קאמרי' הלואי שלא עשני ועכשיו שעשני אני מברך שלא עשני עכו"ם או עבד או אשה ול"נ דאם היה מברך שעשני ישראל לא היה יכול לברך שעשני בן חורין שעשני איש שהכל בכלל וכוונתו לברך על כל דבר בפ"ע כו' עכ"ל ב"ח ומשמע מדבריו דאם טעה ובירך שעשני ישראל שוב לא יאמר שלא עשני עבד או אשה, ול"נ דאע"ג שאמר ישראל או יהודי אשה בכלל דכל התורה נאמרה בלשון זכר ואפ"ה אשה בכלל כמ"ש התוס' דדוקא היכא דכתיב בני ישראל אמרי' ולא בנות ישראל, מיהו בסנהדרין דף פ"ד משמע דהיינו מטעם דהשוה הכתוב אשה לאיש ע"ש ובב"ק דף ט"ו ובמנחות דף צ"ג וריש עירכין וכב"י דיש להקדים עבד לאשה כדאי' בגמרא דעבד זיל טפי ומשמע בב"ח דלפמ"ש ס"ה גבי מתיר אסורים ה"ה הכא אם בירך שלא עשני אשה בראשונה לא יברך שוב על עכו"ם ועבד ע"ש:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Who did not make me a non-Jew.</b> This is the correct text. The version “Who made me a Jew” is a printer's alteration.

Bach cites a question: why do we not recite the positive formulation “Who made me an Israelite,” as in the other blessings such as Pokeach Ivrim?

Some answer that since the schools of Shammai and Hillel concluded that it would have been better for a person not to have been created, we do not bless Hashem for creating us. We therefore say, in effect, “Although You created me, I thank You that You did not make me a non-Jew, slave, or woman.”

Bach answers that if one said “Who made me an Israelite,” this would include being free and male, and he could not afterward recite separate blessings concerning a slave and a woman. His words imply that if one mistakenly recited “Who made me an Israelite,” he should not recite the other two blessings.

It appears to me that even the term Israelite or Jew includes a woman, because the Torah generally speaks in masculine language while women are included, except where it says “sons of Israel,” which excludes daughters of Israel. Sanhedrin 84, Bava Kama 15, Menachos 93, and the beginning of Erchin should be examined.

Beit Yosef writes that the blessing concerning a slave should precede the blessing concerning a woman, because the Gemara says a slave is more degraded.

Bach implies that according to the rule in seif 5 concerning Matir Asurim, if one first recited “Who did not make me a woman,” he should no longer recite the blessings concerning a non-Jew and a slave.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And even a convert.</b> Meaning that he should say, “Who made me a convert,” because conversion too is called making, as in the verse, “And the souls they made in Charan.” Darkei Moshe.

Shelah and Bach write that this formulation is inappropriate, because becoming a convert resulted from his own choice to accept Judaism. He should therefore recite only the blessings concerning a slave and a woman.

According to them, “Who did not make me a slave” refers to his original creation. Although at birth he was even lower than a slave, a slave could not convert unless his master freed him.

According to the kabbalists, however, these blessings concern the soul's departure at night and its return without attachment to a non-Jewish or slave soul. According to this, a convert may also recite them.

<b>לא יקרא פסוקים</b>. ובלבוש כ' סי' תקפ"א וז"ל דוקא בני אשכנז שנוהגין לומר ברכת התורה קודם פרשת התמיד א"כ ס"ל דמותר לומר פסוקים קודם ברכת התורה אבל לדידן שאומרים ברכת התורה מיד אחר אשר יצר א"כ ס"ל דאסור לומר פסוקים קודם ברכת התורה א"כ גם בימי הסליחות יאמר ברכת התורה קודם הסליחות ולדלגה אח"כ עכ"ל וכנ"ל לנהוג וכמ"ש סי' ו' ס"ב ואם יש א' שלא אמרה ירד לפני התיבה ויאמר' אח"כ:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>He should not recite verses.</b> Levush writes in siman 581:

The Ashkenazic communities recite the blessing over Torah before the daily-offering passage, showing that they permit verses before the blessing over Torah. We, however, recite the blessing over Torah immediately after Asher Yatzar, showing that we prohibit verses before it.

Accordingly, during Selichos one should recite the blessing over Torah before Selichos and omit it afterward.

This appears to be the correct practice, as written in siman 6, seif 2. If one participant has not yet recited it, he should lead the service and recite it afterward.

<b>כי לפעמים כו'</b>. פי' משום דלפעמים שוהין הציבור לכן יאמרו בכל פעם אך לא יכוון לצאת אלא א"כ ירא שהצבור יעברו זמן ק"ש אבל כשלא יעברו מוטב לצאת ידי ק"ש עם הצבור ולקרות אותה כדינה בברכותי' ולסמוך גאולה לתפל' ועססי' נ"ו ובזה מתורץ קושי' הב"י [ב"ח] ואף על פי שהוא כתב שלא יאמר בשכמל"ו נ"ל דאין בכך כלום כיון שאין מתכוון  לצאת בזה ורש"ל בתשו' כ' לו' ג"כ פ' ראשונה של שמע ונ"ל דדוקא כשירא שיתעכבו הצבור, יאמר אתה הוא עד שלא נברא העולם ולא יאמר עד שלא בראת העולם (של"ה ב"ח):
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Because sometimes, etc.</b> Since the congregation sometimes delays, this passage should always be recited.

One should not intend to fulfill the obligation of Krias Shema unless he fears that the congregation will pass the proper time. If they will not, it is preferable to fulfill Krias Shema together with the congregation, with its blessings, and juxtapose redemption to prayer. See the end of siman 56.

This resolves Beit Yosef's question. Although Bach writes that Baruch Shem Kevod should not be said, it appears to me that there is no concern, since he does not intend to fulfill his obligation through this recitation.

Maharshal writes that the first paragraph of Shema should also be recited. It appears to me that this applies specifically where he fears the congregation will delay beyond the time.

One should say, “You are He before the world was created,” and not, “before You created the world.” Shelah and Bach.
