<b>שלא עשני גוי. </b> רבים מקשי' למה תקנו ברכ' זו דרך שליל' ולא דרך חיוב דהיינו שעשני ישראל וכן בברכת שלא עשני אשה ומתרצים לפי שנמנו וגמרו ב"ש וב"ה טוב לאדם שלא נברא ע"כ לא תקנו לברך על העשי' שנעש' האדם דטוב לנו שלא נעשה אלא לכל הפחות על שלא נעשה עכו"ם ומו"ח ז"ל פי' לפי שאם היה מברך שעשני ישראל היה משמעו בן חורין ולא אשה ולא היה שייך לברך אח"ז שלא עשני עבד או אשה וק"ל דהא כתבו התוס' במנחות וביומ' פ' בא לו דבכל מקום שיש לפטור שני דבריה בברכ' א' אסור להרבות בברכות וא"כ הכא נמי היה לו לברך ברכה א' והיה בכללם שאר ברכות ונ"ל דרצו להורות בזה שלא יטעה האדם לתלות ח"ו חסרון בבריאת האדם על שבורא עכו"ם וכן בבריאת אשה. ובאמת יש בהן צורך אפילו בבריאת עכו"ם שיצאו מהם גרים כמ"ש בב"ק פרק הפרה שחס הקב"ה על ב' אומות בשביל ב' פרידות טובות שעתיד לצאת מהם רות ונעמה ובדבר זה יש לתרץ מה שקשה במה שראינו שבירך הוא ית' את אברהם ע"י הגר שיוליד י"ב נשיאים וכי מה זו ברכ' שיבואו הרבה כמותם לעולם הלא טוב יותר שיהיו רשעים מעטים אלא שמתוך הרבוי יתרבו גרים כמו שארז"ל הגלה הקב"ה את ישראל כדי שיתוספו עליהם גרים כמ"ש וזרעתי' לי בארץ. ודבר זה הוא ק"ו באשה שיש בה בריאה טובה שגם היא מקיימת קצת מצות ע"כ אמר כאן שנותן הודיה לו ית' שלא עשאו עכו"ם הורה בזה שיש צורך בריאה גם בעכו"ם וכ"ש באשה אלא שזכה האדם שלא נברא הוא באופן אחר שהיה ג"כ צורך הבריאה וזה שלא עשני עכו"ם אבל לאחרים עשה וכן באשה וזה לא היה נרמז אם אמר שעשני ישראל דהיינו אומרים שהבריאה למי שאינו ישראל אין בו צורך כלל וגבי אשה היה מתורץ טפי דהוצרך להזכיר שיש מעלה גם בבריאת אשה אלא שהוא לא ניצטרך לאותה מעלה. ונראה ליעוד מה שהנשים מברכות שעשני כרצונו דברכה זו אינה בגמ' ולמ"ש ניחא דהא חזי' אפי' בברכת האיש שיש מעלה בבריאת האשה ע"כ שפיר חייבת היא לברך על מעלה שלה כנ"ל נכון:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Who did not make me a non-Jew.</b> Many ask why this blessing was instituted negatively rather than positively as “Who made me an Israelite,” and likewise why we say “Who did not make me a woman.”

Some answer that since the schools of Shammai and Hillel concluded that it would have been better for a person not to have been created, the blessing does not praise the creation itself. We therefore thank Hashem at least for not creating us as a non-Jew, slave, or woman.

My father-in-law explains that if one said “Who made me an Israelite,” that would imply free and male, leaving no basis for the two later blessings.

This is difficult. Tosafos in Menachos and Yoma writes that wherever two matters can be covered by one blessing, one may not multiply blessings. Here too, one blessing should have included all three.

It appears that the negative wording teaches that one should not attribute any deficiency, Heaven forbid, to Hashem's creation of non-Jews or women. They too are necessary to creation.

Even non-Jews are necessary because converts emerge from among them. The Holy One, blessed is He, spared two nations for the sake of the two good descendants who would emerge from them, Rus and Naamah.

This also explains Hashem's blessing to Avraham through Hagar that twelve princes would descend from her. The increase of such descendants is a blessing because converts may emerge from among them, as the Sages say that Israel was exiled among the nations so that converts would be added.

All the more so, a woman is a beneficial creation and fulfills certain mitzvos.

A man therefore thanks Hashem that He did not make him a non-Jew, while acknowledging that Hashem did make others as non-Jews for a necessary purpose. The same applies to a woman. He merited not to require that particular role, but its creation remains valuable.

This would not be conveyed by “Who made me an Israelite,” which could imply that all other forms of creation lack purpose.

This also explains why women recite “Who made me according to His will,” although that blessing is not stated in the Gemara. Since even the man's blessing recognizes the value of woman's creation, she may appropriately bless Hashem for her own role.

הרוקח לא היה כן ותו דע"כ אפילו לגי' שלנו אין הברכות נאמרים על הסדר וכמ"ש בתחלת סי' זה בברכת פ"ע כי מנח ידיו על עיניו אבל ודאי  העיקר דז"כ קודם מ"א:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>If he first recited.</b> This is the view of Rav Amram cited by Tur.

Beit Yosef explains that although both blessings are ordinarily required, Matir Asurim must be recited first. The Gemara says: when he sits up, he recites Matir Asurim; when he straightens, he recites Zokef Kefufim.

When he sits and stretches his limbs, he thanks Hashem for moving limbs that had been bound during the night. When he stands upright, he thanks Hashem for straightening his posture.

Rav Amram holds that if he first recited Zokef Kefufim, he should not afterward recite Matir Asurim, because movement of the limbs is included in straightening the body.

Tur disagrees and asks why, since the Gemara assigns a separate reason to each blessing.

Everyone agrees that if two possible blessing arrangements exist, one with a single blessing and one with two, the single blessing should ordinarily be preferred to avoid multiplying blessings.

Here, however, each blessing was instituted at a distinct moment. When he first sat up, Zokef Kefufim was not yet relevant. Only Matir Asurim applied. When he later stood, a new act created the obligation of Zokef Kefufim.

Rav Amram holds that although today we recite all the blessings together in the synagogue and could theoretically recite only Zokef Kefufim, we should not alter the Talmudic order. But if he happened to recite Zokef Kefufim first, he is exempt from Matir Asurim. Even in Talmudic times, if he failed to recite Matir Asurim when sitting and later recited Zokef Kefufim, he would not repeat the included blessing.

Tur holds that because each blessing had its own original moment of obligation, the separate obligation remains even after Zokef Kefufim.

My father-in-law asks why Rav Amram did not similarly rule that one who first recited “Who did not make me a woman” should omit “Who did not make me a slave,” and certainly “Who did not make me a non-Jew.” The Gemara asks why both slave and woman are necessary and answers that a slave is more degraded.

There is no difficulty. A slave or non-Jew may later become fully obligated through emancipation or conversion, while a woman can never become obligated in all mitzvos. Therefore the blessing concerning a woman does not include the other two.

The blessings concerning a non-Jew and a slave are likewise both required. Bava Kama 15 teaches that each has a distinct inferior status. A slave lacks lineage, while a non-Jew is outside the mitzvah system. Each also has an advantage over the other.

A person must therefore thank Hashem separately that he was not created in any of these three states.

Matir Asurim, however, is truly included in Zokef Kefufim. Rav Amram's ruling is therefore correct, and Shulchan Aruch properly follows it because of doubt concerning blessings.

Rokeach lists Zokef Kefufim before Matir Asurim, as do old siddurim. This appears contrary to the Gemara.

Perhaps Rokeach interpreted “when he straightens” as lifting his head while still sitting in bed, and “when he sits upright” as the later movement of separating and stretching his limbs.

His version of the Gemara may also have had the clauses in a different order.

In any event, the primary order is Zokef Kefufim before Matir Asurim.

<b>Their words do not appear correct.</b> Tur explains that Ashkenazic siddurim include HaNosen LaYaef Koach based upon the verse, “New each morning.” Nevertheless, because the blessing does not appear in the Gemara, Beit Yosef rules not to say it. Maharshal, responsum 64, writes likewise.

Semag also lists this blessing, and Rama testifies that Ashkenazic communities recite it. An established custom should not be disregarded.

Rosh's rulings appear contradictory. In Bechoros and Kiddushin he records a blessing upon redeeming a firstborn and concludes that France and Germany did not recite it because no post-Talmudic blessing should be instituted.

Yet at the beginning of Kesubos he records the blessing upon finding virginity and writes that the Geonim may have instituted it and that it is reasonable to recite it afterward.

The resolution is that where an established custom already exists, the blessing should not be canceled even if it is not explicitly in the Gemara. The Geonim may have possessed a Talmudic basis for it.

Rosh did not intend to cancel the firstborn blessing where it was customary, but merely explained why France and Germany did not say it. The virginity blessing already had an established custom, and he therefore did not cancel it.

This supports reciting HaNosen LaYaef Koach where a custom exists.

There is no concern of violating “You shall not take Hashem's Name in vain.” Rabbeinu Tam, cited by Rosh in Kiddushin, proves that mentioning Hashem's Name in a blessing does not violate that prohibition even where no obligation exists.

The custom is therefore sufficient basis for this blessing.

Magbiah Shefalim is different. Beit Yosef writes that some recite it although it is absent from the Gemara. Agudah reports that it appeared in some Talmudic texts, and Bach therefore writes that it should not be removed from siddurim.

Since there is no clear custom, however, one should not enter the doubt and should omit it. Somech Noflim, which appears in no source, should certainly be removed. This is the primary ruling.

<b>Because sometimes, etc.</b> Tur writes that Rabbi Yehudah HaChasid practiced this.

Beit Yosef challenges the custom from the Yerushalmi, where Rabbi Acha wished to protest against those who did so.

Rabbi Yehudah HaChasid apparently relied upon the Babylonian Gemara, which is primary. Rabbi Yehudah HaNasi recited Shema during his study session with his students and later recited it again with its blessings, as Beit Yosef explains at length.

Maharshal, responsum 64, writes that when he reaches the Krias Shema of Rabbi Yehudah HaNasi, he recites the entire first paragraph through “and in your gates.” Ri writes likewise in his novellae to Berachos.

He further says that the blessing afterward, Mekadesh Shimcha BeRabim, from the Yerushalmi, appropriately follows. Just as Hashem sanctifies His Name publicly, we are obligated to sanctify His Name publicly, which is included in “with all your heart,” etc.

His words imply that Mekadesh Shimcha BeRabim should be recited as a formal blessing with Hashem's Name, saying, “Blessed are You, Hashem, Who sanctifies,” etc.

Beit Yosef likewise includes it in the count of blessings. Semag, Tur, and Tosafos, Pesachim 104b, also treat it as a blessing.

This is surprising because it is not mentioned in the Babylonian Gemara. In siman 157, Beit Yosef derives from Borei Nefashos that Modim DeRabbanan should not conclude with Hashem's Name because it appears only in the Yerushalmi. Rambam likewise does not mention Hashem's Name here.

We can only answer as above: since everyone has already adopted this blessing, the custom remains binding.

Tur's text reads, “You are He before the world was created.” This is likewise the text in Tosafos, Pesachim 104b and Berachos 46, Rambam, all old siddurim, the Yerushalmi, and Yalkut VaEschanan in the name of Eliyahu.

Sephardic siddurim read, “before You created the world.”

No one should alter ancient texts. A recent grammarian changed our text to match the Sephardic version, and he did improperly.

Some object that “before the world was created” could imply, Heaven forbid, that another created it.

The opposite is true. The altered text merely says that Hashem existed before and after He created the world, which is obvious because every actor exists before and after his act. If the intention were to identify the Creator, it should simply say, “You created the world.”

The original text testifies to Hashem's eternity: He always exists, both before the world's creation and afterward, as in Yigdal, “Primeval before all that was created.”

One who changes this or other ancient prayer texts is at a disadvantage. My father-in-law writes likewise.
