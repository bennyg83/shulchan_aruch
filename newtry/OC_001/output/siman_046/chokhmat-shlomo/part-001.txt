<b>Seif 3: A person is obligated to recite one hundred blessings every day.</b>

See Magen Avraham, seif katan 5, who writes that one cannot recite a new blessing each time unless there was a lapse of attention.

These are the words of Shelah in the laws of Yom Kippur, where he questions Seder HaYom's instruction to take spices on Yom Kippur and recite a blessing each time. The law explicitly states that one who enters a spice shop and intends to return does not recite a new blessing each time.

With due respect, their words are difficult.

The rule that a new blessing requires a lapse of attention applies only where one did not expressly limit the first blessing. In such a case, the first blessing covers the entire continuing experience so long as there was no lapse of attention.

If, however, he expressly intends that the blessing apply only to this present smelling and not to what he will do later, then when he returns to the spice shop or smells again he must recite another blessing.

Ordinarily one should not arrange matters this way, because it unnecessarily causes an additional blessing when one blessing could have covered everything.

But where he intends to complete one hundred blessings, it is not considered an unnecessary blessing. He needs the additional blessing in order to fulfill the enactment of one hundred blessings. Seder HaYom's ruling is therefore correct.

I was also asked by a scholar from Buczacz concerning Rambam, chapter 6 of Hilchos Berachos, halachah 2. Rambam writes that one who washes his hands, whether for eating, Krias Shema, or prayer, recites Al Netilas Yadayim.

Why, then, in chapter 7 of Hilchos Tefillah, when Rambam lists the one hundred daily blessings, does he count handwashing for eating but not handwashing for prayer?

This is a strong question.

The law of one hundred blessings cannot merely mean that one should count the blessings he happens to recite, because he would recite them in any case. Rather, the enactment teaches that a person should bring himself into situations requiring these blessings.

This is especially so according to Bach, who holds that listening to others does not suffice and that he must recite the blessings himself.

A person can guard his hands from distraction and thereby avoid washing before prayer, or he can pray immediately after eating and avoid another washing. Even washing before eating can sometimes be avoided by washing once with a condition for the entire day.

Nevertheless, the obligation of washing before eating is stronger than washing before prayer. Our custom does not require a blessing for washing before prayer at all, while everyone agrees that washing before eating requires a blessing. Rambam too must regard the former obligation as stronger.

Rambam therefore teaches which blessings one should especially seek to incur personally in order to reach one hundred. He lists those whose obligation is stronger and more essential.

If a person happened to recite other blessings, such as because his attention lapsed and he needed to wash before prayer, those blessings certainly count toward the one hundred and may replace others.

Initially, however, he should seek to bring himself into obligation specifically for the blessings Rambam lists, because they are more significant and necessary.

Ordinarily it is forbidden to cause an unnecessary blessing, as stated in Yoma 70a and Magen Avraham, siman 215. To fulfill the mitzvah of one hundred blessings, however, we are not concerned for this, as Magen Avraham writes there.

Rambam therefore gives practical advice. For the blessings he lists, one should bring himself into obligation without concern for creating an unnecessary blessing. Beyond those, he should avoid unnecessary blessings. If he nevertheless incurred them, he must recite them and they count toward the one hundred. This appears correct.
