**** ENGLISH ****
Se'if 1. When one puts on his shoes, he should recite the blessing “Who has provided me with all my needs.” Gloss: The reason this blessing was instituted specifically when putting on shoes appears to follow the Sages' statement that a person should always sell everything he owns and purchase shoes for his feet. When he sees that he has shoes and therefore does not need to sell everything he owns, he blesses and thanks the Omnipresent for providing all his needs: he need not sell everything he owns, and he has shoes as well. This is well explained. One may further explain a great allusion in this practice. By reciting “Who has provided me with all my needs,” a person constantly fulfills the Sages' teaching that one must bless over misfortune just as he blesses over good. Every day he says that the Omnipresent provided all his needs, yet there are days on which, Heaven forbid, misfortune befalls a person. How can he then bless that all his needs were provided? Would this not be a blessing in vain? Necessarily, he declares that the apparent misfortune is also good and gives thanks for it. Misfortune is good because, as the Sages teach, suffering brings a person to the life of the World to Come. The Midrash states that Yitzchak requested suffering for this reason. A righteous person is called one who “walks,” because he proceeds from level to level, as the commentators of blessed memory explain. The blessing was therefore instituted when putting on shoes. Just as shoes are needed for walking (one remaining in one place does not need shoes, but one who walks does) so a person needs suffering, because through it he advances toward attachment to the Omnipresent, blessed is He, and observance of Torah and mitzvot. He therefore gives thanks even for misfortune. This is well explained, with the help of the Omnipresent; examine it carefully.

<b>Seif 3: A person is obligated to recite one hundred blessings every day.</b>

See Magen Avraham, seif katan 5, who writes that one cannot recite a new blessing each time unless there was a lapse of attention.

These are the words of Shelah in the laws of Yom Kippur, where he questions Seder HaYom's instruction to take spices on Yom Kippur and recite a blessing each time. The law explicitly states that one who enters a spice shop and intends to return does not recite a new blessing each time.

With due respect, their words are difficult.

The rule that a new blessing requires a lapse of attention applies only where one did not expressly limit the first blessing. In such a case, the first blessing covers the entire continuing experience so long as there was no lapse of attention.

If, however, he expressly intends that the blessing apply only to this present smelling and not to what he will do later, then when he returns to the spice shop or smells again he must recite another blessing.

Ordinarily one should not arrange matters this way, because it unnecessarily causes an additional blessing when one blessing could have covered everything.

But where he intends to complete one hundred blessings, it is not considered an unnecessary blessing. He needs the additional blessing in order to fulfill the enactment of one hundred blessings. Seder HaYom's ruling is therefore correct.

I was also asked by a scholar from Buczacz concerning Rambam, chapter 6 of Hilchos Berachos, halachah 2. Rambam writes that one who washes his hands, whether for eating, Krias Shema, or prayer, recites Al Netilas Yadayim.

Why, then, in chapter 7 of Hilchos Tefillah, when Rambam lists the one hundred daily blessings, does he count handwashing for eating but not handwashing for prayer?

This is a strong question.

The law of one hundred blessings cannot merely mean that one should count the blessings he happens to recite, because he would recite them in any case. Rather, the enactment teaches that a person should bring himself into situations requiring these blessings.

This is especially so according to Bach, who holds that listening to others does not suffice and that he must recite the blessings himself.

A person can guard his hands from distraction and thereby avoid washing before prayer, or he can pray immediately after eating and avoid another washing. Even washing before eating can sometimes be avoided by washing once with a condition for the entire day.

Nevertheless, the obligation of washing before eating is stronger than washing before prayer. Our custom does not require a blessing for washing before prayer at all, while everyone agrees that washing before eating requires a blessing. Rambam too must regard the former obligation as stronger.

Rambam therefore teaches which blessings one should especially seek to incur personally in order to reach one hundred. He lists those whose obligation is stronger and more essential.

If a person happened to recite other blessings, such as because his attention lapsed and he needed to wash before prayer, those blessings certainly count toward the one hundred and may replace others.

Initially, however, he should seek to bring himself into obligation specifically for the blessings Rambam lists, because they are more significant and necessary.

Ordinarily it is forbidden to cause an unnecessary blessing, as stated in Yoma 70a and Magen Avraham, siman 215. To fulfill the mitzvah of one hundred blessings, however, we are not concerned for this, as Magen Avraham writes there.

Rambam therefore gives practical advice. For the blessings he lists, one should bring himself into obligation without concern for creating an unnecessary blessing. Beyond those, he should avoid unnecessary blessings. If he nevertheless incurred them, he must recite them and they count toward the one hundred. This appears correct.

Se'if 9, in the gloss: But the practice follows the latter opinion, etc. Gloss: See what Turei Zahav writes concerning the prayer text, “You are He from before the world was created,” etc. In truth, neither of the two readings is mistaken. In Midrash Rabbah, parashat Shemot, on the verse “Who is the Omnipresent, that I should heed His voice?” Moshe Rabbeinu, peace be upon him, is described as telling Pharaoh: “Our God; His power and might fill the world. He existed before the world was created and He will exist at the world's end.” See there. This accords with our text. But in parashat Beshalach, on “Then Moshe sang,” etc., the Midrash applies the verse “Your throne was established from of old,” etc.: “Thus Israel said: Before You created the world, You are He; and after You created the world, You are He.” This explicitly follows the text that Turei Zahav deletes. Turei Zahav objects that it is obvious that every agent exists before the act he performs. The objection presents no difficulty. This statement does not intend to teach merely that the Omnipresent, blessed is He, existed before the world. Rather, it rejects the heretics' belief that the Holy One, blessed is He, requires assistance from human beings below, like other celestial officers whose condition is changed by incense offered to them below, as it is written, “From the time we ceased burning incense to the queen of heaven, we have lacked everything.” We believe, however, that He, blessed is He, needs no assistance from any creature and undergoes no change. If, Heaven forbid, the Holy One, blessed is He, received some change from the world, He would not remain on the same level before creation and after creation, but would enter a different state. We therefore say, “You are He from before You created the world”: before creating the world You existed in one unchanging state, and after creating the world You remain continually in that same state; no change occurred in You. This is likewise the intent of the aforementioned Midrash in parashat Beshalach. In all His attributes, the Holy One, blessed is He, underwent no change from before the world's creation to afterward. He is, so to speak, the same before and after creation. Only one designation was newly introduced: at first He was described as standing, and afterward, through Israel, He was described as sitting. The two readings are therefore both precise. The reading “from before the world was created” refers to His very existence, blessed is He, and teaches that He precedes everything. In that context the passive form “was created” is appropriate, as Turei Zahav writes. Thus, in parashat Shemot, where Pharaoh denied His existence entirely and Moshe informed him of the fundamental reality of His existence, it is appropriate to say, “before the world was created.” The reading “from before You created,” however, teaches only that He remains the same before creation and afterward and undergoes no change. The active form is therefore appropriate, as at the sea: Israel's intent there was not to teach that He precedes creation, which is obvious, but that He underwent no new change, except that He came to be described as sitting. It is therefore appropriate to say, “before You created the world.” Neither reading should be rejected. See my work Ma'aseh Yedei Yotzer on the Pesach Haggadah, concerning the phrase “I am He and no other”; see there. Regarding the prayer's essential intent, it appears that it is not merely to say that the Holy One, blessed is He, precedes everything, but that He undergoes no change from before creation to afterward. This also explains the concluding language, “Sanctify Your name upon those who sanctify Your name, and through Your salvation raise our horn.” We declare that our prayer is not intended to bring You, Heaven forbid, any benefit. That is impossible, because no change occurs in You: You are He after the world was created and You are He from before the world was created; You receive neither increase nor diminution. Rather, we ask: “Sanctify Your name upon those who sanctify Your name”; for the sake of those who sanctify Your name, for our sake, sanctify Your name, etc. “Through Your salvation” (through the salvation extended to us) “raise our horn,” so that benefit will come to us, even though You gain nothing from the matter. Since this is the intent, the reading “before You created” should not be rejected. So it appears to me; examine carefully.
