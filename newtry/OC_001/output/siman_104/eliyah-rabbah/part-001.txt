[7] <b>“If he paused long enough to complete, etc.”</b> In Eliyahu Zuta I objected that the Bach disagrees with Shulchan Aruch here. He rules that one returns to the beginning only when the interruption resulted from a condition affecting the person himself, such as a place unfit for recitation because excrement was present, or urine flowing upon his knees. Since he should have taken care initially not to come to such a situation, he is regarded as negligent. Here, however, where the interruption arose from an external circumstance such as oxen or a wagon, he need not return to the beginning, because this is not called compulsion. Olas Tamid cites him this way. This is difficult, because above in siman 25 the Bach explicitly rules that there is a distinction between Krias Shema and prayer, and that in prayer one returns to the beginning even if the interruption was not caused by compulsion. Nachalas Tzvi there also rules in his name this way. This requires great study. The proof the Bach brings here from Tosafos, Berachos 33, who write that one does not return to the beginning, is also no proof in my humble opinion. Tosafos may follow the French rabbis cited by Beit Yosef in siman 65, who hold that even in prayer and even under complete compulsion one does not return to the beginning. Taz was later printed, and I saw that he objected to the Bach because Tosafos discuss a case where he did not pause long enough to complete the entire prayer, concerning which there is a dispute on page 23 according to Rav Ashi’s answer. I do not understand his reasoning. There, the view requiring one to return to the beginning is based on the fact that the person was unfit when he began to pray because he needed to relieve himself, as Rashi explains. Here, in the case of a scorpion, this does not apply, and Tosafos did not need to prove at all that he does not return to the beginning. This also resolves the objection of Levushei Yom Tov against Rama and Levush, who rule here without qualification, whereas in siman 68 they distinguish between an interruption caused by compulsion and one that was not. Lechem Chamudos also objects on pages 24, 27, and 40. According to what I have written, there is no difficulty, because there is a distinction between prayer and Krias Shema, and they rely upon the authorities who hold that in prayer one returns even without compulsion. I later saw that Levush writes in siman 85, regarding Krias Shema, that the same law applies to Hallel, prayer, and Megillah. This shows that he requires specifically an interruption caused by compulsion. Accordingly, it is obvious that Lechem Chamudos and Levushei Yom Tov are not objecting to Levush, for it is clear that here too he discusses compulsion and relies upon what he wrote above, as Levush explicitly writes in his glosses to simanim 65 and 85. Rama’s words are also resolved, for in siman 65 he explicitly writes, “And so it is in siman 104.” He therefore did not need to add a gloss here that the ruling applies only where there was compulsion. One could nevertheless say that his reference concerns how the duration is measured according to the reader. There is also proof from his writing in siman 23, seif 2, “See above, siman 85,” where he refers from the law of prayer to the law of Krias Shema, which implies that one returns to the beginning only where the interruption was caused by compulsion. Accordingly, one must say that here he discusses a condition affecting the person himself, as above in siman 65, whereas oxen and a wagon are not compulsion, as the Bach rules here. I saw, however, that Derishah writes that the Semag cited by Beit Yosef in siman 65 shows that anything not done voluntarily is called compulsion, unlike Beit Yosef’s citation of the Rashba that only where the person himself was disqualified is it called compulsion. According to this, Levush and Rama may indeed be speaking here even of oxen and a wagon, and one would return to the beginning. In truth, this requires study, for these are the words of Tosafos in Berachos 22, and there it is clear that the matter depends on whether the person was fit. This is also implied by the Tosafos rulings there, as well as by the Tur and Rabbeinu Yerucham. The language of the Rosh in the chapter Mi Shemeiso, however, implies the Derishah’s view, as does Abudraham. It further appears to me that even if we say that the matter depends upon the person’s fitness, one should nevertheless be stringent in prayer with every involuntary interruption, because according to the Rif and Rambam, if one paused long enough to complete the entire prayer he returns to the beginning even without compulsion. I found that Magen Avraham, siman 65, rules this way, especially according to my explanation there that this is also the Raavad’s view.

[8] <b>“If he spoke, etc.”</b> Levush writes, “If he transgressed and spoke, etc.,” which clearly refers to intentional speech. This is also implied by Beit Yosef’s statement that this was an unauthorized interruption. It is therefore difficult that Knesses HaGedolah explains the case as one where he spoke because of compulsion, such as when he saw a king. How does he reconcile Beit Yosef and Levush? Perhaps he understands them to mean that he could have shortened the prayer or moved aside, as at the beginning of this siman, in which case he was forbidden to speak but nevertheless did so. It is still called compulsion, though this explanation is strained. In any event, we learn from his words that one who speaks with full intent must return to the beginning even if he did not pause long enough to complete the entire prayer, as stated below in siman 114, seif 6. The Bach also rules this way, and this is the primary halachah. According to my explanation, all of Taz’s objections against Beit Yosef and Bach are resolved, and his lengthy discussion was unnecessary. Examine this carefully. Taz’s extended explanation of Abudraham, that the case is one where the person did not begin again from the start of the blessing but resumed where he had stopped, completed the prayer, and stepped back, and must therefore pray again from the beginning, is also forced. Nor does this appear correct as a matter of halachah. The Rashba disputes the basic ruling that one must return to the beginning of the blessing, holding that he need return only to the place where he stopped, as Beit Yosef writes, just as regarding Krias Shema in siman 64. We certainly should not be more stringent and require him to return to the beginning of the entire prayer on that account. Taz also objects from siman 114 and says that the reason will be explained there, but he does not explain it. Olas Tamid writes that the case there is worse because he changed the order of the prayer. This is unconvincing, for the Tur and Shulchan Aruch there write, “Whenever they said that he returns to the blessing in which he erred, this applies when the error was inadvertent, etc.” Perhaps the phrase “in which he erred” does not, in his view, include ordinary speech during prayer. Olas Tamid further rules that even after intentional speech one need not return because in cases of doubtful blessings we are lenient. This is difficult. The reason for leniency in doubtful blessings is that blessings are D'Rabbanan, yet he himself writes below in siman 107 that prayer is D'Oraisa and therefore a doubt is treated stringently. See Lechem Chamudos, page 23, and his proofs there. This requires study. If he interrupts between one blessing and another, it is clear that he resumes from the place where he stopped.
