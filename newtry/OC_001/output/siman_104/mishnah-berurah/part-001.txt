(9) During supplications. At that point it is permitted if moving serves some minor mitzvah need, such as our custom that immediately after the repetition of the Amidah the chazzan moves his feet and sits for Tachanun in places where Tachanun is recited sitting. He likewise ascends the bimah for Torah reading even though he has not yet reached the Kaddish containing “May their prayers be accepted,” during which he takes the three steps. Similarly, the Gemara states regarding Rabbi Akiva that one would leave him in one corner and find him in another because of his many bowings and prostrations, referring to the supplications after prayer. Without need, however, a person may not move from his place until he takes the three steps. The Olas Tamid writes that after completing the prayer, before saying “He who makes peace,” there is no prohibition against moving one’s feet for a minor mitzvah need, such as being honored with opening the Aron Kodesh while Avinu Malkeinu is recited, which people consider somewhat of a mitzvah. Nevertheless, it appears that he should first say, “May the words of my mouth be accepted.”

(16) To the beginning. According to our practice, following the Rama’s ruling above in siman 65 regarding Krias Shema, in accordance with the authorities who maintain that one returns to the beginning only when the interruption resulted from duress, the same rule applies to prayer. Some maintain, however, that our case, involving a scorpion or ox approaching him, or an interruption caused by robbers and the like, is also considered an interruption caused by duress, because at that time he was unable to pray, and he therefore returns to the beginning. Others maintain that this is not considered duress unless the person himself was unfit to pray or the place was unfit, as opposed to another type of duress. The Magen Avraham and many other Acharonim conclude that regarding prayer, other forms of duress are also considered duress, since some Rishonim maintain that during prayer, if one paused long enough to complete the whole prayer, he returns to the beginning even without any duress. Regarding Krias Shema and all other matters, however, duress exists only where the person or place was unfit.

(19) The blessing. The Chayei Adam writes that after the fact, if one did not return to the beginning of the blessing and has already completed it, he may not go back. Magen Gibborim disagrees entirely with the Shulchan Aruch’s ruling and writes that according to the essential law, if one did not pause long enough to complete the whole prayer, it is not considered an interruption and he returns only to the place where he stopped. Tosafos’ ruling that he returns to the beginning of the blessing concerns a case where he paused long enough to complete the whole prayer, and Tosafos maintain that this type of duress is not considered duress even regarding prayer. Biur HaGra agrees with the Rashba’s view cited in Beit Yosef. It appears from his words that in every case one returns only to the place where he stopped, unless he paused long enough to complete the whole prayer because of duress, in which case he returns to the beginning of the prayer.

(כה) כדין ההפסקות - ר"ל דאם שח באמצע ברכה שיחה מועטת ואפילו אם לא שהה עי"ז כדי לגמור אותה ברכה חוזר לראש הברכה ואם שהה ע"י השיחה כדי לגמור כל התפלה מראשו לסופו חוזר לראש התפלה ולפי מה שביארנו לעיל בסקט"ז לא יהיה דין זה רק בששח ע"י אונס כגון ע"י אנס וליסטים וכה"ג וכנ"ל אבל אם שח שלא באונס כ"א בשגגה ע"י איזה טעות או שסבר שהוא מותר אפילו שהה כדי לגמור כולה אינו חוזר לראש כ"א לתחלת הברכה ואם שח בין ברכה לברכה אע"ג דעשה איסור בזה מ"מ לא שייך בזה שום תיקון לכו"ע אלא מיד אחר השיחה גומר תפלתו ואם שח במזיד צע"ג בדין זה י"א דחוזר תיכף לראש התפלה אפילו בשיחה מועטת וי"א דלא שנא בין שוגג למזיד:
**** ENGLISH ****
(25) According to the laws of interruptions. Meaning, if he spoke briefly in the middle of a blessing, even if the speech did not delay him long enough to complete that blessing, he returns to the beginning of the blessing. If the speech delayed him long enough to complete the entire prayer from beginning to end, he returns to the beginning of the prayer. According to what we explained above in seif katan 16, this applies only where he spoke because of duress, such as because of a violent person, robbers, or the like. If he spoke without duress, but inadvertently because of an error or because he thought it was permitted, then even if he delayed long enough to complete the whole prayer, he returns only to the beginning of the blessing. If he spoke between blessings, although he committed a prohibition, all authorities agree that no corrective repetition applies, and immediately after speaking he continues his prayer. If he spoke deliberately, the law requires great study. Some maintain that he immediately returns to the beginning of the prayer even after brief speech, while others maintain that there is no distinction between inadvertent and deliberate speech.

**** ENGLISH ****
(29) He does not interrupt. If he did interrupt for this, or for Kaddish or Kedushah, his law is like that of one who deliberately spoke. According to the authorities who maintain that such a person returns to the beginning, he too returns to the beginning. If, however, he thought it was permitted to interrupt, he is considered inadvertent and does not return to the beginning, as explained above. If he was called to the Torah while standing after the Amidah, even if he has not yet begun Elokai Netzor, he interrupts and ascends, but he should be careful to say “May the words of my mouth be accepted” first.
