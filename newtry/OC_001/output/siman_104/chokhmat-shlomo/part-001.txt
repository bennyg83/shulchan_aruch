<b>Seif 1. </b>He should not interrupt, etc. Note: See Magen Avraham, seif katan 1, who writes concerning the incident with that pious man that it must be that he was certain, etc. This is likewise explained in Taz, siman 66. In truth, however, this is not correct, for how can one say that he was assured of this when free choice remains in the hands of the other person, and one may not rely on a miracle? In my opinion, the intent may be explained according to what is written in the work Maon HaBerachos regarding the officer's statement to him, 'Who would demand your blood from me?' The meaning is that he would not even be punished by Heaven, because the pious man would be considered one who knowingly destroys himself. See there. Accordingly, the pious man's answer may be understood as follows. Although it is forbidden for a person knowingly to destroy himself, and his sin is exceedingly grave, why is this so? A person may generally do as he wishes with what belongs to him, so why may he not relinquish his own life? The answer is that had man been created for his own sake, one could say that if he wished, he could relinquish his life. But man was created solely for the honor of the Creator and to serve Him. Just as a servant of a mortal king cannot say that he does not wish to serve, so too a person cannot waive Hashem's honor and service when Hashem desires his service. Yet this applies only when his death does not itself bring honor to the Creator. If, by knowingly accepting death, he also brings honor to the Creator, then although initially it is forbidden, since he has no authority to exchange one form of service for another and perhaps he was created for another task, nevertheless, after the fact he should not be punished, because through this as well he has brought honor to the Creator. Therefore, those who surrender their lives for Kiddush Hashem even where they are not obligated to do so are not considered to have knowingly destroyed themselves, even according to those who hold that one is forbidden to surrender his life. This is what the pious man told him. The officer's principal challenge was that he had acted like one who knowingly destroys himself, whose sin is exceedingly grave, and therefore asked how he was permitted to do so. The pious man answered that since his refusal to respond was intended for the honor of Hashem, as illustrated by the parable of a mortal king, and since here too he intended Hashem's honor, he at least does not have the status of one who knowingly destroys himself. Had the officer killed him, the officer would have been punished by Heaven, contrary to his statement, 'Who would demand your blood from me?' This is well explained, with Hashem's help. Alternatively, he answered that although with regard to commandments other than the three cardinal sins one may not surrender his life, that is because in those cases he is not standing before the King, and therefore there is no Kiddush Hashem except in those three matters. Prayer, however, is regarded as standing before the King. Therefore, even regarding a minor matter, there is Kiddush Hashem, even in private, analogous to, 'Will he also force the queen while I am in the house?' Thus, during prayer, when one stands before the King, even a minor matter resembles the three severe sins and is comparable to a public transgression, and one must surrender his life for it. This provides a sound resolution between the differing opinions, unlike Magen Avraham, who equates the cases. Here the matter is different. A further proof may be brought. Earlier in Berachos, concerning one who prayed behind the synagogue, Eliyahu came appearing as an Arab merchant, took a sword, and killed him. He was therefore liable to death, for Eliyahu certainly did not kill him unlawfully, although this transgression ordinarily carries no death penalty. Necessarily, because during prayer one stands before the King, even a seemingly minor violation is different and may warrant death. It is a subsidiary form of idolatry, because he appears as though two authorities stand before him. The poskim likewise write, as cited by the Rama in siman 16, that one who has relations with a non-Jewish woman must be killed rather than transgress. They derived this from the rule that zealots may strike him, demonstrating that it falls within the category of forbidden relations. Here too, since Eliyahu acted zealously and killed him, this demonstrates that the act is a subsidiary form of idolatry, and one must therefore be killed rather than transgress. Likewise, speaking to another person before the King constitutes rebellion against the kingdom, making it a subsidiary form of idolatry, for which one must be killed rather than transgress. This also explains very well why the pious man added and asked the officer, 'Had you answered him, what would they have done to you?' This seems superfluous, because the officer's initial answer, 'No,' would have sufficed for the pious man's a fortiori argument. Why did he need to continue and ask what would have been done to him had he answered? According to the above, it is clear. The officer's principal question was how the pious man had been permitted to place himself in mortal danger, thereby knowingly destroying himself. The pious man therefore answered that had one done so before the king, they would have cut off his head with a sword. Thus, this is a capital offense and an act of rebellion against the kingdom, making it a subsidiary form of idolatry for which one must surrender his life. Although it was stated earlier that one interrupts before gentile kings, this pious man disagreed and held that one must surrender his life. At the very least, according to our ruling, everyone agrees that one may be stringent with himself, unlike Magen Avraham. This appears correct to me.

<b>Seif 6. </b>If one spoke during prayer, the law concerning whether he must repeat it is the same as for other interruptions, etc. Note: Beit Yosef understood the view of Abudarham to be that if one spoke, he returns to the beginning of all eighteen blessings. He wrote that most poskim disagree. Taz explains Abudarham's words at length. I am astonished at all of them, for how did they overlook the Gemara in Taanis 3b? There Rabbi Chanina states that in the rainy season, if one omitted 'Who makes the wind blow,' he is not required to repeat; if he omitted 'Who causes rain to fall,' he must repeat. Moreover, even if he said, 'Who removes the wind and drives away the dew,' he is not required to repeat. This is difficult. Even granting that his request is treated as nonexistent because he did not restrain the rain, nevertheless, since he mentioned words unrelated to the prayer, this should constitute an interruption in the middle of prayer. It is no less than ordinary speech, which is an interruption. Necessarily, if one spoke in the middle, even during the first three blessings, he does not return to the beginning, and certainly not during the other blessings. This is a clear proof. While considering this, I observed that the Rif records this statement, but Rambam and the Rosh omit the conclusion of the Gemara that if he said, 'Who removes the wind and drives away the dew,' he need not repeat. Why did they omit it? This requires study. A possible resolution is that they agree with Abudarham that one who speaks returns to the beginning, but they maintain that this depends on the dispute in Berachos concerning one who paused long enough to complete the entire prayer. According to the view that where the delay was caused by circumstances beyond his control he returns to the beginning after pausing long enough to complete the entire prayer, one who speaks returns to the beginning even without such a delay. Actual speech constitutes a direct interruption even without a prolonged pause, just as a silent pause long enough to complete the entire prayer constitutes an interruption. The two are equivalent. When one merely pauses without action, the pause counts as an interruption only if it lasts long enough to complete the whole prayer. Without that duration, it is not regarded as a pause. But when one makes an actual interruption through speech, even a shorter period constitutes an interruption. Therefore, according to the opinion that even a pause long enough to complete the whole prayer does not require returning, although the full measure of interruption occurred, interruption does not invalidate the prayer. Consequently, speech also does not require returning. According to the opinion that a pause long enough to complete the prayer requires returning, interruption does invalidate in the middle. A shorter silent pause is harmless only because it is too brief to count as an interruption. Actual speech, however, certainly constitutes an interruption, and therefore he returns even without a prolonged delay. Rambam and the Rosh accordingly understood that Rabbi Chanina followed the opinion in Berachos that even after pausing long enough to complete the entire prayer one does not return. The same would then apply to an interruption by speech. According to our ruling, however, that one does return after a pause long enough to complete the entire prayer, the same applies when he interrupts through speech even without such a pause. They therefore omitted this ruling, because he must in any event repeat due to the interruption. Abudarham thus has support from Rambam and the Rosh. The Rif, by contrast, follows the poskim who hold that one who spoke does not return, and therefore records the ruling. Although the Rif rules that regarding Krias Shema one does not return even after pausing long enough to complete it, he nevertheless holds that regarding prayer one does return to the beginning after such a pause. He decided the practical halachah between Rabbi Abbahu and Rabbi Yochanan by distinguishing that in prayer one returns, whereas in Krias Shema one does not. The disputants themselves, however, make no such distinction. According to the one who says he does not return, even in prayer he does not return, and according to the one who says he returns, even in Krias Shema he returns. The Rif adopted a compromise for practical halachah, that in Krias Shema one does not return but in prayer one does. This is well explained. See also my work Tuv Taam VaDaas, second edition, Yoreh Deah, laws of interest, siman 160, in the responsum to the community of Tulchin, where I discussed incidentally the law of those in our time who speak in the vernacular during prayer. See there.
