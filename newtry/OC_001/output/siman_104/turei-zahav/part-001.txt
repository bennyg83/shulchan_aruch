<b>אבל יכול לילך כו'. </b> כב"י בשם הרר"י שלא מצינו הליכה שנקראת הפסק' בשום מקום עכ"ל. וקשה ממ"ש הטור והש"ע אבל בענין אחר אין לו לצאת ממקומו עד שיגמור תפלתו וההיא דר"ע כשהי' מתפלל בינו לבין עצמו אדם היה מניחו בזוית זו והי' מוצאו בזוית אחרת מרוב כריעו' והשתחויות בתחנוני' שלאחר התפלה היו עכ"ל וכ"כ הרא"ש משמע מזה דהליכה הוי הפסק וע"כ דהרר"י פליג ע"ז וא"כ להטור אין לו ללכת להשליך את הנחש כיון שאין סכנה והאיך הביא רמ"א זה לפסק הלכה. ואין להוכיח דגם הר"י ס"ל כהטור בזה מדפי' גם הוא דר"ע מיירי בתחנונים שלאחר התפל' כמ"ש ב"י זה אינו דהא דפי' הר"י כן משום קושיא אחרת דהיינו שהיה קשה לו דאין משתחוים באמצע הברכות אבל לא משום הוכחה דאין לצאת מן התפלה וכן התו' הביאו תי' זה על קושי' זאת  ודחוהו משום דמשמונה עשר' קאמר ותי' בע"א דהיינו באמצע הברכה מותר לשחות כו' מ"מ משמע גם מדבריהם דמותר לצאת מן התפלה של י"ת בשביל ההשתחוי' והנה ריב"ש סי' של"ב תופס כדברי הטור והרא"ש ואוסר לש"ץ לעקור בי"כ מן התפלה במה שכורעים בסדר עבודה ורמ"א מביאו לקמן והתימה עליו שכאן פסק כהרר"י שהליכה לא הוה הפסק בתפלה וצ"ע ועמ"ש סי' ח' סי"ג:

<b>However, he may walk, etc.</b> The Beit Yosef writes in the name of Rabbeinu Yonah that nowhere do we find walking defined as an interruption. This is difficult in light of the Tur and Shulchan Aruch, which state that for another purpose one may not leave his place until he completes his Amidah. They further explain that the incident of Rabbi Akiva, when he prayed privately and a person would leave him in one corner and later find him in another because of his many bowings and prostrations, occurred during the supplications after the Amidah. The Rosh writes likewise. This implies that walking is an interruption. Rabbeinu Yonah must therefore disagree, and according to the Tur one should not be permitted to walk in order to cast away a snake when there is no danger. How, then, could the Rama cite Rabbeinu Yonah as the practical ruling? One cannot prove that Rabbeinu Yonah agrees with the Tur merely because he too explains Rabbi Akiva’s conduct as occurring during supplications after the Amidah. Rabbeinu Yonah gave that explanation because of another difficulty, namely that one may not prostrate oneself in the middle of the blessings, not because he inferred that one may not move during the Amidah. Tosafos likewise cite this answer to that difficulty and reject it because the passage refers to the Eighteen Blessings. They answer instead that one may bow in the middle of a blessing. Their words also imply that one may move during the Amidah for the purpose of prostration. The Rivash, responsum 332, adopts the position of the Tur and the Rosh and prohibits the chazzan on Yom Kippur from moving during the Amidah for the prostrations in the Avodah service, and the Rama cites this below. It is therefore astonishing that here the Rama rules like Rabbeinu Yonah that walking is not an interruption in the Amidah. This requires study. See what I wrote in siman 8, seif 13.

<b>He returns to the beginning.</b> Although in siman 65 the ruling concerning Krias Shema is that he need return only to the point where he stopped, the Amidah is more stringent. The Rif proves this from the ruling in the chapter “Mi Shemeiso” concerning water flowing upon one’s knees, where if he paused, etc., he returns to the beginning, while concerning Krias Shema he returns to the point where he stopped. The Rif brings proof that this is the conclusion at the end of tractate Rosh Hashanah, and likewise regarding Hallel, shofar blasts, and the Megillah. Tosafos, the Rosh, and the Tur, however, do not distinguish between Krias Shema and the Amidah. Rather, they distinguish between a pause caused by coercion, which is more severe, and another pause. The Rama rules this way in siman 65. Here the Tur did not need to state that one who paused long enough to complete the entire prayer returns to the beginning, because he already wrote in siman 85, also regarding the Amidah, that there is a distinction between a pause caused by coercion and one not so caused. The Shulchan Aruch’s ruling here that if one paused he returns to the beginning is accepted by all. Even the Rosh and Tur agree, differing only in the reason. According to the Shulchan Aruch, the reason is the greater stringency of the Amidah. According to the Rosh and Tur, it is because the pause was caused by coercion. The Rama therefore had no need to comment on the Shulchan Aruch here. My father-in-law, the Bach, needlessly disputes the Shulchan Aruch and Rama here, maintaining that even if one paused long enough to complete the entire prayer he returns only to the point where he stopped. He relies on Tosafos in the chapter “Ein Omdin,” page 34, concerning one who interrupts the Amidah because of a scorpion. Tosafos write that they did not find that one who interrupted returns to the beginning, since the Gemara does not say that if he interrupted he returns to the beginning, but only that he returns to the beginning of the blessing. In truth, Tosafos discuss a case in which he interrupted without pausing long enough to complete the entire prayer. There is a dispute concerning such a case in the chapter “Mi Shemeiso,” page 24, according to Rav Ashi’s answer, and here everyone agrees that he returns to the point where he stopped. If, however, he paused long enough to complete the entire prayer, it is obvious that according to all opinions he returns to the beginning.

<b>If one spoke during the Amidah, etc.</b> The Beit Yosef cites Abudraham as follows: Rabbi Gershom son of Shlomo was asked whether one who spoke during the Amidah must repeat it, and if he must repeat, whether he returns to the beginning or only to the blessing in which he spoke. They answered that he must return to the beginning, because all eighteen blessings are considered one blessing. Orchos Chaim writes likewise in the name of the Raavad. The Beit Yosef comments that the authorities do not appear to agree. Although one might distinguish that the interruptions discussed elsewhere were permitted while this interruption was forbidden, this does not appear sufficient to require returning to the beginning. Nor is the statement that all eighteen blessings are considered one correct, for only the first three are considered one unit, and likewise the last three. The Mechaber therefore rules here that speech is treated like the other interruptions. I find an even greater difficulty: the Raavad’s ruling here contradicts what the Beit Yosef cites in his name in siman 65, where he follows Tosafos, the Rosh, and the Tur that if one paused without coercion, even long enough to complete the entire prayer, he need return only to the point where he stopped. Such a pause without coercion appears equivalent to the forbidden speech discussed here. Why, then, does the Raavad rule here that one who spoke must return to the beginning? My father-in-law, the Bach, writes that Rabbi Gershom discusses deliberate speech, and that in such a case all eighteen blessings are treated as one. He proves this from the Tur in siman 114, citing Avi HaEzri, that whenever the Sages say one returns to the place of his error, that applies to an inadvertent error, but if one erred deliberately he returns to the beginning. He therefore states that the Beit Yosef inadvertently erred, since the Beit Yosef himself rules in siman 114 like Avi HaEzri, which accords with Rabbi Gershom here, yet here he disputes Rabbi Gershom. I am astonished, because according to this the Tur too would have erred, Heaven forbid. In siman 65 the Tur rules that whether in the Amidah or in Krias Shema, if one paused, even long enough to complete the whole prayer, but not because of coercion, he returns only to the point where he stopped. A pause not caused by coercion would then be deemed deliberate. This would contradict Avi HaEzri, whose ruling the Tur himself cites in siman 104. I have another difficulty according to the Beit Yosef’s understanding of Rabbi Gershom and the Raavad. The Raavad’s own rulings would contradict one another, since in siman 65 the Beit Yosef cites the Rashba in the Raavad’s name as following the Tur’s view that one who paused long enough to complete the entire prayer without coercion returns only to the point where he stopped. Why, then, does the Raavad rule here that one who spoke returns to the beginning? One cannot answer that spoken interruption is worse than silent delay, for the Rashba explicitly writes in responsum 244, cited by the Rama in siman 690 concerning the Megillah, that even if one paused and spoke, he returns only to the point where he stopped. The Megillah and the Amidah are equal according to the approach of the Tur and Raavad, who distinguish between a pause caused by coercion and another pause, as the Tur states explicitly in siman 85. It appears to me that according to the Beit Yosef’s understanding, Rabbi Gershom and the Raavad discuss one who spoke in the middle of a blessing and is about to resume immediately. In that situation the Mechaber correctly rules that he need return only to the beginning of that blessing, for the speech is no worse than omitting the entire blessing, which is corrected by reciting it. This applies equally whether the speech was inadvertent or deliberate. If the speech lasted long enough to complete the entire prayer, it is governed by the general laws of an extended interruption discussed above. All agree with this. Rabbi Gershom, however, did not discuss that case. This is evident from his wording, “or does he return to that blessing in which he spoke.” Had he meant one who had not yet resumed after speaking, he should have said, “or does he return to the beginning of the blessing in which he spoke,” as the Tur does here when discussing how one should proceed immediately after speaking. Rather, Rabbi Gershom discusses one who spoke, completed the Amidah, and moved his feet. The question was rooted in the Mishnah’s statement that even if a king greets him he may not respond. If he violated this rule and responded, what is his status, whether the act was inadvertent or deliberate? We find a parallel regarding writing the Divine Name in Yoreh Deah, siman 276, where one may not respond even to the greeting of a Jewish king, and the Beit Yosef discusses whether a Sefer Torah remains fit if the scribe nevertheless responded. Here too, the questioner asked Rabbi Gershom whether, after completing the whole Amidah, the speech invalidates the prayer. The doubt likely arises from siman 114 concerning additions required in the Amidah, such as Yaaleh Veyavo on Rosh Chodesh. If one omitted it and has not moved his feet, he returns to Retzei; if he moved his feet, he returns to the beginning. Avi HaEzri writes that if the omission was deliberate, even without moving his feet he is treated as though he had moved them and requires a more extensive correction. The reason will be explained in siman 114, with Hashem’s help. The question here is whether forbidden speech should be treated like omitting Yaaleh Veyavo, since in both cases the Amidah was not recited in its prescribed form, or whether speech differs because he did recite everything required and merely introduced an interruption. Had he remembered immediately after the interruption, he could have corrected it by restarting that blessing, and the interruption would then cause no further harm. Once he completed the Amidah, however, perhaps he should not be required to pray a second time merely because of the interruption, whether the interruption consisted of delay or speech. Rabbi Gershom answered that the interruption is indeed like omitting Yaaleh Veyavo or another required passage, which requires returning to the beginning when one has moved his feet, for it is as though he did not recite the blessing of Retzei at all. The same applies here. All the blessings of the Amidah are indispensable to one another. If one recited only seventeen, he did not fulfill his obligation. Likewise, the blessing interrupted by speech is treated as though it was not recited at all, because its prescribed text was spoiled, leaving him with only seventeen blessings. He therefore returns to the beginning after moving his feet. This fully resolves the Beit Yosef’s objection to Rabbi Gershom’s statement that all eighteen blessings are one. The issue is not the rule that only the first three and last three are grouped together. Once he has completed the Amidah and moved his feet, omission of any one blessing means that he has recited only seventeen, and all are invalid because eighteen are mutually indispensable. Rabbi Gershom and the Raavad therefore ruled correctly. The Beit Yosef and the Shulchan Aruch likewise agree with this law, but because the Beit Yosef did not explain their words in this manner, he thought to dispute and reject them. In truth, all their paths are peaceful according to this explanation, with Hashem’s help. It further appears to me that one who spoke between blessings requires no corrective repetition, even immediately after the speech, but simply continues the Amidah. Therefore the Sages said that if possible he should shorten his prayer, implying that if he cannot, he continues. If, however, he paused long enough to complete the entire prayer, he returns to the beginning even where he spoke between blessings. Speech constitutes an interruption only in the middle of a blessing, because it spoils the blessing’s text. Between blessings, although speaking is forbidden, it does not spoil the blessings, and he completes the Amidah unless the pause lasted long enough to complete the whole prayer, in which case the sections no longer combine. This resembles one who speaks during a meal, as the Rashba compares the cases in responsum 244 concerning the Megillah. A pause long enough to complete the whole prayer resembles food having been digested, which requires a new blessing of Hamotzi. Ordinary speech within a blessing, however, spoils that blessing itself.
