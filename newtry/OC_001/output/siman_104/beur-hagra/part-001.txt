**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Seif 3, “But he may walk, etc.”</b> For we do not find that walking is called an interruption. This is the view of Rabbeinu Yonah, who explains “he should not interrupt” to mean by speech. He follows his own position, for he writes: “And in the Yerushalmi it says that even if a snake was coming toward him, he should move aside, and this is not an interruption.” The correct reading there is: “If it was threatening and coming toward him, he moves it away from before him, provided that he does not interrupt his prayer.” The word “moves it away” is used as in “from the side of the Mishkan.” This means that the ruling concerns specifically a snake already coiled around him, where walking will not help because it is already coiled around him. But if it is approaching him and he can move aside, he should do so, because walking is not an interruption. This is not because of danger, since an ordinary snake does not constitute danger. This is the meaning of “provided that he does not, etc.” Regarding Rabbi Akiva’s bowing there, he follows Tosafos’ explanation. Although he agreed with the Rosh’s explanation that this occurred after the prayer, because he found the prohibition of bowing difficult, as Tosafos did, according to the Rosh’s explanation cited in the preceding siman it would indeed constitute an interruption. Tosafos on 33a and the Rosh explain the Yerushalmi differently, that the animal was enraged and dangerous, as they wrote in Shulchan Aruch, “and likewise a snake, etc.” The phrase “provided that he does not, etc.” means that he can avoid it by walking, as stated there regarding a donkey and wagon, and as explained above regarding gentile kings.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Seif 5, “If he paused, etc.”</b> This follows the ruling of the Rif and Shulchan Aruch, who distinguish between Krias Shema and prayer, as the Rashba writes in his novellae. According to Tosafos and the gloss, however, which distinguish between a person or place that was disqualified and one that was not, the Bach writes that only where the person or place was disqualified must he return to the beginning. Therefore Tosafos, the Rosh, and the Tur did not write here that one who paused must return to the beginning. See Magen Avraham, siman 65, seif katan 2.

<b>“He returns to the beginning of, etc.”</b> Tosafos, s.v. “But, etc.” The Rashba already wondered why this differs from the case of urine flowing upon his knees, where the ruling is that he returns to the place where he stopped, and likewise where he needs to sneeze and says, “Master, etc.,” after which he returns to the place where he stopped. This applies even in the middle of a blessing, just as in Krias Shema one may interrupt in the middle of a blessing to inquire after someone out of honor or fear, and then returns to the place where he stopped. Beit Yosef’s answer that prayer is more stringent is untenable, for the Yerushalmi, cited by Tosafos on 34a, s.v. “From where, etc.,” derives a difficulty from prayer to Krias Shema. We also say at the beginning of the second chapter, regarding one who reads in order to proofread, that although he makes a lengthy interruption between one paragraph and another with unrelated matters, this does not invalidate it. One cannot distinguish between an optional interruption and one for a mitzvah. See Magen Avraham, siman 25, seif katan 17. Likewise, regarding Rabbi Yehudah HaNasi, it states that he returned and completed it, even though he made a lengthy interruption between the first verse and the rest of the paragraph. This is the Rashba’s view. Similarly, if one heard nine shofar blasts at intervals, etc., it is valid, although the entire order is considered one blessing.
