<b>(ס"ק א) מלך כו'</b> משום הפסד ממון א"צ להפסיק עס"ג שהביא מ"א ס"ק ג' בשם סמ"ג דאין להפסיק כ"א היכי דאיכא סכנת נפשות וכן הביא דברי סמ"ג הללו לעיל סי' צ"ב סס"ק ב' וסי' ק"ח סס"ח דאין לעבור זמן תפלה משום הפסד ממון וס"ל למ"א דה"ה להפסיק בתפלה דינו כך:

ומעשה דאותו חסיד בברכות דף ל"ב ע"ב בחסיד אחד התפלל ובא הגמון א' ונתן לו שלום ולא השיבו והמתין לו ההגמון עד שגמר תפלתו אח"ז שאלו למה לא השיבו הלא התחייב בנפשו ופייסו בדברים ונתפייס ונפטר החסיד בשלום מ"מ תקשי להפוסקי' דס"ל להחמיר על עצמו כה"ג. א"כ למה לא השיבו ושמא לא ימתין עד אחר התפל' שיהי' לו זמן לפייסו אלא יהרגנו מיד או לא יקבל פיוס:
**** ENGLISH ****
Regarding the incident of that pious man in Berachos 32b, a pious man was praying when an official came and greeted him, and he did not respond. The official waited until he completed his prayer, afterward asked him why he had not responded, since he had endangered his life, and the pious man appeased him with words. The official was appeased, and the pious man departed in peace. Nevertheless, according to the poskim who maintain that one may be stringent upon himself in such a case, it remains difficult why he did not respond, since perhaps the official would not wait until after the prayer and allow him time to appease him, but would kill him immediately or refuse to accept his explanation.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And this is the widespread custom, etc.</b> This refers to the chazzan. It is explained below, at the end of siman 123, that when he prays aloud he does not say at the end of the prayer, “May the words of my mouth be accepted,” because he relies upon “May their prayers be accepted,” which he will say in the Kaddish after the prayer. Accordingly, everything the chazzan says until Kaddish is comparable to the supplications after prayer, and its law is like that of an individual reciting Elokai Netzor after prayer, before “May the words of my mouth be accepted.” Nevertheless, the custom is that the chazzan moves his feet when reciting Tachanun and sits, as explained in siman 131, seif 2, that it is proper to sit while reciting Tachanun. The same applies when he ascends the bimah with the Sefer Torah.

<b>ועב"ח ר"ל דהרב"י חלק על תשו' הר"ג ב"ש.</b> הובא בט"ז דפסק ששיחה הוי הפסק וכן פסק בו בש"ע. דהא הרב"י גופי' פסק לקמן סי' קי"ד דאם במזיד אמר בימות הקיץ משיב הרוח או ותן טל ומטר חוזר לראש וכי גרע אמירת משיב הרוח או ותן טל ומטר משיח' ולכן חלק הב"ח על הרב"י ופסק דגם שיחה במזיד צריך לחזור לראש וכ' דכן היה דעת הרר"ג ב"ש אבל ודאי הרב"י ס"ל הכא דאפי' שח במזיד אינו חוזר לראש כנראה מדבריו שכ' כאן בחבורו הארוך ע"ד הרר"ג ב"ש ע"ש. בעובדא דר"א כו' דף כ"ד ע"ב דר"א הוי אזיל בתרי' דר"י והוי קרי ק"ש כי מטי למבואות המטונפות אשתיק עד שיצא מן אותה מבוי. וא"ל לר"י להיכי אחזור. א"ל ר"י אם שהיתה כדי לגמור כולה חוזר לראש. ות"ל אפי' לא שהה כדי לגמור כולה הוא שח דהא שאלו להיכי אחזור אע"כ דבק"ש אפי' שח במזיד א"צ לחזור לראש:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And see Bach, meaning that the Beit Yosef disagreed with the responsum of Rabbi Gershom ben Shlomo.</b> It is cited by the Taz, who ruled that speech constitutes an interruption, and he likewise ruled this way in the Shulchan Aruch. For the Beit Yosef himself ruled below, siman 114, that if one deliberately said “He causes the wind to blow” in summer, or “Grant dew and rain,” he returns to the beginning. Is saying “He causes the wind to blow” or “Grant dew and rain” less serious than ordinary speech? Therefore, the Bach disagreed with the Beit Yosef and ruled that one who deliberately spoke must also return to the beginning, and wrote that this was likewise the view of Rabbi Gershom ben Shlomo. Certainly, however, the Beit Yosef here maintains that even one who deliberately spoke does not return to the beginning, as is evident from what he wrote in his longer work regarding the words of Rabbi Gershom ben Shlomo. See there. Regarding the incident of Rabbi Abbahu, Berachos 24b, Rabbi Abbahu was walking behind Rabbi Yochanan and reciting Krias Shema. When he reached filthy alleyways he stopped until he left the alley. He then asked Rabbi Yochanan, “To where should I return?” Rabbi Yochanan answered, “If you paused long enough to complete all of it, return to the beginning.” Yet even had he not paused that long, he had spoken, since he asked where to return. It follows that during Krias Shema, even deliberate speech does not require returning to the beginning.

בי"ד סי' צ"ט ס"ק ט' דקיי"ל אסור לבטל איסור לכתחלה ואם ביטל בשוגג מותר ואם ביטל במזיד אסור: ואם היה סבור שמותר לבטלו וביטלו מקרי שוגג ע"ש. ועיין במכות ריש פ"ב דף ז' ע"ב ת"ר בחייבי גלות כתיב בשגגה פרט למזיד ופריך הש"ס פשיטא בר קטלא הוא ומשני רבא באו' מותר שאני או' אומר מותר קרוב למזיד ולכך קראו בריית' מזיד. וכתבו התו' בד"ה אלא כו' וז"ל וקשה דגבי חלב וגבי שבת כתיב בשניהם בשגג' וחייבים בתינוק שנשבה לבין הגוים דה"ל אומר מותר וי"ל דשאני הכא גבי גלות דכתיב בשגג' יתיר' למעט או' מותר עכ"ל. הרי דבעלמא או' מותר שוגג הוא וגלות שאני דאיכ' קרא יתיר':
**** ENGLISH ****
In Yoreh Deah, siman 99, seif katan 9, we rule that one may not intentionally nullify a forbidden substance. If he nullified it inadvertently it is permitted, but if deliberately, it is forbidden. If he thought it was permitted to nullify it and did so, he is considered inadvertent. See there. See also Makkos 7b, where the Sages taught concerning those liable to exile that the Torah states “unintentionally,” excluding one who acted deliberately. The Gemara asks that this is obvious, since such a person is liable to death, and Rava answers that it refers to one who says the act was permitted, for such a person is close to deliberate, and therefore the baraisa calls him deliberate. Tosafos explain that although regarding forbidden fat and Shabbos the Torah also says “unintentionally,” one raised among non-Jews who believes the act permitted is liable, since ordinarily one who says it is permitted is considered inadvertent. Exile is different because there is an additional verse excluding him. Thus, in general, one who believes an act permitted is considered inadvertent.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And in the Chiddushei Rashba at the beginning of the second chapter of Berachos, etc.</b> He brings proof that regarding Krias Shema, even if one interrupted and paused long enough to complete it all, he has fulfilled his obligation. He writes that this is also implied by the incident involving Rabbi, Berachos 13b, who recited the first verse of Krias Shema and then taught his students. When he finished teaching, he returned and completed Krias Shema, according to Rabbi Shimon son of Rabbi. Yet even if the delay itself was not an interruption, the speech involved in teaching his students was certainly an interruption. It follows that speech during Krias Shema does not invalidate it.
