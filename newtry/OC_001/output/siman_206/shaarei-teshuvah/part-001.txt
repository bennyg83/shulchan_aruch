<b>(•)</b> In Shulchan Aruch, seif 1: If one recited Borei Peri HaAdamah over fruits of a tree, he has fulfilled his obligation, etc. See Rishon LeTzion, who writes that one should recite Shehakol because we follow Rabbi Yehudah only after the fact. Birkei Yosef writes that Borei Peri HaAdamah is preferable because it is more specific. See Shaagas Aryeh, siman 23, who discusses whether a God-fearing person should initially be concerned for the reading of older manuscripts of Rambam, according to which he has not fulfilled his obligation. A practical difference would arise where one is uncertain whether to recite Borei Peri HaEtz or Borei Peri HaAdamah, for according to that reading he should not recite Borei Peri HaAdamah because of the doubt. Shaagas Aryeh concludes that the correct reading in Rambam is as Kesef Mishneh records it, that he has fulfilled his obligation. He also proves this from Ulla's statement that the dispute applies where their blessings are equal, etc., for Ulla follows Rabbi Yehudah, who holds that he has fulfilled his obligation. See there. In Shulchan Aruch, seif 2: If he intended to discharge the fruit of the tree with Borei Peri HaAdamah, he has fulfilled his obligation. See Magen Avraham, who refers to siman 211, where it is explained that one should not intentionally do this. Ateres Zekeinim writes likewise.<br><b>(1)</b> <b>He has not fulfilled his obligation.</b> See Baer Heitev and Yad Ephraim on the words of Even HaOzer. The caper does not prove the law regarding a fruit that is not the plant's primary fruit; it proves only the law regarding an unripe fruit, and for that purpose the proof from unripe grapes is valid. Eliyah Rabbah likewise writes only concerning completion of the fruit. See Birkei Yosef, who rejects even this proof from unripe grapes, because Borei Peri HaAdamah is nevertheless the more accurate blessing: Borei Peri HaEtz implies that this is the tree's primary fruit. Even so, if one recited Borei Peri HaEtz after the fact, he fulfilled his obligation. Panim Meiros, siman 58, writes that over unripe grapes and similar produce one should recite Borei Peri HaEtz to satisfy Rambam, but this is incorrect, for the accurate reading in Rambam is as Kesef Mishneh records it, that Borei Peri HaAdamah recited over a tree fruit is effective. See there. Panim Meiros further rules that if one recited Borei Peri HaAdamah over ground produce that is better cooked than raw, whose proper blessing when raw is Shehakol, he has fulfilled his obligation after the fact. The reverse is plainly also true: produce that is better raw than cooked, over which he mistakenly recited Borei Peri HaAdamah after cooking, is effective. It appears that if one is uncertain whether a fruit is better raw or cooked, he should recite Borei Peri HaAdamah both before and after cooking, because that blessing is effective after the fact. Although Shehakol would also be effective after the fact, Borei Peri HaAdamah is more specific, as cited above in the name of Birkei Yosef. The ruling at the end of siman 204, that one who is uncertain about a blessing recites Shehakol, refers to a case where Borei Peri HaAdamah or Borei Peri HaEtz would not be effective even after the fact. Here, Borei Peri HaAdamah is preferable because it is effective after the fact. See Taz, who asks why the Shulchan Aruch specifies that both items were before him, since even if he intended to discharge the other item the same law applies, as stated below in seif 5. Shaagas Aryeh, siman 27, writes that Taz went too far, for there the two blessings are both Borei Peri HaEtz. Here, even if both were before him, Rashi and Tosafos imply that the blessing over radish does not discharge an olive. He rules like Rashi, that Borei Peri HaAdamah does not discharge the tree fruit and one must recite Borei Peri HaEtz. Birkei Yosef cites Shaagas Aryeh and explains the sugya in accordance with Shulchan Aruch. His reasoning is persuasive: since Borei Peri HaAdamah over a tree fruit is effective after the fact, when one holds a ground fruit and specifically intends to discharge the tree fruit, he has fulfilled his obligation, for explicit intention is powerful. Rabbeinu Yonah's words explicitly support Taz, as do Kol Bo, Orchos Chaim in the name of Rashba, Rama in Darchei Moshe, and Peri Chadash in his glosses. Therefore, when both items were before him, one should not repeat the blessing, following Shulchan Aruch, because in doubtful blessings we rule leniently. Initially, he should decide not to eat the tree fruit immediately, for no blessing was recited in vain, since the ground fruit's blessing certainly took effect. The same course is proper even if the tree fruit was not before him, to avoid the dispute. Eliyah Rabbah explains that intention is required; if no intention was present, even when both were before him, Borei Peri HaAdamah does not discharge the tree fruit. This resolves Taz's question. Panim Meiros, part 1, siman 58, rules likewise. He further writes that if both an item requiring Shehakol and an item requiring Borei Peri HaEtz or Borei Peri HaAdamah were before him, and he mistakenly blessed first over the Shehakol item, the others are not discharged without intention. Indeed, Beit Yosef cites Rashba that even where the blessings are equal, intention may be required. Therefore, after reciting Shehakol, he must still recite each item's proper blessing. See Responsa Rivash, siman 384, who discusses meat and cheese before a person, whose blessings are equal, and asks whether the inability to eat them together because of prohibition would require a second blessing. It appears that this concerns a case where he intended, or at least had general intent, to eat the meat after cleansing and rinsing his mouth; otherwise, it would in any event be a new decision.
