<small>On the Mechaber, seif 2. </small><b>But one who is riding.</b> See Magen Avraham, Taz, Peri Chadash, and Chemed Moshe. In my humble opinion, it also requires study why Rambam, in the first chapter of Hilchos Biyas HaMikdash, omitted the rule concerning Italian wine, although Rambam records all the relevant laws. It therefore appears that both answers of the Gemara are difficult. According to the first answer, if Italian wine is more intoxicating, why did the baraisa say that we learned from Rabban Gamliel that a revi'is of Italian wine intoxicates? Ordinary wine also intoxicates. It should have said that Italian wine intoxicates more. According to the second answer, if Italian wine is no different from other wine and the distinction is only that riding is less effective, as Chemed Moshe explains, why did Rabban Gamliel specify Italian wine? What difference would there be between it and any other wine? I therefore suggest the following principle. The rule in Kerisos that separated drinks do not combine because the wine was not consumed at once, as Magen Avraham writes in seif katan 1, applies only to the initial revi'is. Once a full revi'is was consumed at once, whatever else he subsequently drinks combines so that he is treated as having consumed more than a revi'is. See below, siman 128, seif 38. But if none of the wine was consumed at once, even several half-revi'is portions do not combine to make him one who drank more than a revi'is. See Kesef Mishneh, Hilchos Biyas HaMikdash 1:5, who makes a similar distinction regarding diluted wine. Rabban Gamliel's precise reference to Italian wine can now be understood in two ways. Either Italian wine intoxicates more, and he wished to teach his students why more than a mil was required, though the wording of the baraisa would then be difficult, for it should have said that Italian wine intoxicates more. Alternatively, Rabban Gamliel had also drunk other wine. He knew that the other wine did not amount to a revi'is, but was uncertain whether the cup of Italian wine contained a revi'is, which he drank at once. If it did, the other wine would combine with it and he would be treated as having consumed more than a revi'is, for which one mil would not suffice. The Sages therefore properly said that they learned from him that a revi'is of Italian wine intoxicates, for otherwise the other wine would not have combined to make more than a revi'is. It need not be more intoxicating; indeed, the fact that this law had to be learned from the incident may indicate otherwise. When the Gemara challenged from the baraisa, it could have answered that Rabban Gamliel had drunk more than a revi'is, but that would raise the difficulty that travel itself further disturbs one who drank more. To avoid that, it adopted the strained answer that Italian wine intoxicates more and that he did not drink more than a revi'is, assuming that the road would not further disturb him because the actual quantity was no more than a revi'is. When the Gemara then asked that one who drank more than a revi'is is also further disturbed by travel, it had to answer that riding is like sitting at home: one waits until the wine dissipates, each person according to his own condition. The conclusion no longer requires distinguishing Italian wine from other wines. Rabban Gamliel had drunk more than a revi'is, but because he rode, travel did not disturb him. Rif and Rambam therefore omitted both the stringency of Italian wine and the law of riding, whether as a leniency, that riding does not worsen the condition of one who drank more than a revi'is, or as a stringency, that riding one mil does not suffice for one who drank a revi'is. Rabban Gamliel's wording, 'until our wine dissipates,' without a fixed measure, is precise, indicating that each person must assess himself. Rambam and Rosh are thus well understood. Halachos Gedolos, Tur, and Shulchan Aruch, however, must explain that riding certainly allows the road to dissipate the wine; they only mean that riding does not tire him as walking does, and the wine therefore dissipates through the passage of time, as Magen Avraham writes.
