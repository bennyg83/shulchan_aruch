**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Seif 1</b> One who did not make a sukkah, whether inadvertently or intentionally, etc. Note: Regarding the law if the sukkah became invalid on Yom Tov or on Shabbat, whether it is permitted to repair it through a gentile, in my humble opinion it is certainly forbidden to do so through a gentile because of making a tent. Although afterward I saw in two places in the Peri Megadim that he wrote that one can say that where he has no sukkah in which to eat, it is like preparation for food needs and is permitted, and he noted to see siman 514, meaning that there it is explained that one may sweep the house when he has no place to eat; see there. His words are astonishing. It is understandable there, for if he has no house, he truly will have no place to eat, and therefore this is preparation for food needs. But with sukkah, if he has no place to eat in a sukkah, he is permitted to eat this way without a sukkah. If so, it is not preparation for food needs, since he can eat in any case. Therefore it appears that it is forbidden to build the sukkah on Yom Tov through a gentile. True, according to the Ba'al HaMaor, who holds that dismantling a sukkah is only rabbinic since it is a temporary tent, see there in the sugya of not taking wood from a sukkah, then its building is also only rabbinic. One could again say that through a gentile it is a rabbinic prohibition upon a rabbinic prohibition, which is permitted in a place of mitzvah. However, since this is not agreed upon, one should not desecrate Yom Tov with this, and one should not do it through a gentile, as appears correct in my humble opinion. Examine carefully. Regarding what else requires examination in several matters in this siman, see my work on Orach Chayim from the year 607, in the laws of Eruvin, siman 362, in the sugya of Eruvin 44, what I wrote there. Also regarding the principle that since it is a wall for Shabbat it is a wall for sukkah, explained in siman 632, see there and examine carefully.

<b>(There, seif 3)</b> A stolen sukkah is valid, etc. Note: The Magen Avraham brought in this siman the view of Sefer Yere'im, that theft from a gentile, even if permitted, is nevertheless not called "yours"; see there. I have a very great astonishment about his words from the Talmud in Sotah 43b, where it asks regarding what we say, "he dedicated it and did not inaugurate it" excludes a robber: let us say this is not like R. Yose HaGelili, who says that "the fearful and fainthearted" means one who is afraid because of transgressions in his hand. It answers: even if you say it is R. Yose HaGelili, it is where he repented and paid money. If so, he should have bought it and then returned, since from the outset it came into his hand as theft. End quote of the Talmud. This is difficult: what forced it to answer this? It would have been better to answer that it speaks of theft from a gentile; if so there is no transgression, and nevertheless it is not his and is not called "he inaugurated it," and therefore he does not return. R. Yose could hold like the one who says theft from a gentile is permitted, and the one who says theft from a gentile is forbidden could hold like the rabbis, who do not accept "one who is afraid because of transgressions in his hand." It would then accord with everyone either way. Perforce it is proven that if theft from a gentile is permitted, it is truly his, it is his house, and it is like an actual purchase. Why should he not return? This is proof against the author of Yere'im, and it requires great examination. Examine carefully. While on this subject, another difficulty arose for me from the Talmud in Sukkah 33, where we say that one fulfills with a sukkah on stolen land because it holds that land is not stolen, and it is considered a borrowed sukkah. This is difficult: since we rule that a mitzvah that comes through a transgression is invalid, either way there is a problem. If with stolen land there is the prohibition of "you shall not steal," then it is a mitzvah that comes through a transgression, so why does one fulfill with a stolen sukkah? And if with land, since it is not stolen, there is no prohibition of "you shall not steal," then what does the Talmud ask in Sotah, to say it is not like R. Yose HaGelili, who says "one who is afraid because of transgressions in his hand"? One can say it accords even with R. Yose HaGelili and speaks of stolen land, where there is no prohibition of "you shall not steal." Then there is no transgression, and therefore the Torah invalidated a stolen sukkah so that even others are invalid. But at first glance this is not so: if he is pleased for a mitzvah to be fulfilled with his property, then it is like borrowed, and a borrowed sukkah is valid. However, it works well. One can say that certainly, since it is written "for yourself," it implies that it is yours. With a borrowed sukkah, since he lends it to him knowingly, not only according to the one who says ownership of produce-rights is like ownership of the body is it certainly his with regard to the produce-rights; even according to the one who says it is not like ownership of the body, that applies only where ownership of the body is required, such as with first fruits, where one must say "the land that You gave me" and we require the body of the land to be his, so ownership of produce-rights is not like ownership of the body. But with sukkah, where the mitzvah is only sitting and we require only that the sitting be his, ownership of produce-rights is at least ownership with regard to the produce-rights, and it is called "for yourself." If so, since we at least require ownership of produce-rights, it is understandable where it is with his knowledge and he transfers it to him; but without his knowledge, it is ineffective, for transfer of ownership requires the owner's knowledge, and without his knowledge there is no transfer. From the body of the verse "for yourself," borrowed should be valid. Since they learned it from another verse, it is proven that borrowed is not included in "for yourself" and is not considered his through ownership of the sitting; another verse was needed. One can further answer Tosafot's question by saying the practical difference is if Levi stole from Reuven and Shimon from each one less than a perutah's worth and roofed with it. This works according to those who accept the principle of mitzvah through transgression. But what shall we do according to the one who does not hold of mitzvah through transgression at all, or according to the decisors who hold it is only rabbinic? Then the difficulty remains: perhaps "for yourself" excludes only a stolen sukkah from an individual and not the public domain. It appears that certainly the two depend on one another. According to R. Eliezer, who holds land is stolen, since he steals the body of the land, certainly the public domain is like theft from an individual, for the body of the land is not his and he is now stealing the land. On the contrary, the public domain is worse, for who can claim and who can forgive, as the Tosafot Yom Tov wrote in his question. Therefore certainly from this verse, "for yourself," both are excluded, for each has an advantage and a disadvantage and they are equal; which would you exclude? But the Mishnah in Orlah follows the rabbis, and according to us land is not stolen. If so, since wherever the body of the land is, it remains in the owner's domain, and he is not stealing the body of the land but only temporary use, and he has a share in the right to use it, the public domain is indeed better than an individual. Therefore the Mishnah in Orlah listed it in a way of "not only this, but even this." Examine carefully. What the Rema wrote, "and likewise a Jew should not cut the roofing, etc., but should buy it from a gentile": the language of Hagahot Ashiri in chapter 3 of Sukkah is as follows: Ra'avyah in the name of his father Rabbenu Yoel, that people must be careful not to cut willow for the lulav from the fields of a gentile, even with his permission, because ordinary gentiles are robbers of fields, etc. Rather, he should hire the gentile to cut it, so that owner-despair will be in their hand and change of domain in your hand. End quote. It implies that it is permitted to hire the gentile to cut. In truth this matter requires examination: from where do we know this is effective? The Talmud speaks where the gentile himself, the owner of the field, cuts it, and since it was his from the outset and he intends to steal it, the despair properly takes effect in their hand, and change of domain in yours. In that case, since he himself is the seller, he acts for his own intent and not for the Jew, and therefore the despair is in their hand. But if the Jew hires another gentile to cut, this gentile does not intend to steal it and does not do it for his own intent but only for the Jew. If so, the despair does not take effect in the gentile's hand, but only when it reaches the hand of the Jewish buyer who intends to acquire it; if so, it is still as though the Jew stole it. All the more so according to the Machaneh Ephraim in the laws of agency, who holds that where it is through hiring there is agency for a gentile, and this is brought in Sha'ar HaMelech, laws of Terumot; according to that it is even more difficult, for the gentile is the Jew's agent and it is as though the Jew stole it himself. One cannot say the intent of Hagahot Ashiri is that the same gentile who sells it to him hires himself out to cut, for if so why mention hiring? It is understandable if his intent is to another gentile, for he states the normal case, since a gentile would not obey him for free. One can also say that this itself teaches that even through hiring it is permitted for the gentile to cut. But if the intent is that same gentile himself, why mention hiring? Without hiring he will do so, for he needs to sell them, and if he does not cut, the Jew will not buy from him. He should have used the language of the Talmud: rather, let them cut it. Perforce his intent is another gentile, and if so it is difficult why this truly helps. The Rema indeed changed the language of Hagahot Ashiri and wrote only: he should buy from the gentile, etc. The words of Hagahot Ashiri require examination in this respect. Regarding the law whether it is permitted to make a sukkah in the public domain, I wrote to permit in Sefer HaChayim. Now I found a clear proof to this from the words of the Yerushalmi and the Rashba in his novellae to Bava Kamma at the end of chapter HaKones. This is his language there: R. Yochanan says that regarding a Chanukah lamp he is exempt because it is with permission, meaning with permission of mitzvah, as we say in chapter HaManiach Et HaKad regarding one who puts out his flax. However, not everyone who puts something out with permission of mitzvah is exempt, for if he made a mitzvah sukkah at the entrance of his house outside and people were damaged by it, he is liable, as stated in the Yerushalmi, such as those who make a sukkah at the entrance of their shops on the festival. But with Chanukah he is exempt, because there is a mitzvah to place it at the entrance of his house because of publicizing the miracle. End quote. His intent is clear: specifically with Chanukah, where it is specifically a mitzvah to place it outside, he is exempt. But with sukkah, since it is not specifically a mitzvah that it be outside, he is liable, even though in any event it is a valid sukkah and a mitzvah, for otherwise it is obvious he is liable. Perforce it is a valid sukkah. And what obligates him must be where he made it in the public domain, for if it were in his own domain he would be exempt for all damages. The main law of damages is where he did something in the public domain, as is proven in Bava Kamma in several places. Perforce it speaks where he made the sukkah in the public domain, and it is proven that it is a mitzvah and the sukkah is valid. This is clear and correct. I later remembered that this proof is already explained in Mekor Chayim from the Tosefta. In my Sefer HaChayim I rejected this; see there.
