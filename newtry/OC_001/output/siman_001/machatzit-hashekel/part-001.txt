<b>And according to the first answer in our Gemara, etc.</b> This refers to Berachos 3a: Rabbi Eliezer taught that the night consists of three watches. The sign for the first watch is a donkey braying, for the second dogs barking, and for the third a child nursing at its mother's breast while a woman speaks with her husband. The Gemara asks what points in time are being counted. If it counts the beginning of the first watch, why is a sign required, since that is the ordinary time when night begins? If it counts the end of each watch, why is a sign required for the last watch, since it is already daytime? The Gemara answers that it counts the end of the first watch, the beginning of the last watch, and the middle of the middle watch. Alternatively, it counts the end of all the watches. Although a sign for the last watch would seem unnecessary because it is already day, it is relevant to one who sleeps in a dark house and does not know the time for Krias Shema. When a child nurses and a woman speaks with her husband, he should rise and recite it.

According to the first answer, which provides a sign for the middle of the middle watch, the practical implication must be that this is a time of favor for prayer. This is midnight, in accordance with the Zohar. Rashba in his novellae writes that the practical implication concerns sacrificial meat, whose period of consumption extends until midnight, as stated in the first Mishnah of Berachos. Rashba did not possess the Zohar, which had not yet been revealed in his time, and therefore explained the matter in relation to offerings. Now that the Zohar has been revealed and explicitly states that midnight is a time of favor for prayer concerning the destruction, it is preferable to harmonize our Gemara with the Zohar and interpret Rabbi Eliezer's statement concerning the middle of the middle watch as referring to Tikkun Chatzos. Rabbi Eliezer's own language supports this, for he concludes that during every watch the Holy One, blessed is He, sits and roars. For this reason Rosh writes there that every God-fearing person should grieve and be distressed at that hour and pour out supplications over the destruction of the Beit HaMikdash, as stated in seif 3.

The second answer does not imply this, because it gives no sign for midnight, but rather counts the ends of the watches. Although Yevamos 71a states that during all forty years in the wilderness there was no day when the north wind did not blow for them at midnight, and derives from the plague of the firstborn that midnight is a time of favor, this still does not establish that it is specifically a time of favor for prayer, especially prayer over the destruction of the Beit HaMikdash. See Maharsha there, who explains that the north wind at midnight was naturally beneficial for those who had undergone circumcision, yet Hashem struck the firstborn at that very time, demonstrating His power to alter nature.

<b>That the night is always reckoned, etc.</b> Although this is not the rule regarding the times of prayer, Shaarei Zion writes similarly. His words, and those of Magen Avraham below in siman 233, imply that both in summer and winter the beginning of the night is reckoned from tzeis hakochavim, followed by twelve equal sixty-minute hours. After those twelve hours it is considered day. Thus, even in winter, when the natural night is longer, its final additional hours are treated as daytime. According to this, midnight is not at the same civil time in summer and winter. In summer it occurs after our ordinary midnight, and in winter before it. The later authorities discuss this at length.

Responsa Shev Yaakov, siman 1, challenges this view at length and concludes that the Zohar implies otherwise. Just as the additional hours at the end of a winter night are assigned to the day, so too the additional winter hours at the beginning of the night should be assigned to the preceding day. Why should the beginning of the night differ from its end? Rather, one reckons from midday, when the sun stands at the center of the sky. The following six equal hours are day in both summer and winter. At the beginning of the seventh equal hour the night begins and continues for twelve equal hours; afterward it is day, in both summer and winter. According to this, midnight is the same in summer and winter, namely twelve equal hours after midday, when the sun is at the center of the sky.

He further writes that one who wishes to lament at the three watches should calculate them from tzeis hakochavim until alos hashachar, as appears from our Gemara. Only one who observes Tikkun Chatzos, whose practice is based upon the Zohar, should likewise determine midnight according to the Zohar. The watches, whose observance is based upon our Gemara, should be calculated according to the times indicated by the Gemara. See there.
