<b>4) There, in the gloss: 'And immediately when he awakens,' etc.</b> This is not literal: he should wait briefly and not rise suddenly immediately after sleep. Magen Avraham, se'if katan 3; Eliyah Rabbah there. Seder HaYom writes that upon rising he should say: 'Modeh ani lefanecha, Melech chai v'kayam, shehechezarta bi nishmati b'chemlah, rabbah emunatecha.' He need not wash his hands for this, even if they are soiled, because he mentions neither God's Name nor a Divine title. Shalmei Tzibbur, page 17d; Chayei Adam, rule 1, end of ot 1; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, ot 5; Kitzur Shulchan Aruch, siman 1, ot 2. Although Chida writes in Kesher Gudal, siman 1, ot 1, that some say Modeh Ani requires washing the hands, since most poskim write that it does not, that is how we rule.

<b>7) [Seif 2] One who rises early to supplicate before his Creator, etc.</b> Zohar, Parashat Vayigash, page 207a, recounts the praise due to one who is awake precisely at midnight. Sefer Nahar Shalom, page 86b, states: Immediately after six of the twelve hours of the night; which begin from midday, when the sun is directly overhead, for that is certainly midday everywhere, in Gan Eden and in every climate, as is known, since day and night in Gan Eden are equal in summer and winter; from the time the evening shadows begin to incline until twelve hours have been completed is certainly actual midnight. In winter the night borrows hours from the beginning and end of the day: approximately two thirds of the added time from the beginning of the day and one third from its end. In summer, likewise, the day borrows hours from the night: approximately one third of the added time from the beginning of the night and two thirds from its end. This is stated in Zohar, Vayakhel, page 195b: the hours added to the night in winter belong to the day and are not counted among the night's own twelve hours; likewise, the hours added to the day in summer belong to the night and are not counted among the day's own twelve hours. The afternoon tamid offering was likewise brought from the time the evening shadows began to incline, in both winter and summer. Thus we have a decisive practical proof from the time of the tamid. Although in summer there are more than six hours from when the evening shadows incline until sunset, the time exceeding six hours belongs to the beginning of the coming night; in winter the reverse is true. Consequently, after six of the aforementioned twelve hours of the night it is actual midnight. He should immediately strengthen himself like a lion and rise from his sleep. He must take great care that midnight not pass while he sleeps. If it passes while he sleeps, he tastes the taste of death, attaches and binds himself to the mystery of death, and the side of the impure spirit rules over him, because he descends into the evil half of kelipat nogah. Sleep during the first half of the night pertains to the upper, good half of kelipat nogah. The first six hours of the night are called 'sixty breaths,' as explained in the words of the Rav, of blessed memory, and as will be written, with God's help, in the meditation for sleep. Any sleep during those first six hours, whether little or much, is called 'sixty breaths less one'; he does not taste death and remains alive, as stated in Zohar, Vayigash, page 207a. Even if he sleeps after midnight, once he has recited the blessings and Tikkun Chatzot there is no concern. The impure spirit no longer rules over him and he no longer tastes death after awakening at midnight and drawing the mochin to Zeir Anpin through the blessings. The danger of the aforementioned grasp and rule of the kelipot applies to one who was asleep before midnight, midnight passed over him, and he did not awaken; the kelipot then rule over him. They do not rule over one who goes to sleep after midnight. This is unlike Mikdash Melech, who writes in the name of Ramaz regarding the aforementioned Zohar; see there. Their Zohar text lacked the emendation of Derech Emet. The Zohar reads: 'When the night divides and the supernal holiness awakens, and a person sleeps in his bed and does not awaken, etc.' Arizal emended 'a person who sleeps in his bed,' changing the word to indicate that he had already been sleeping and did not awaken. The words 'and did not awaken' likewise imply that he was already sleeping and failed to awaken. The Rav, of blessed memory, explicitly permits sleep after midnight in Shaar HaKavanot, Discourses of the Night, discourse 4: if one cannot remain awake from midnight until morning, he may sleep afterward. Thus great diligence and care are required so that at the instant of midnight one is awake, especially if the root of his soul is from the union of Yaakov and Leah that occurs after midnight. Shalmei Tzibbur, page 17, cites this. Birkei Yosef, ot 5, likewise cites Shav Yaakov, Orach Chayyim, siman 1, that twelve hours after midday is midnight in Heaven and in Gan Eden on earth. So write Ikarei HaDat, siman 1, ot 2; Birkat Yosef, ot 2; Shulchan Aruch HaRav, ot 8; and Shaarei Teshuvah, ot 6, who concludes that this appears to be the primary view. R' Chayyim Palachi likewise writes in Kaf HaChayyim, siman 3, ot 33, that this view is agreed upon by the Talmud, the poskim, and the kabbalists, as he writes in Lev Chayyim, part 1, siman 6, and Ruach Chayyim, siman 1, ot 20; see there. So too writes Ben Ish Chai, Parashat Vayishlach, ot 4.

<b>ח) ואף  </b>מי שלא נתעורר בנקודות חצות אלא אחר שעה או ב' צריך לקום באותה שעה ולא יאמר כיוון שלא נתעורר בנקו' חצות ישאר ישן אלא כל מה שמקדים בקימה מן השינה עדיף טפי לטעם הנז' כדי שלא יתאחזו בו החיצונים ויטעום טעם מיתה זמן ארוך ואם גם זאת אינו יכול לעשות לפחות ישתדל לקום קודם עלות השחר כדי שיכול לומר תיקון חצות ולחבר לילה ויום בתורה כמבואר לקמן:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>8) Even</b> one who did not awaken precisely at midnight but only an hour or two later should rise at that time. He should not say that since he failed to awaken precisely at midnight he might as well remain asleep. The earlier he rises from sleep, the better, for the aforementioned reason: so that the external forces do not attach themselves to him and he does not taste the taste of death for an extended time. If he cannot do even this, he should at least endeavor to rise before dawn so that he can recite Tikkun Chatzot and join night and day through Torah study, as explained below.

<b>ט) בליל  </b>שבת מי שאינו ניעור בנקודת חצות ועבר עליו חצות בשינה יש להסתפק אם מתלבשים בו החיצונים וטועם טעם מיתה או כיון שלא יש להם אחיזה לחיצונים בשבת כמבואר בשער הכוו' בדרושי שבת ובשער המצות פ' בשלח ופ' בהר סיני וכ"כ אמל"י במערכת התיו או' ע"ד וז"ל נמצא דקלי' נוגה בשבת הטוב שבה הכלים שבה עולים למעלה וכו' עי"ש א"כ גם אדם התחתון אין מתלבשים בו. אח"כ ראיתי בס' ש"ש ח"ג בדרוש א' משנוי התפילות או' ח' דכתב דאין צורך לקום בליל ש"ק בחצות לילה דלילה כהיום יאירו ואין פחד מהחצונים יעו"ש וכ"כ בספרו ש"ש ח"ד הנקרא פתח עינים בדרוש תיקון חצות. ומ"מ גם בלילי שבת ויו"ט ישתדל לקום בחצות כדי שלא יבטל הרגלו וגם מטעם שכתבנו בסי' רל"ח או' ג' יעו"ש.

<b>9) On Shabbos night,</b> if one was not awake precisely at midnight and midnight passed while he slept, there is room to question whether the external forces clothe themselves in him and he tastes the taste of death, or whether they do not, since the external forces have no grasp on Shabbos, as explained in Shaar HaKavanot, the Discourses of Shabbos, and Shaar HaMitzvot, Parashat Beshalach and Parashat Behar Sinai. Emet LeYaakov, entry tav, ot 74, likewise writes: 'It follows that on Shabbos, the good within kelipat nogah (its vessels) ascends above, etc.' See there. If so, they do not clothe themselves in the lower human being either. I later saw in Shemen Sasson, part 3, discourse 1 on the changes in the prayers, ot 8, that there is no need to rise at midnight on holy Shabbos night, because 'night will shine like day' and there is no fear of the external forces; see there. He writes likewise in Shemen Sasson, part 4, called Petach Einayim, in the discourse on Tikkun Chatzot. Nevertheless, even on the nights of Shabbos and Yom Tov one should endeavor to rise at midnight so as not to break his habit, and also for the reason we wrote in siman 238, ot 3; see there.
