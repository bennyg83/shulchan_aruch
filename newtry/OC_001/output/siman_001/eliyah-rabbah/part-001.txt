Knesses HaGedolah writes in siman 619: Regarding those people who rise early for the synagogue every day but doze during prayer, and my own eyes have seen sleep overtake them even during Krias Shema, of them I apply the verse, “Woe to those who rise early in the morning.” Therefore, the good and proper course is that, if they can also concentrate upon their prayers, they should rise during the pre-dawn watch. [4] <b>From his sleep, etc.</b> Shelah, page 124, writes that if one does not wish to return to sleep, he should wash his hands even though he remains lying down. It is forbidden, however, to remain awake while lying upon his bed unless he is reflecting upon words of Torah. Bach, siman 4, writes as follows: Sefer Tola'as Yaakov states in the name of the Zohar that one who walks four amos in the morning without washing his hands is liable to death. It is surprising that Beit Yosef did not record this. It appears that, since the overwhelming majority are unable to be careful about it, and it is better that they remain inadvertent rather than become deliberate transgressors, he omitted it. These are his words, but the explanation is strained. It also requires study why the public, including those knowledgeable in halachah, is lenient. Levush writes at the beginning of siman 25 that immediately upon rising from bed one should relieve himself and wash his hands. Since an ordinary person's privy is not within four amos of his bed, this implies grounds for leniency. The same appears from Levush, siman 8, seif 11, who writes that one may wrap himself in tzitzis before washing so that he will not walk four amos without tzitzis. The implication is readily understood.

My brother-in-law, the distinguished Rabbi Yaakov, said that people relied upon Shach, Yoreh Deah 344, who cites Mordechai that the entire room in which a deceased person lies is regarded as four amos. This does not appear correct, first because people are even more lenient and leave that room, and second because Mordechai itself is difficult. In Berachos 25a Rava states that the halachah does not follow Rabbi Shimon ben Elazar, who says that an entire house is regarded as four amos. We likewise rule below, siman 79, regarding excrement in a room, and the discussion there implies that “the entire house” means the entire room. Likutei Maharil similarly writes that throughout the Gemara the word “house” commonly means a room.

It appears to me that Mordechai may be resolved as follows. At the beginning of Berachos the Gemara states that in the presence of the dead one may speak only of matters relating to the dead, without mentioning four amos. Mordechai therefore explains that the prohibition applies throughout the room, which is treated like the deceased person's four amos. The reason is as Beit Yosef explains there: when one speaks of other matters in the deceased person's presence, he appears to have despaired of attending to the burial. Where the Gemara expressly limits a prohibition to four amos, however, the prohibition applies only within four amos, even in the same room.

Incidentally, I shall briefly mention what I wrote in Eliyah Rabbah on Yoreh Deah there, resolving Beit Yosef's question why Mordechai connected the rule that the whole room is prohibited specifically to the first version of the Gemara. Mordechai reverses the order of the Gemara's versions. According to the first version, even ordinary conversation is forbidden before the deceased. The prohibition is therefore not based on mocking the helpless, but upon the appearance that one has abandoned attending to him, and it applies throughout the room. According to the second version, only words of Torah are forbidden because of mocking the helpless, and the prohibition therefore applies only within four amos, like Krias Shema near a corpse and walking in a cemetery while wearing visible tzitzis or tefillin, discussed there in siman 366. This appears to be the meaning of Tosafos on Berachos 3b, s.v. Ein, who write, “specifically within his four amos.” Examine this carefully.

I later found in Damesek Eliezer of Apta, page 326a, regarding the above words of Tola'as Yaakov: perhaps this applied specifically in their times, like the laws of uncovered liquids and pairs. This resolves Bach's question why Beit Yosef did not record it, and upon this the public relies. Nevertheless, a God-fearing person should be stringent and follow the mussar works, preparing beside his bed two vessels, one containing water and one empty. Etz Chaim writes that it is preferable for the empty vessel to be chipped, for it then represents dominion over the Other Side. Sefer Mekor Chaim writes that one should not touch his clothing or anything else until washing his hands. Seder HaYom writes that immediately upon rising one should say, “I give thanks before You, living and enduring King, for You have mercifully restored my soul within me; great is Your faithfulness.” This does not require washing the hands, even if they are soiled, because no Divine Name or appellation is mentioned. See what I wrote at the end of siman 5.

[6] <b>The passage of the Akeidah, etc.</b> It is recited to invoke before Hashem the merit of the Patriarchs and to remember that one is obligated to surrender his life to Hashem. The passage of the manna is recited so that one will believe that all of his sustenance comes through Hashem's providence. Sefer HaKavanos writes that it should be recited before the passages of the offerings and that it should be recited even on Shabbos.

Shelah, page 202, writes that on Shabbos and Yom Tov, among the passages of the offerings one should recite only the daily offering and all that accompanies it, the additional offerings, and the incense, because these are offered on Shabbos and Yom Tov. The other passages of the offerings should not be recited at all, even with the intention merely to read Torah. In Eliyahu Zuta I cited Lechem Chamudos, page 84, that one who has the ability to understand and learn should preferably learn Torah and suffice with the established prayers; instead of supplications and passages of offerings, he should study. Kitzur Shelah cited my words and objected that the Gemara distinguishes between the time for Torah and the time for prayer. This is not a valid objection, because the Gemara there refers to Shemoneh Esrei and Krias Shema. It is also difficult that he contradicts himself, for in the laws of washing the hands he ruled in accordance with my words. I also found this in Piskei Tosafos: one who is not burdened by business and is not a Torah scholar should not omit the daily ma'amados, words of supplication, and other verses printed in siddurim. One who is occupied with business should omit them, and a Torah scholar should preferably study in their place. Piskei Tosafos further writes that it is forbidden to recite ma'amados and supplications during Pesukei DeZimra or while the chazzan repeats Shemoneh Esrei.

The precise authorities further write that one who does not recite ma'amados every day should at least recite the psalm for the day immediately after the passage of the daily offering in the morning. Even on Shabbos he should recite the psalm for Shabbos immediately after the verse concerning the Shabbos offering. Afterward he should recite, “You are He before Whom our fathers burned the incense,” the passage beginning “Hashem said to Moshe,” and the order of the incense as printed in the ma'amados. He then need not recite it again in Musaf, even on Shabbos or Yom Tov.

I likewise saw in Taz, siman 132, that the incense should be recited before prayer. He reasons that the passage of the daily offering corresponds to its blood, while prayer corresponds to its limbs. Otherwise, why would both be needed? He therefore writes that the incense should be recited after the passage of the daily offering, so that it is read between the blood and the limbs. This reasoning is surprising, for when the daily offering was actually brought they afterward prayed, as stated in Sukkah 53a. Moreover, Rabbeinu Yonah at the beginning of the chapter Tefillas HaShachar explains that prayer principally corresponds to the blood. Nevertheless, the practical ruling is correct, not for the reason given, but because it is found in Responsa Menachem Azaryah, siman 14; see his explanation there.

Seder HaYom writes that he saw a custom to introduce the daily psalm by saying explicitly, “The song that the Leviim recited in the Beit HaMikdash on the first day was,” and similarly on the other weekdays. Responsa Menachem Azaryah, siman 25, indicates that this wording should not be said; see there for the laws of these psalms. Levush HaOrah on Parashas Va'eschanan writes that the omission of Tachanun on Shabbos and Yom Tov refers to supplication that breaks a person's heart. A request that the Holy One, blessed is He, grant him abundant gifts is permitted. This is why requests were arranged in the Shabbos ma'amados.
