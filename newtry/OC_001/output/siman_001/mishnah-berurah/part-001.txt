<b>(1) To serve his Creator.</b> For this is why man was created, as Scripture states, “Everyone who is called by My Name, and whom I created for My glory,” etc. Even if his evil inclination entices him in winter, saying, “How will you rise in the morning, for the cold is severe?” or entices him in summer, saying, “How will you rise from your bed when you have not yet had enough sleep?” he should overcome it and not listen. He should think to himself: If he had to rise to serve a mortal king, how careful and diligent he would be to rise early and prepare himself for that service. All the more so, many times over, before the King of kings, the Holy One, blessed is He.

<b>(2) The dawn.</b> The Shelah writes of a mystical practice to connect day and night through Torah or prayer, both in the morning and in the evening. As soon as one awakens and does not intend to return to sleep, he should wash his hands, even while he remains lying down. All the more so, he should not walk four amos without washing his hands, and one must be exceedingly careful about this. The holy Zohar greatly emphasizes the punishment for delaying, because one leaves the spirit of impurity upon himself. Nevertheless, God forbid that one violate any prohibition because he has not washed his hands. For example, he must not restrain himself from urinating, nor take water that another prepared for himself unless he is certain that he will immediately replace it. Some people stumble in this matter. If the water is far away and no one can bring it to him, some meticulous people have the practice of walking in increments of less than four amos. Shaarei Teshuvah writes that this does not appear correct, and that it is preferable to walk quickly so as not to leave the evil spirit upon his hands. Some say that for this purpose the entire house is considered like four amos, but one should rely on this only in pressing circumstances. If one rises during the night and lacks enough water to wash his hands three times properly and remove the spirit of impurity, God forbid that he refrain from Torah study until daylight. Rather, he should wash with a small amount of water, or clean his hands with any cleansing substance, then recite the blessing and study, in accordance with the Gemara and the poskim.

<b>(4) The righteous.</b> He should constantly picture himself as standing before Hashem, for the Holy One, blessed is He, fills the entire earth with His glory. It is written in the name of the Arizal that one should constantly visualize the Name Yud-Hei-Vav-Hei before his eyes with the vocalization associated with awe. This is the mystical meaning of, “I have placed Hashem before me always,” and it is of great benefit in attaining awe. Some therefore had the practice of making illustrated parchment plaques to place in siddurim, writing upon them, “I have placed Hashem,” together with the four-letter Name and other sacred Names. Such a plaque was called a Shivisi. Its purpose was to remind a person not to engage in idle speech during prayer, out of awe of Hashem. Shaarei Teshuvah writes in the name of Tevuot Shor that one should exert himself to abolish this custom, because these parchments are generally not guarded properly, are cast about publicly, and often lead to erasure of the Divine Name. It appears, however, that there is no objection to large parchment plaques affixed in the synagogue before the lectern beneath a glass panel, because the preceding concern does not apply. End quote. Those who affix them to the lectern without a glass panel should be protested, because the candles placed there frequently cause erasure of the Divine Name, God forbid.

<b>(8) With alacrity.</b> This is not literal. One should pause briefly and not rise suddenly, because doing so is harmful to the body, as stated in Gittin, folio 70. It is proper to say immediately upon rising: “I give thanks before You, living and enduring King, for You have mercifully restored my soul within me; great is Your faithfulness.” One should pause after “mercifully,” and say “great is Your faithfulness” without a break between those words. This is based on the verse, “They are new every morning,” because the Holy One, blessed is He, faithfully restores in the morning the souls entrusted to Him. Washing the hands is not required before saying this, even when the hands are unclean, because the formula contains neither a Divine Name nor a descriptive Divine title. It nevertheless appears forbidden to study before washing the hands, and in any event Torah study is forbidden before Birkat HaTorah. See above, at the end of s.k. 2.

<b>(9) It is proper for every person, etc.</b> The kabbalists wrote at great length about the immense virtue of rising at midnight. The appropriate order of conduct, based on the writings of the Arizal, has already been printed in the siddurim. Midnight is always the exact midpoint of the night in every location, whether the nights are long or short, and it is twelve seasonal hours after midday. The most fitting time to mourn the destruction is shortly before midnight. From midnight onward one should engage in Torah, and near the end of the night he should request his needs. Some then recite Shomrim LaBoker. If sunrise is near, he should recite only the psalms, omit the lamentations, and afterward say Shomrim LaBoker. After Tikkun Chatzos, study of Mishnah takes precedence over every other subject. If one has merited knowledge of the hidden Torah, that time is especially suited for it. The later authorities received a tradition that from the period of “Lie down” until “Arise,” meaning from Shavuos, when the haftarah includes “Lie down,” until Tisha B'Av, when it includes “Arise,” one need not rise during the night. Others say that one should rise from the fifteenth of Av until the fifteenth of Iyar. Those who follow kabbalistic practice hold that one should rise throughout the year. A group that gathers to recite Tikkun Chatzos communally should not be prevented. On the contrary, “In a multitude of people is the glory of the King.” It is preferable that they recite the Tikkun in the synagogue rather than in an adjoining room, because the synagogue has greater sanctity and they will also be among the first ten. One must be especially careful to avoid mundane conversation while awake at night. A person who cannot rise before dawn because he is naturally weak, or who knows that rising during the night watch will cause him to sleep during prayer, should instead sleep as much as he needs. Nevertheless, he should be very careful to rise one hour, or at least half an hour, before the attendant calls the congregation to the synagogue, so that he can prepare himself to pray with the congregation in cleanliness.
