**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Since he was compelled.</b> This resembles a woman whose experience began under coercion and ended willingly, which is nevertheless regarded as coercion. There. Responsa Rashba, siman 794, states: “You asked concerning an ill person who ate on Yom Kippur. One of the later authorities wrote that he does not recite a blessing. Although he inevitably derives benefit, that benefit comes through compulsion, and one does not recite a blessing in such a case. It resembles a woman whose experience began under coercion and ended willingly, which is regarded as coercion,” etc. It is known that the Rashba’s disputant is the Raah. Since we rule that one who eats on Yom Kippur must recite a blessing even though he does so under compulsion, why does Rama rule here that one who was forced to eat is exempt? What difference is there between being compelled by others and being compelled by Heaven? It may be possible to distinguish between them. Yet this is difficult, for in siman 475, seif 4, Rama rules that one who was forced to eat matzah has fulfilled his obligation, following Beit Yosef there in the name of Maggid Mishneh and Ran. Their reason is that one who inadvertently engages in forbidden fats or forbidden relations is liable because he derives benefit. See there. This shows that the benefit is halachically significant. Why, then, should he not recite a blessing? One cannot answer that the case concerns a person who finds eating repulsive and is fed against his will, for then his palate derives no benefit. This requires study. See also Bamidbar Rabbah, Parashas Naso, page 229b. It therefore appears to me that Beit Yosef omitted this ruling for precisely this reason.
