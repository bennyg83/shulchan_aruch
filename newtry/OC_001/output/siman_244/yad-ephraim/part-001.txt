<b>במג"א סק"ב</b> בצנעה כצ"ל כוונתו דכל שהוא מלאכה שבצנעה אף היודעין שהוא של ישראל אין חושדין אותו ודנין לו לכף זכות שבודאי הוא עושה בהיתר ע"י קציצת דמים וכדומ' משא"כ מלאכת פרהסיא אף שהוא דר בין העכו"ם מ"מ חיישינן לאורחים ובני ביתו שמחמת שהן רואין שמצוה לעשות מלאכה בפרהסיא בשלו בשבת הן חושדין אותו במה שאין בו וסבורים ששכר העכו"ם לימים ואומר ששכר בקבלנות כמ"ש המג"א בס"ק ד':
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>In Magen Avraham, seif katan 2,</b> “privately” is the correct reading. His intent is that where the work is private, even those who know that it belongs to a Jew do not suspect him. They judge him favorably and assume that the gentile is acting permissibly under a fixed-price arrangement or the like. Public work is different. Even if the Jew lives among gentiles, we remain concerned for guests and members of his household. Since they see him directing that public work be performed on his property on Shabbos, they may falsely suspect him and assume that he hired the gentile by the day while claiming that the arrangement was contractual, as Magen Avraham writes in seif katan 4.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Magen Avraham, seif katan 18:</b> When he hires the gentile under a contracted arrangement, it is permitted as a matter of law because the gentile acts for his own benefit, receiving a share of the customs revenue itself. This is comparable to giving hides to a tanner, which is permitted when a fixed price was set. Although here the Jew profits from the customs collected by the gentile on Shabbos, which resembles a bathhouse or mill as explained above in siman 243, seif katan 2, the Jew’s profit itself is not the concern there, just as there is no concern with a mill operated under sharecropping. The reason for prohibition there is that we do not say the gentile acts for himself, since even if he does not work on Shabbos he must still pay the fixed rent, as Magen Avraham writes and as I explained there at length. Where the gentile receives a fixed share of every dinar collected, however, it is comparable to a mill under sharecropping. The only concern is that here the Jew assigns the work specifically to Shabbos. The Sages nevertheless permitted this because of financial loss. Thus there is no prohibition from the Jew’s appointing the gentile to collect the customs, and the gentile does not appear to be his agent. Nor is there a prohibition in the customs revenue itself entering the Jew’s possession because of Shabbos wages. The Jew did not purchase only the customs rights of Shabbos, but the customs rights for the entire year, including Shabbos. He acquired the Shabbos income inclusively together with the weekday income, and when he receives it, it constitutes Shabbos wages included within a broader payment. If, however, he leases to the gentile only the profits of Shabbos, then even though there is no agency on behalf of the Jew, the income itself entering the Jew’s possession is separate Shabbos wages and is forbidden. The Sages did not permit that because of financial loss. Their leniency applies only where the concern is that the gentile appears to act as his agent because the Jew specifically assigns him to Shabbos, as explained in siman 243 regarding an oven and bathhouse. So it appears to me. I have seen some explain Magen Avraham’s statement that the Jew receives Shabbos wages inclusively to mean that the gentile is also obligated to collect during part of Friday, so the arrangement resembles leasing it to the gentile for a fixed payment while the gentile keeps the remainder, and the Jew’s Shabbos wages are included with weekday wages. In my humble opinion this is incorrect. Where the underlying enterprise belongs to the Jew and the gentile receives a fixed amount, it is not a lease but sharecropping. Rather, the explanation is as I wrote. Magen Avraham’s statement regarding an oven and bathhouse, that sometimes there is financial loss, and “nevertheless on that day,” appears possibly to contain a scribal error. It should read: “Nevertheless, on that day,” and his intent is as follows. With an oven or bathhouse, one may sometimes lose more than the amount lost from one day’s customs revenue. One could answer that a bathhouse or oven differs: even though, if it had been constructed specifically for Shabbos, the loss of the money invested in it would be considered a genuine loss, nevertheless, since it can be operated during the weekdays, the fact that it is not operated on Shabbos is treated merely as loss of profit. Customs rights are different because the Jew paid the king for a fixed number of months. If he does not collect on a particular day, he loses money from his own pocket. Magen Avraham responds that one cannot make this distinction, because even regarding customs there may be cases where failing to collect on Shabbos does not cause an out-of-pocket loss. We must therefore say that the loss is assessed day by day. But if so, the same should apply to an oven or bathhouse. Although it can be used on weekdays, since he spent money constructing it and its entire purpose is to be operated each day and earn rental income, failure to operate it on Shabbos should likewise be considered a loss. To this Magen Avraham writes, “Therefore it appears,” etc. If there is no scribal error in the phrase, “and one could say, nevertheless,” the words “therefore it appears” should perhaps be emended to “indeed it appears.”

<b>ס"ק י"ט</b> ומותר ליתן כסף לעכו"ם ר"ל שהישראל הממונה מן המלך על הכאת המטבע מותר ליתן לעכו"ם הכסף לתקן המעות אפילו בשבת דאין כאן חשש משוה שמשמיע קול בשבת דאסור לדעת הגהמ"י כמבואר בסימן רנ"ב בהג"ה דזה דוקא היכא שהשמעת קול הוא מחמת כלי ישראל כגון ברחיים וכה"ג משא"כ כאן שהפטיש המכה וגם הכסף המתוכן ומטבע הכל אינו של ישראל רק שהוא ממונה מן המלך ע"ז דהיינו שהמלך מתפשר עמו וקוצץ לו איזה סך שהוא ישכור פועלים ויכה לו סך כך וכך מטבעות אבל מ"מ הכסף והכלים שעושים בהם מלאכה הכל שייך למלך לאומני' ובכה"ג אין חשש משום השמעות קול כיון שאינו של ישראל. וגם משום ששוכר פועלים אין חשש כיון שהן באים בשכרם ועושין המלאכה בביתו של גוי ואין מקפיד שיעשו בשבת דוקא כעורות לעבדן דבקצץ שרי ויש מי שנראה מדבריו שמ"ש המ"א בשם הגהמ"י שאין כאן השמעת קול שהוא של ישראל ר"ל שאין הדבר מפורסם שהוא שייך לישראל וליתא אלא פשוט כוונתו וכמ"ש ג"כ בלשון הג"ה כאן אעפ"י שמשמיע קול בהכאת המטבע והוא פשוט:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Seif katan 19:</b> “It is permissible to give silver to a gentile.” Meaning, a Jew appointed by the king over the mint may give the gentile silver to prepare the coins even on Shabbos. There is no concern of prohibited sound production, which Hagahos Maimoniyos forbids, as explained in the gloss to siman 252. That concern applies only when the sound is produced by a Jew’s equipment, such as a mill or similar machinery. Here, however, both the striking hammer, the silver being prepared, and the minting equipment belong not to the Jew but to the king or the king’s craftsmen. The Jew is merely appointed by the king over the work. The king makes an arrangement with him and fixes a sum for which he will hire workers and produce a specified number of coins. The silver and all the tools used in the work nevertheless remain the king’s property. In such a case there is no concern regarding the sound, because it is not Jewish property. Nor is there concern regarding his hiring the workers, because they come for their wages, perform the work in the gentile’s premises, and he does not insist that they work specifically on Shabbos. This is comparable to giving hides to a tanner under a fixed-price arrangement, which is permitted. Some interpret Magen Avraham’s citation of Hagahos Maimoniyos, that there is no sound production involving Jewish property, to mean merely that it is not publicly known that the enterprise belongs to a Jew. This is incorrect. His straightforward intent is as stated here, and the gloss itself uses the same wording: although coin minting produces sound, it is permitted because the equipment does not belong to a Jew. This is clear.
