<b>לבנות לו חצירו. </b> התוס' ספ"ק דע"א  בשם ר"ת דר"ת היה מתיר לבנות בית ע"י עכו"ם בקבלנות כיון דאין שבח לישראל בזה במה שבונה בשבת  כמו בשדה דאריס אריסותיה עביד הכי נמי בזה דעכו"ם עושה קבלנות שלו ור"י אוסר מטעם שנזכיר בסמוך וכ"פ הרמב"ם והיא סעיף זה ור"ת עצמו לא סמך על הוראתו כשבנה ביתו וכתב בהג"מ פ"ק דשבת בשם רבינו שמחה דיש ללמוד מירושלמי דהשפחות אסורים לעשות מלאכה בבית ישראל בשבת עכ"ל  והטעם מפני הרואי' שלא יאמרו מלאכ' של ישראל היא עושה:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>To build his courtyard for him.</b> Tosafos at the end of the first chapter of Avodah Zarah writes in the name of Rabbeinu Tam that he permitted constructing a house through a gentile contractor, since the Jew gains no advantage from the work being performed on Shabbos. Just as regarding a field the sharecropper is performing his own sharecropping arrangement, so too here the gentile is performing his own contracted work. Rabbeinu Yitzchak forbids it for the reason explained below, and Rambam rules likewise, as stated in this seif. Rabbeinu Tam himself did not rely on his own ruling when he built his house. Hagahos Maimoniyos, chapter 1 of Hilchos Shabbos, writes in the name of Rabbeinu Simchah that one may derive from the Yerushalmi that maidservants may not perform work in a Jew’s house on Shabbos. The reason is that onlookers may say that she is performing the Jew’s work.

<b>או לקצור שדהו כו'. </b> ואע"ג דאמרינן לעיל שדה עביד לאריסותא ומותר להשכירו בהבלעה שאני התם שלא התירו חכמי' אלא כשהאמת כן כלו' שהם יתלו הדבר באריסות  ואם יחקרו הדבר ימצאו שכן הוא שהעכו"ם חולק בפירות אבל כה"ג שהבעלים נוטלים כל הפירו' מחזי לאינשי כשכיר יום כ"כ הרב המגיד וכב"י ע"ז שכ"ד התו' והפוסקים ואע"ג דהתו' כתבו בספ"ק דע"א לחלוק על ר"ת בענין היתר לבנות ע"י עכו"ם בשבת ואמרו שאין זה דומה לאריסות דשדה דלעולם היא רגילות לקבל השדה באריסות למחצה או לשליש אבל בבנין בית רגילים לשכור מידי יום יום והרוא' אינו אומר קבלנותי' עביד אלא שכיר יום הוא עכ"ל משמע מזה דבעבודת שדה מותר בקבלנות שמקבל עליו העכו"ם לעבוד עבודת שדה ישראל זה כתבו לחלק מסבר' על ר"ת אבל אח"כ הביאו התו' וש"פ הירושלמי דאינו מתיר בקבול' העכו"ם בשבת אלא בתלוש אבל לא במחובר וכמ"ש כאן בש"ע שהוא דברי רמב"ם ואפי' בתלוש דוקא בבית עכו"ם ש"מ דבקרקע ישראל  אפי' בשדה ולאו דוקא בבית שהרי לא משכח היתר אלא בתלוש דוקא ועוד נ"ל דברי התו' כי לכאורה תמוה הוא מ"ש אבל בבנין בית רגילין לשכור מידי יום יום והלא עינינו רואות דאדרבה רגילין להשכיר לאומן שיבנה בית דרך כלל בקבלנות אלא נרא'  דהכי מחלקי התו' דבשד' יש ב' דרכים להתיר הא' שעובד לעצמו למחצה או שעובד לישראל כולו והוא בקבלנות ודרך א' לאיסור שעובד לישראל דרך שכיר יום בזה אזלי' לקולא דרגילין שעובד לעצמו ג"כ מה שא"כ בבנין בית אין שם היתר שעובד לעצמו שאין זה בשום פעם אלא עובד לישראל ואין היתר אלא מה שעושה בקבלנות ויש כנגדו צד איסור שמא שכיר יום הוא ע"כ יש להחמיר אפי' בקבלנות  וא"כ שדה נמי אם האמת הוא שעובד לישראל בקבלנות אזיל ליה הצד היתר השני כיון דודאי אינו ה"ל כמו בית ואסור ונמצא הוה כונת התו' כמו דברי הרב המגיד שזכרנו גם ש"פ שמבי' הב"י בזה שהביא הירושלמי הנ"ל מסקנתם להחמיר בכל מחובר הקבלנות הן בשד' הן בבית וכת' ב"י ומ"ש ה"ר ירוחם דרחיים של מים מותר ליתנם לעכו"ם בקבלנות הוא לדעת המתירין אבל לדעת האוסרין בקרקע ה"ה ברחיים דמ"ש עכ"ל:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Or to harvest his field, etc.</b> Although we stated above that a field is ordinarily worked through sharecropping and therefore may be leased under an inclusive arrangement, that case is different. The Sages permitted it only where this is in fact the arrangement. Onlookers attribute the matter to sharecropping, and if they investigate they will discover that the gentile indeed receives a share of the produce. Here, however, where the owner receives all the produce, it appears that the gentile is a day laborer. So writes Maggid Mishneh, and Beit Yosef states that this is also the view of Tosafos and the poskim. Tosafos at the end of the first chapter of Avodah Zarah, when disagreeing with Rabbeinu Tam’s permission to build through a gentile contractor on Shabbos, write that house construction differs from field sharecropping: fields are commonly given out for one-half or one-third of the produce, whereas houses are ordinarily built by day laborers. An observer will therefore not say that the gentile is working under a contract, but that he is a day laborer. This appears to imply that contracted field work is permitted when the gentile undertakes to perform the Jew’s agricultural work. Yet this distinction was offered only as a logical response to Rabbeinu Tam. Tosafos and the other poskim subsequently cite the Yerushalmi, which permits contracted gentile work on Shabbos only with detached materials, not with anything attached to the ground, as the Shulchan Aruch rules here following Rambam. Even with detached materials, it is permitted only in the gentile’s house. It follows that on Jewish land it is forbidden even in a field, and not only in house construction, since the only permitted case is detached work. The words of Tosafos themselves can also be explained this way. At first glance their statement that houses are ordinarily built by day laborers is surprising, for we see that craftsmen are commonly hired to construct an entire house under contract. Rather, Tosafos distinguishes as follows. With a field there are two possible permitted arrangements: the gentile works for himself in return for a share, or he works entirely for the Jew under a contract. There is one forbidden arrangement, namely day labor. Since it is common for the gentile to work for himself, we judge leniently. House construction has no arrangement in which the gentile works for himself. He always works for the Jew. The only possible leniency is a contract, and opposite it stands the possibility that he is a day laborer. One must therefore be stringent even under a contract. If the truth regarding a field is that the gentile works entirely for the Jew under contract, the first basis for leniency is absent; it is then like a house and forbidden. Thus Tosafos’ intent agrees with Maggid Mishneh. The other poskim cited by Beit Yosef likewise conclude, based on the Yerushalmi, that all contracted work involving attached property is forbidden, whether a field or a house. Beit Yosef writes that Rabbeinu Yerucham’s permission to give a water mill to a gentile contractor follows the permissive opinions, but according to those who forbid work involving land, a mill is likewise forbidden, for there is no distinction.

אם כבר בנאוהו כן וכן מסיק מו"ח ז"ל דכדאי הוא ר"ת לסמוך עליו בשעת הדחק בזה וכ"נ עיקר:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>It is proper to be stringent, etc.</b> In siman 544 the Tur writes regarding Chol HaMoed that anything forbidden on Shabbos within the techum is forbidden there even beyond the techum, since one can travel there and suspicion remains. He further writes that even if the house was already built unlawfully by a gentile, it is proper to be stringent and not enter it. Beit Yosef explains the reason from the Gemara in the chapter Mi SheHafach: gentile contractors built a mansion for Mar Zutra beyond the techum. Rav Safra and Rav Huna bar Chinana came there and did not enter. Some say Mar Zutra himself also did not enter. The Gemara asks that Shmuel permits gentile contractors beyond the techum, and answers that a distinguished person is different. Another version states that Mar Zutra had assisted them by supplying straw for the work, and this made it forbidden. This certainly concerns Shabbos, since the Gemara challenges from Shmuel’s ruling concerning Shabbos. The two versions yield different results. According to the first, the case involves an entirely permitted arrangement beyond the techum, yet a distinguished person must still refrain. Where there is an element of prohibition, namely within the techum on Shabbos or even beyond the techum on Chol HaMoed, perhaps every person is forbidden, or perhaps only a distinguished person is forbidden and the novelty is that even beyond the techum such a person must refrain. According to the second version, which involves his having assisted with straw, the case is one of actual prohibition, and all people must refrain from entering. The same would then apply where he did not assist with straw but the construction was nevertheless forbidden, namely within the techum on Shabbos or even beyond the techum on Chol HaMoed. Where the work was entirely permitted, such as beyond the techum on Shabbos without his assistance, even a distinguished person may enter. The prohibition for ordinary people mentioned above is not an absolute prohibition like other prohibitions. We find only that those Amoraim refused to enter, indicating a proper stringency rather than a strict prohibition. The Tur rules in accordance with the second version and therefore writes that it is proper to be stringent not to enter a house built unlawfully. This implies that it is not strictly prohibited. He did not repeat this law here because he relied on what he wrote in siman 544 regarding Chol HaMoed, where the Gemara discusses it, and all the more so it applies on Shabbos. Beit Yosef’s explanation there is concise but includes the entire analysis stated here. It is sound, unlike my father-in-law Bach, who writes that Beit Yosef’s learning failed him. His learning is complete and clear. The Tur’s statement in Hilchos Chol HaMoed that even a structure already built unlawfully by a gentile should not be entered does not refer only to a case beyond the techum on Chol HaMoed; it applies equally within the techum on Shabbos. The rulings are therefore consistent. It appears to me, however, that this stringency applies only where the gentile built it under an arrangement forbidden according to all opinions. If it was built under a contracted arrangement, which Rabbeinu Tam permits, then although he himself did not rely on this permission when building his house, we do not find that he prohibited entering a house already built in that manner. Bach likewise concludes that Rabbeinu Tam is worthy of reliance in a pressing circumstance. This appears primary.

<b>כאלו קצץ עמו כו'. </b> זה דעת הרמב"ם והיש מי שאוסר הוא הראב"ד שכתב ע"ז שאע"פ שאינו מדקדק עמו כשהוא בטל מ"מ אותה מלאכה משתרשא ליה ואינו דומה לקבלנות עכ"ל משמע דגם הרמב"ם לא התיר בקוצץ לזמן  אלא באינו מקפיד אם יבטל איזה יום אבל אם מקפיד עליו שלא ישב איזה יום בטל ודאי אסור לכולי עלמא וכן אפילו בסתם והוא שכרו לגמור ספר או בגד תוך אותו זמן הוה כמקפיד דלא התיר הרמב"ם אלא כשאין עליו לגמור המלאכה ואינו מקפיד על ביטולו כנלע"ד:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>As though he had fixed a price with him, etc.</b> This is Rambam’s view. The opinion that forbids it is Raavad, who writes that although the Jew does not object when the gentile is idle, the work performed on Shabbos nevertheless benefits the Jew, and the arrangement is not comparable to contracted work. This implies that even Rambam permits hiring for a fixed period only where the Jew does not object if the gentile remains idle on a particular day. If he insists that the gentile not be idle on any day, it is forbidden according to all opinions. Likewise, even without an explicit stipulation, if he hired him to complete a book or garment within that period, it is as though he insists on uninterrupted labor. Rambam permits only where the gentile is not obligated to complete a particular task and the Jew does not object to his being idle. So it appears to me.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>If it is under a contracted arrangement, etc.</b> Mordechai writes in the name of Maharam: Even if one distinguishes ordinary contracting, where the Jew does not designate Shabbos specifically for the gentile’s work, from customs collection, where he assigns him specifically for Shabbos, it is nevertheless permitted because the Sages permitted certain matters to prevent financial loss. In the chapter Mi SheHechshich, they permitted conduct that would otherwise be forbidden lest a person carry four amos in a public domain. The same applies here, and all the more so where this saves the Jew from the authorities, for if it were not permitted he might lose the entire debt because of even a brief delay. Beit Yosef asks why Maharam did not permit it simply because customs collection involves no Torah prohibition. He answers that customs collectors ordinarily must write. He further answers that regarding instructing a gentile outside the context of a mitzvah, there is no distinction between a Torah and Rabbinic prohibition. Beit Yosef also asks that public work is forbidden even in the gentile’s house, as stated in seif 4, and answers that this case is different because of financial loss. He then asks that the Sages permitted the case in Mi SheHechshich only to prevent a greater Torah violation, namely carrying four amos in a public domain, whereas here customs collection involves only a Rabbinic prohibition. Why should it be permitted? He answers that customs collection too may involve a Torah prohibition because the Jew may write. According to his explanation, however, the leniency produces an undesirable stringency. It would follow that the leniency applies only where there is concern that the Jew may write. In places where no writing is done, especially in small communities where customs collectors do not keep written records, hiring the gentile would be forbidden because there is no risk of a Torah violation. The poskim should then have distinguished between such cases, yet they did not. I am also astonished at the holy words of the author of the Shulchan Aruch, for the chapter Mi SheHechshich shows that concern for financial loss can justify permitting a lesser Rabbinic prohibition in order to prevent a more severe Rabbinic prohibition as well. The Gemara had to find an arrangement the person would not otherwise be allowed to use, and states that the Sages were not concerned for a minor loss, implying that they were concerned for a major loss. Even in a courtyard there is no Torah prohibition. It is logical to arrange matters so that a person does not come to violate a more severe prohibition, even if both are Rabbinic, by allowing the lesser Rabbinic prohibition because of the loss. So too here: customs collection involves no Torah prohibition, but among Rabbinic prohibitions there is a severe one, namely that the Jew collect the customs himself, and a lesser one, namely that he lease it to a gentile. Since one of them will certainly occur, it is preferable to allow the lesser prohibition, for we can testify that otherwise he will commit the more severe one, even though both are Rabbinic. One may ask why siman 243 did not likewise permit leasing a bathhouse to a gentile because of financial loss, lest the Jew commit the more serious violation of employing the gentile as his worker. It appears that the Sages permitted this only where a major loss is involved, such as customs duties and minting, which generally involve substantial enterprises. They permitted the category generally even where a particular instance is small. A bathhouse and similar matters do not ordinarily involve such a large loss. Moreover, when the Jew initially established the bathhouse he knew that he could not operate it on Shabbos even through a gentile. There is therefore no serious concern that he will violate the more severe prohibition. This appears correct.

<b>שלא ישב הישראל אצל העכו"ם. </b> פי' אותו הישראל שהשכיר לו או שלוחו ויליף לה במרדכי ממה דאי' ס"פ בכל מערבין לא יהלך אדם בתוך שדהו לידע מה היא צריכ' כו' ונ"ל דאף בזה שאסור לישב שם היינו כדי לידע מה היא ההתעסקות שעוש' העכו"ם וכדי לקבל ממנו חשבון ע"ז למחר אבל אם אינו יושב שם אלא כדי לשמור את העכו"ם שלא יגנוב אלא יניח לתיבה המכס מה שיקבל מותר וראיה ממ"ש שמור לי פירות שבתחומך כו' ותו ראי' בפ' הזהב (בבא מציעא דף נ"ח) השוכר את הפועל לשמור את הפר' כו' ולשמור הזרעים אין נותנין לו שכר שבת הרי שעכ"פ עושה היתר בשמיר' רק שאין נותנין לו שכר ע"ז בשבת משום שכר שבת ואמר שם בסיפא דאם היה שכיר חדש מותר אפי' ליקח שכר שבת ש"מ שמותר להיות יושב ושומר בשבת וה"נ כן היא כנלע"ד ובזה ניח' מה שנהגו מחזיקי ריחיים מהשר להושיב שם ישראל אפי' בשבת רק יזהרו שלא ידברו כלום בהעסק:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>That the Jew should not sit beside the gentile.</b> Meaning, the Jew who leased the enterprise to him, or the Jew’s agent. Mordechai derives this from the end of the chapter Bechol Me’arvin: a person may not walk through his field to determine what work it requires, and so forth. It appears to me that the prohibition against sitting there likewise applies only when he sits to observe the gentile’s activity so that he can receive an accounting the following day. If he sits merely to guard the gentile from stealing and to ensure that the customs receipts are deposited in the chest, it is permitted. A proof is the rule, “Guard for me the produce within your techum,” and so forth. Another proof appears in the chapter HaZahav, Bava Metzia 58a: one who hires a worker to guard the red heifer, or to guard seeds, does not pay him Shabbos wages. This shows that the guarding itself is permitted, but he may not receive separate payment for Shabbos because of the prohibition of Shabbos wages. The conclusion there states that if he is hired by the month, he may even receive payment for Shabbos. Thus sitting and guarding on Shabbos is permitted. The same applies here. This explains the custom of those who lease mills from the ruler and station a Jew there even on Shabbos, provided they are careful not to discuss the business at all.
