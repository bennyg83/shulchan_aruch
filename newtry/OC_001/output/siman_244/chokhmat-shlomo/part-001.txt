<b>Seif 1: He contracts with the non-Jew for the work.</b> Note: I copy here briefly what I answered to someone from the community of Zalishchik concerning his father-in-law's distillery. Renting his father-in-law's distillery to a non-Jew certainly works, since his father-in-law himself holds it by lease. Thus, whichever way one looks at it, if leasing does not effect acquisition, it is not his but belongs to the first non-Jew; and if leasing does effect acquisition, the second non-Jew acquires it. Nevertheless, it is preferable to write that he sells him all his rights. One could argue that this is not a true either-or: since the father-in-law possesses documents effective under the law of the kingdom, even if leasing does not effect acquisition under Torah law, he acquired it under secular law. The second non-Jew, however, who lacks such documents, may not acquire it under secular law but only under Jewish law, and perhaps leasing does not effect acquisition. Therefore, one should use language of sale, or write the document in German on stamped paper so that it is effective under the law of the kingdom; then leasing language suffices. Regarding ashes not yet in existence, they should write that they sell all the ashes when they come into existence and enter their possession. For additional assurance, they should obligate their persons and property to provide and transfer the ashes to the non-Jew when they come into existence and enter their possession. Such an obligation is effective even regarding something not yet in existence. Moreover, even though one does not acquire something not yet in existence, if he has taken possession we do not remove it from him. Here too, since the sellers do not in fact retract and the non-Jew works on Shabbos for himself, he is regarded as having taken possession, and it is his. Nevertheless, for greater assurance they should write as I have stated, based on what I answered to the community of Zalishchik.<br><b>Seif 1, in the gloss: Even if he lives among non-Jews, one must be concerned for visitors who come there.</b> Note: See Magen Avraham, seif katan 8, concerning the permissive custom in our city. See what I wrote in a responsum to the community of Faltishan, in my work on Yoreh Deah, sixth edition, siman 220, prohibiting this as a matter of law for several reasons and bringing proof for Eliyah Rabbah's view. See there, with Hashem's help.<br><b>Regarding the gloss's statement, “Even if he lives among non-Jews, one must be concerned for visitors who come there or for members of his household, who may suspect him.”</b> Note: See Magen Avraham, who asks from the Sabbatical year and answers that the Sages were lenient regarding the Sabbatical year. Concerning the meaning of this leniency, I saw in Responsa Noda BiYehudah, first edition, Orach Chayim, that he explains it is because the prohibition is relatively light. From this he derives that the same applies to Chol HaMoed, whose prohibition is likewise light, so visitors need not be taken into account. In the recently published Yeshuos Yaakov on Orach Chayim, the explanation is that the Sabbatical year is a long period, and therefore the Sages were lenient. At first glance, the Gemara appears to refute Noda BiYehudah. If Chazal distinguished between a lighter and a graver prohibition, and Noda BiYehudah himself writes that labor on Chol HaMoed is a light prohibition comparable to the Sabbatical year, what is the Gemara's question in the first chapter of Avodah Zarah, 21b: “Is renting a bathhouse to a Kuthean permitted? But the Kuthean performs labor in it on Chol HaMoed.” Perhaps suspicion was considered only for Shabbos and Yom Tov, which are severe, but not for Chol HaMoed, which is lighter. This would seemingly disprove Noda BiYehudah's reasoning. Upon careful analysis, however, Magen Avraham's intent must indeed be that the Sabbatical year is lighter. Yeshuos Yaakov's explanation, that the leniency is due to its long duration, cannot be accepted. Had it been permitted to rent to a non-Jew on weekdays and prohibited only for Shabbos, one could say that the loss caused by Shabbos is not as great as that of the Sabbatical year. But since renting it altogether is prohibited because of Shabbos, the financial loss is greater than during the Sabbatical year. I later saw that Yeshuos Yaakov himself noticed this difficulty and forced an answer. Therefore, in my humble opinion, Noda BiYehudah's explanation is primary. The question from Avodah Zarah can be resolved as follows. Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel states there explicitly that “this non-Jew performs labor in it on Shabbosos and Yamim Tovim.” He includes concern for labor on Yom Tov. The primary prohibition of operating a bathhouse on Yom Tov derives only from the verse, “that which may be eaten by every person,” since bathing is not a need common to all. This is a prohibition inferred from a positive commandment, and the original prohibition is not thereby restored, as Tosafos writes in Beitzah 12a regarding “for you” and not for offerings. Thus, the prohibition of something not common to all is no more severe than Chol HaMoed, which according to several poskim is itself Torah-level. The Gemara therefore asks correctly: since you are concerned even for labor on Yom Tov, which is only a positive-commandment prohibition, then a Kuthean's labor on Chol HaMoed is comparable to the bathhouse prohibition on Yom Tov. But Rama here discusses only Shabbos. In comparison with Shabbos labor, the Sabbatical year, and likewise Chol HaMoed, is certainly lighter. Regarding bathhouse labor on Yom Tov, one may indeed say that visitors are not considered. This also explains why the Gemara does not challenge the Mishnah itself by asking that renting a bathhouse to a non-Jew should be permitted. The Mishnah may be understood to prohibit it only because of Shabbos, not Yom Tov; one can then say that the Sages were lenient regarding Chol HaMoed while Shabbos is severe. Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel, however, explicitly includes Yom Tov. Bathhouse labor on Yom Tov, which is only a prohibition inferred from a positive commandment, is comparable to Chol HaMoed, which is also Torah-level according to several authorities, as I explained in the laws of Chol HaMoed. The Gemara's question is therefore sound, and Noda BiYehudah's ruling is upheld. See also my novellae to the laws of Chanukah, where I brought proof from another source against Noda BiYehudah, and my novellae to Avodah Zarah concerning several errors I found in Yeshuos Yaakov's treatment of this sugya. I also saw that Yeshuos Yaakov asks on Taz, who challenged Rama's permission where no observers are present on the ground that anything prohibited because of appearance is prohibited even in private chambers. Yeshuos Yaakov wonders why Taz did not ask the same question about the Gemara's ruling that beyond the techum is permitted. The question is no question. The rule that a matter prohibited because of appearance remains prohibited even in private applies only where observers could in principle be present, though presently they are absent, and where their absence results merely from the manner in which one chooses to perform the act. In such a case Chazal did not distinguish, lest people say that all ordinary loaves are prohibited but the loaves of a particular household are permitted. Beyond the techum on Shabbos and Yom Tov, however, observers cannot arrive because of an independent prohibition. Since there is no possible avenue for observation and the barrier is not merely the present arrangement but a separate prohibition, Chazal did not enact a decree there. The principle of uniform application does not apply in such a case. This is clear and straightforward.

<b>There, seif 6: A Jew who purchases the customs rights and rents a non-Jew, etc.</b> Note: Regarding a case in which townspeople rent the bathhouse to a Jew for the entire year, and whether the renter may sublet the Shabbos days to a non-Jew, see my work on Even HaEzer, ninth edition, siman 192, in a responsum to the province of Wallachia, where I discussed this incidentally.
