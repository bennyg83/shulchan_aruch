<b>Se'if 1: The strings must be spun for the sake of the mitzvah, etc.</b> Beit Yosef cites Rabbeinu Yerucham's view that whenever it is sometimes possible to fulfill both the positive and negative commandments, then even if circumstances arise in which both cannot now be fulfilled, the positive command does not override the negative command. A proof appears in Makos 8a, where Rava says: Since, if one found wood already chopped, chopping would not be a mitzvah, even now chopping is not a mitzvah. The entire passage shows that whenever the mitzvah can sometimes be fulfilled without a particular act, that act is not considered the mitzvah even when it is presently impossible to fulfill the mitzvah without it. Consequently, it does not override a negative command. The same passage also appears to prove the law concerning preparatory acts for a mitzvah: whether they override a negative command and whether they are considered to occur at the same time as the mitzvah. When circumstances could have arisen in which these preparations were unnecessary, the preparatory act is not considered the mitzvah and a negative command is not overridden for it. When the mitzvah cannot be performed without these preparations, they are considered part of the mitzvah, occur at the time of its fulfillment, and override the negative command. This is proven by the cited Gemara, which with Hashem's help provides clear evidence for these laws. See my Torah commentary, Parashas Shemini, from the year 5613, where with Hashem's help I brought another proof. Regarding the universal objection to this Beit Yosef from Shabbos 133b, where the Gemara answers, 'If another person is available, etc.; the case is where no other person is available,' see what I wrote in my second edition, in a responsum to the holy community of Semyatitch concerning circumcision. See also my second edition on Menachos 72, where I supplied another proof. The cited responsum appears in my work on Orach Chayyim from the year 5599. In Hilchos Shofar, siman 586, I likewise discussed this in the passage concerning a shofar of Rosh Hashanah with which one may not traverse, etc.; see my work from that year. I was asked to explain Ramban's words in Milchamos, chapter 2 of Shabbos. Ramban explains that Beit Shammai holds kilayim is forbidden in tzitzis. The adjacency of the verses is no difficulty, he says, because the verse may teach the reverse; that a positive command does not override a negative command. The questioner asked: Why is a verse necessary to teach non-displacement? Logic itself says the positive command does not override the negative one, as the Gemara asks, 'From where do we know that a positive command overrides a negative command?' This implies that without a source it would not override it, and Tosafos write likewise in the first chapter of Yevamos. I answered that Ramban's words are readily understood. To permit initially overriding a negative command with a positive command, a verse is indeed necessary. Without a verse, logic does not establish which is stronger: Perhaps the negative command is stronger, or perhaps they are equally strong. Therefore, initially the positive command does not override it, and the Gemara properly asks for a source that permits such displacement. The reverse question arises after the fact, regarding lashes where one violated the negative command while fulfilling the positive one. Logic would then suggest that they are equal: What makes one stronger than the other? He should therefore not receive lashes after the fact; even if their relative strength were merely doubtful, he could not be lashed in doubt. Beit Shammai consequently needs the verse to teach that a positive command does not override a negative command even after the fact and that the violator receives lashes, because the negative command is more severe than the positive command. This is Ramban's intent. The matter is well resolved. This also resolves the views of Rambam and Ramban cited by Tosafos Yom HaKippurim in the passage concerning feeding a dangerously ill person the less severe prohibition first. They hold that when the choice is between produce of Shevi'is and neveilah, one feeds him neveilah because a positive command is more severe. This is apparently contradicted by Yevamos 7a, which says, 'A positive command overrides a negative command; is the negative command not more severe than it?' The Gemara explicitly implies the reverse. See what I wrote concerning this in Hilchos Sukkah, siman 631, in the passage about the principle that since an object serves as a wall for a sukkah, and in my Orach Chayyim work from the year 5601. It now appears that Ramban follows his own position, which he proved from the Gemara through the question and answer just discussed, and Rambam agrees with him. They were troubled by Tosafos's and Ramban's question: Why does Beit Shammai need the adjacency of the verses concerning kilayim and tzitzis? It must be resolved as Ramban does; the adjacency teaches that the positive command does not override the negative one. Under our explanation, it teaches that even after the fact the violator receives lashes. This proves that the positive command is at least equal to the negative command, and perhaps more severe. Otherwise there would have been no initial thought that fulfillment of the positive command should at least exempt him from lashes after the fact. Consequently, it is preferable to feed the dangerously ill person neveilah regardless: If the positive command is more severe, neveilah is certainly preferable; if the two are equal, either may be fed to him, while choosing Shevi'is risks violating the more severe positive command. Rambam and Ramban understand that the statement on Yevamos 7 that the negative command is more severe is R' Shimi bar Ashi's explanation of the Baraisa. He disagrees with Rav Yosef on 6a and holds that one who does not expound adjacent verses does not expound them even in Mishneh Torah. One can therefore say that Beit Shammai does not expound adjacent verses. On that approach, the negative command may indeed be more severe than the positive command, while the positive command overrides it only by a specific Scriptural decree; for Beit Shammai, no such verse exists. We, however, rule like Rav Yosef, who says that even one who generally does not expound adjacent verses does so in Mishneh Torah. His unqualified statement implies that all agree. Tosafos's and Ramban's question therefore returns: According to Beit Shammai, why is 'You shall make twisted cords' placed adjacent to the prohibition of shaatnez? It must be answered as Ramban does: The adjacency teaches that the positive command does not override the negative one. Yet non-displacement initially was already known by logic. The novelty must therefore concern the after-the-fact case and teach that he receives lashes. Without the verse we would at least have said that he is not lashed after the fact. This proves that the positive command is at least equal to the negative command and is not inferior to it, and it may be stronger; if it were inferior, logic alone would require lashes and no verse would be needed. No disagreement with Beit Shammai is found concerning this relative assessment, for all can agree to it. Rav Yosef therefore teaches this principle, and it follows that one feeds the dangerously ill person neveilah regardless of which possibility is correct. This appears to me a very sound resolution of Rambam's and Ramban's position. Since the source for the principle that a positive command overrides a negative command is kilayim in tzitzis, I will record here a branch of thought that arose while I studied Shevuos 15b. The Gemara asks: 'Let us build it on Yom Tov and consecrate it on Yom Tov.' It answers that construction of the Beis HaMikdash does not override Yom Tov. Rashi explains that Scripture says, 'You shall observe My Shabbosos'; from this the Sages learned that construction of the Beis HaMikdash does not override Shabbos, and the same applies to Yom Tov, which is called Shabbos, etc. This is very difficult in light of Tosafos on Yevamos 5b. After saying that the whole principle derives from shaatnez, Tosafos writes: 'One can answer that there the mitzvah is not thereby lost, because it can be burned after Yom Tov. Nevertheless, without the verse specifying the second morning, I would not have distinguished this, while that which overrides, etc.' If so, the Gemara's question here remains: 'Let us build it on Yom Tov.' Construction of the Beis HaMikdash may not override an ordinary Shabbos or Yom Tov because it can be built afterward and the mitzvah is not lost. On Shavuos, however, if consecration must occur specifically through the chametz loaves, there is no other means and the mitzvah will be lost. It should therefore override Yom Tov and be entirely permitted. What, then, is the Gemara's objection? This requires very great study. I am also troubled by Yevamos 6a, where the Gemara says: The reason is that the Torah wrote 'You shall observe My Shabbosos'; otherwise we would have thought that construction overrides Shabbos. The Gemara answers that the anticipated violation is the negative command against driving an animal on Shabbos, etc. According to Rashi here, it could instead have answered that the verse is needed for Yom Tov, which is also called Shabbos. The phrase 'the day after the Shabbos' regarding the Omer is understood as the day after Yom Tov. Here too, then, the verse may address Yom Tov, which entails only a negative command. Why did the Gemara need to reduce the case to the negative command against driving an animal? This too requires study. Incidentally, another difficulty should be mentioned. The Gemara there says that even according to the view that consecration can be achieved with one of the two thanksgiving loaves, both together constitute one mitzvah. Why is that difficult? Perhaps one loaf truly suffices, but two are required because the mitzvah includes eating the loaf. A single loaf cannot be eaten because it will be disqualified upon leaving its existing boundary. They therefore bring two: The area is consecrated through the first and the second is eaten. In truth, one could have sufficed for the consecration itself. This too requires study. Above I cited Rabbeinu Yerucham's view brought by Beit Yosef and the questions raised against it. See further my work Ner Mitzvah, in the introduction and at the end of part 2, and my Orach Chayyim work from the year 5612, page 140, in Hilchos Pesach, siman 475, where with Hashem's help I wrote sound resolutions. Study them carefully.

<b>Se'if 11, in the gloss: It is preferable to measure the width of the knot, etc.</b> I do not know why this should be so, because an explicit Mishnah establishes that the hem is included as part of the garment. Keilim 28:7 states: Regarding the measure of three by three that the Sages prescribed, R' Shimon excludes the hem, while the Sages require an exact three by three. This explicitly proves that the hem is otherwise part of the garment. Why, then, should it not be counted within the thumbbreadth measurement? Study this carefully.
