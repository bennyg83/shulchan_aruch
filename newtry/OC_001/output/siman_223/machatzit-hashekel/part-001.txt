**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Seif katan 4: His father died, etc.</b> See Yoreh Deah, siman 340, seif 6, regarding who is considered an upright person and the law of tearing one’s garment for an upright person. See Taz, who writes that the custom is not to mention Hashem’s name and kingship over another person unless one was distressed by his death. Regarding a Torah scholar, however, Heaven forbid, one must certainly mention Hashem’s name and kingship.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Seif katan 5: Utensils, etc.</b> Mitzvos were not given for personal benefit, and therefore one cannot recite a blessing over the benefit. If he would instead recite Shehecheyanu over fulfilling the mitzvah, the answer is that blessings are recited over mitzvos only when they occur from time to time.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Seif katan 6: At the time, etc.</b> For we rule that an artisan does not acquire ownership through the improvement he makes to an article. This is also implied in siman 600. Meaning, siman 600 states that because the two days of Rosh Hashanah are considered one extended day, when reciting Shehecheyanu during Kiddush on the second night it is preferable initially to wear a new garment, so that in any event he must recite Shehecheyanu because of the garment. He certainly did not purchase it on Yom Tov. Thus, Shehecheyanu over the garment would seemingly have been recited at the time of purchase. Necessarily, siman 600 discusses one who purchased cloth and gave it to a tailor to make into a garment, in which case he recites the blessing when wearing it. Nevertheless, the statement that we rule that an artisan does not acquire ownership through the improvement of an article must contain a scribal error. First, how would the rule that an artisan does not acquire ownership cause the blessing to be recited when the garment is worn? Furthermore, this is not actually a settled ruling. It is disputed whether an artisan acquires ownership through the improvement he makes. Thus, Yoreh Deah, siman 120, discusses one who gave silver to a non-Jewish artisan to fashion into a utensil. According to the view that an artisan acquires through the improvement, it is as though he purchased a utensil from a non-Jew and it requires immersion with a blessing. According to the view that an artisan does not acquire through the improvement, it requires no immersion. Rama therefore rules that it should be immersed without a blessing. The same dispute appears in Choshen Mishpat, siman 306, as discussed by Sema and Shach, and in Even HaEzer, siman 28, regarding kiddushin. In all these places it is clear that the poskim dispute the issue. Perhaps the text should read: “There is an opinion that an artisan does not acquire through the improvement of an article.” The meaning would be as follows. If we definitively ruled that an artisan does acquire through the improvement, then even if the owner recited Shehecheyanu when purchasing the material, when the artisan completed the article and returned it he would have to recite Shehecheyanu again, because he now acquired the artisan’s improvement, as though he had purchased another article. For the same reason, according to that opinion the article requires immersion with a blessing. Since, however, there is an opinion that an artisan does not acquire through the improvement, according to that view, once the owner recited the blessing at the time of purchase he would not recite it again after completion. Why should he enter into a doubt? It is therefore preferable not to recite the blessing at the time of purchase, and then he will certainly recite it when the article is completed.
