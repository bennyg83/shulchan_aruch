**** ENGLISH ****
<b>As written in Yoreh Deah, etc. </b>Regarding Havdalah. He must mean that this refers to what he wrote above, that one may pray until midday; alternatively, something is missing from the Magen Avraham. In Yoreh Deah it is explained that one whose relative died on Shabbos should eat that night without Havdalah, because an onen is exempt from Havdalah. After the burial, he should recite Havdalah until the end of Tuesday. He should not, however, pray Maariv as a compensatory prayer. Although we rule that one who erred and did not pray Maariv prays Shacharis twice, as below in siman 108, that is because during Maariv on Motzaei Shabbos, when the obligation took effect, he was exempt, and therefore he need not compensate for it. This applies specifically to Maariv, whose prescribed time is only at night, while during the day it can be recited only as compensation. Havdalah, however, was initially instituted by the Sages through the end of Tuesday and is not compensatory. Therefore, although he was exempt at night, he must nevertheless recite Havdalah during the day. Accordingly, Shacharis resembles Havdalah, since its initial prescribed time extends until midday. Bircas HaShachar, however, in the Magen Avraham's view, resembles Maariv there and is not made up, because he was exempt when its obligation took effect. The Sages assigned each blessing a fixed time, as stated in the Gemara and likewise in the Tur above, siman 46: when one awakens from sleep he should say Elokai Neshamah; when he hears the rooster he should say, 'Who gives the rooster understanding,' and similarly for all the blessings. Only now, because hands are not clean, they instituted arranging them in the synagogue, as above in siman 46. Alternatively, it can indeed be said that since he was exempt when the obligation took effect, etc. Nevertheless, I do not know why proof from Havdalah is needed to show that he may pray Shacharis until midday, since this is stated explicitly there in Yoreh Deah. Perhaps the words 'regarding Havdalah' are a scribal error and should read, 'regarding Maariv of Motzaei Shabbos.' He would then be bringing proof only that he is exempt from Bircas HaShachar. In my humble opinion, however, he is certainly obligated to recite Bircas HaTorah, because it resembles Havdalah. Likewise, if his relative died in the morning after he arose but before he recited Bircas HaShachar, he must recite Bircas HaShachar after the burial, since he was obligated when their time arrived. I am also uncertain and ask whether, even if the relative died at night, he is not obligated after the burial the next day to recite the three blessings, 'Who has not made me a non-Jew,' 'a slave,' or 'a woman.' These are mentioned in Menachos 42, and the Gemara assigns them no fixed time. Although they are recited in the morning because of the mitzvah of acting promptly, their time extends throughout the day, and they therefore resemble Havdalah or Shacharis until midday. According to the kabbalists cited by the Magen Avraham in siman 46, seif katan 14, who explain that these blessings were instituted because the soul of a non-Jew, slave, or woman did not attach itself to his soul during the night, one may indeed say that their principal time is in the morning. According to the Shelah and Bach cited there, however, who explain that they were instituted for the beginning of creation, I do not know whether their principal time is specifically in the morning. Alternatively, since in the morning he becomes a new creation, as above at the beginning of siman 4, he must bless Hashem for the beginning of his creation in the morning, and their principal obligation is therefore then.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Seif katan 2: We do not, etc. </b>His question, 'Why is this different from his married sister, etc.?' means that the Yerushalmi gives two reasons why an onen is exempt from mitzvos. First, because of the honor of the deceased, for if he occupies himself with another matter, it appears that he is unconcerned with the deceased's needs. Second, because there is no one else to bear his burden and attend to the burial needs if he occupies himself with a mitzvah or another matter. The Yerushalmi says that the practical difference is where someone else can bear his burden. According to the second reason, he is then obligated in all mitzvos, while according to the first reason, the honor of the deceased, he remains exempt. Rabbeinu Tam acted accordingly when his married sister died, ruling that since she had a husband who was obligated to bury her, there was someone else to bear his burden, and the laws of aninus did not apply to her brother. He thus followed the second reason of the Yerushalmi. The Beit Yosef, however, rules that the exemption applies even where the obligation to bury does not rest upon him, because he follows the Rosh, who rules according to the view that the reason is the honor of the deceased. He therefore writes that even if the onen wishes to be stringent, he may not do so. It is consequently difficult why he then writes that if someone else will attend to the burial needs, the onen may be stringent, which appears to follow Rabbeinu Tam. The answer is that he appointed another person in his place. Meaning, even according to the reason of honoring the deceased, there is no affront when he personally appointed someone else, for a person's agent is like himself, and it is apparent that he is concerned for the deceased's honor. In the first case, however, although the sister has a husband upon whom her burial rests, there remains an affront to the deceased when her brother also does not participate in her burial.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Seif katan 3: He is obligated, etc. </b>The Bach rules, etc. The Beit Yosef raised a contradiction between the Tur's statements. Here in Orach Chaim the Tur writes that toward evening, since he can go beyond the boundary after nightfall, he is exempt, implying that he is exempt even if he does not actually go. In Yoreh Deah, siman 381, the Tur writes, 'if he goes beyond the boundary after nightfall,' implying that if he does not do so, he is obligated. The Bach resolves this and rules that the passage there concerns other mitzvos, which do not require concentration. Therefore, if he does not go beyond the boundary, although he is somewhat preoccupied because he could do so, he remains obligated. Here, however, the discussion concerns Krias Shema, which is recited with awe and requires additional concentration. The Tur therefore holds that even if he does not actually go beyond the boundary, since he could do so and is somewhat preoccupied, he is exempt.

With respect to Krias Shema, it is entirely inapplicable to speak of exemption or obligation, whichever way one approaches it, as the Magen Avraham writes. Rather, the Tur's purpose is to teach that this rule should not be extended to all the mitzvos in which an onen is obligated throughout Shabbos and Yom Tov, for he is exempt from them while going beyond the boundary after nightfall. Since he did not write this because it was necessary for the laws of Krias Shema, he was not concerned to clarify his wording and relied upon what he wrote in the Tur, Yoreh Deah, which is the principal location of this law. If the passage refers to the evening Krias Shema, whose time according to our practice begins while it is still daytime, he should nevertheless be exempt even if he does not go beyond the boundary after nightfall.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Nevertheless, on Motzaei Shabbos everyone, etc. </b>Meaning, on a weekday permission to recite the evening Krias Shema before nightfall was granted only because of the burden upon the congregation, and they rely upon the Krias Shema recited before bed, as explained below in siman 236. An individual was therefore not permitted to recite it before nightfall. Certainly on Motzaei Shabbos, when even the congregation recites it at night, its time begins only after the emergence of the stars, and the Magen Avraham's question against the Bach returns. As a matter of practical law, the Taz writes that in such a case, if he will not go beyond the boundary, he should recite Krias Shema before nightfall as on a weekday. Responsa Shevus Yaakov, part 1, siman 8, cites a certain authority who ruled like the Taz and further permitted him to pray the Motzaei Shabbos prayer on Shabbos after plag haminchah, as stated below in siman 293, that in pressing circumstances one may pray the Motzaei Shabbos prayer after plag haminchah and also recite Havdalah. That responsum disagrees with this authority. Even if we say that he should recite Krias Shema, it is certainly improper for him to pray and recite Havdalah, because this combines two contradictory positions. You obligate him in prayer on the ground that it is still Shabbos and aninus has not taken effect, yet how can he then pray a weekday prayer? If it is a weekday, aninus applies and he may not pray. These are the responsum's words. In my humble opinion, I do not understand this, because as long as he may not attend to the deceased's needs, aninus does not apply, and the reason is not the sanctity of Shabbos. Even after he prays a weekday prayer and recites Havdalah, he remains forbidden to perform melachah until night, as stated below in siman 293. Thus, although he prays a weekday prayer, aninus still does not apply, since he is not yet able to attend to the deceased's needs. The Taz further writes that even on a weekday, if a son has someone else who will attend to the needs of his father or mother, he should go to the synagogue and recite Kaddish, because this honors his father or mother. Eliyah Rabbah challenges the Taz from his own words, for in Yoreh Deah the Taz writes specifically that he should recite Kaddish on Shabbos, implying that he should not do so on a weekday. In my humble opinion, this is no difficulty. There the Taz discusses the usual case, in which the son has no one else to attend to their needs, for who will be more concerned to attend to them than the son? He therefore writes specifically Shabbos and Yom Tov. Regarding the principal ruling, however, the Nekudas HaKesef and Avodas HaGershuni disagree with the Taz, because Kaddish was instituted to save the deceased from Gehinnom, and before burial there is not yet any judgment in Gehinnom. The aforementioned Shevus Yaakov agrees with the Taz, arguing that, on the contrary, the remedy should precede the affliction, just as prayer should precede affliction. Nevertheless, that responsum concludes that he had not seen people follow this practice, though if an authority ruled that the son should recite Kaddish, the ruling is not reversed.
