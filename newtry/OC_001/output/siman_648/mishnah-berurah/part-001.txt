This is especially so where nothing is missing, such as if it was punctured by a needle, for there is a double doubt here: perhaps the halachah follows the 'some say' mentioned in se'if 2, that even by through-and-through it is invalid specifically when there is deficiency.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Unless it returns, etc.</b> Meaning, where we know the nature of the esrogim of that place, that their way is afterward to return to the appearance of an esrog after they remain in a vessel for a long time, then even while it is still green it is valid, for since it returns to its appearance, its fruit necessarily finished developing. Nevertheless, the later authorities agreed that one should not rely on this in practice, and one should not buy esrogim unless it has begun somewhat to return to the appearance of an esrog, lest it remain green. The poskim agreed that a grafted esrog is invalid, for it is not called an esrog at all. Grafted means from an esrog and lemon or orange or any other fruit, even if he layered a branch from an esrog tree into a lemon tree; all the more so if he layered a branch from a lemon tree into an esrog tree. But if he grafted two esrog trees together, such as a branch of a tree whose fruits are thin and small into a tree whose fruits are large, it is valid. [Eliyah Rabbah in the name of Bach] There are three signs by which we can recognize whether it is grafted or not. First: the grafted one is smooth, while an esrog has small protrusions over its entire body, and they are raised. Second: in the grafted one the stem protrudes, while in an esrog the stem is sunken. Third: the inside of the grafted one is broad, its juice is abundant, and its middle peel, meaning between the upper peel that is thin like a glaze and the inside, which is the seed chambers with their peel, is thin. In an esrog it is the opposite: the peel is thick and the inside is narrow and almost dry. In Olat Shabbat he wrote another sign, that in an esrog the seed stands upright along the length of the esrog, while in a grafted one the seed lies across the width of the esrog. But in Bikkurei Yaakov he wrote that he checked the esrogim common among us, which have all the signs of a valid esrog, and sometimes the seeds lie lengthwise and sometimes widthwise; see there. Therefore one cannot distinguish at all by this sign. In Responsa Chatam Sofer, Orach Chayim, siman 183, he wrote that esrogim that have the external signs, namely the sunken stem and many protrusions, need not be suspected lest the internal signs be found opposite to this. It is implied from this that if it has only one sign indicating that it is an esrog while the second sign indicates that it is grafted, such as if it is smooth but its stem is sunken, one should be stringent because of doubt. So wrote Mor U'Ketziah. However, in siman 207 the Chatam Sofer greatly questions the matter of the signs, since they are not mentioned in the Talmud, and his view is that in practice one should not rely on signs at all to be lenient. The law of esrogim is like the law of a kosher bird, which is eaten by tradition; meaning that the place must be established from ancient times that its esrogim are not grafted. He concludes there: what emerges from this is that all esrogim that are not from Yanova, and the same applies to another place established as valid from ancient times, should not be taken without a certificate of kashrut where the one testifying knows that they are not from the grafted ones, and one should not rely on the signs. End quote. Something similar is also written in the book Shenot Chayim, siman 270; see there. However, on the second day of Yom Tov, which is rabbinic, one may rely on the signs even according to the one who holds that a grafted esrog is invalid also on the second day of Yom Tov; see there. In truth, however, regarding a certificate of kashrut there are also many matters to be precise about. First, one must find people who know well the signature of the one testifying, or at least that the merchant himself recognizes the certificate, and he must be established for us as trustworthy. Second, regarding esrogim that are not already established as valid, to testify that they are not grafted, the one testifying should be believed only if he is established as trustworthy, and he must be God-fearing enough to be exact, lest he make a mistake, especially regarding the work of planting. There are also several common deceits in this matter: he places the certificate on one box, and the merchant, when he sells that box, places the certificate on another box. Therefore one must be careful to buy from a trustworthy person. If one finds an esrog to buy from places that have always been known and established as not grafted, it is better to buy from them. See Biur Halacha, where we wrote in the name of Bikkurei Yaakov. According to what is explained there, it appears that after the fact, when he already bought it, or when he has no places known to be established, it appears that he may rely on the two external signs and also bless, as I wrote above. Know that the later authorities agreed that one should be stringent regarding a grafted esrog even on the other days. If he has no other in the city, he should take it all seven days without a blessing. Some are stringent that even to take it without a blessing is also not worthwhile, lest he come to become habituated to fulfill with it always. Regarding hadasim, if he knows they are grafted, he should not take them, for they are invalid like esrogim. But where there is no specific indication, it implies from the later authorities that there is no concern, for ordinarily they are not grafted; see Peri Megadim in siman 649, letter 11. And that which Shevut Yaakov wished to invalidate those that grow in the gardens of nobles because of concern for grafting, the Chacham Tzvi and Panim Me'irot disagreed with him. See Bikkurei Yaakov, who wrote that the widespread custom is to validate them.
