**** ENGLISH ****
<b>(There, se'if 7 in the gloss)</b> However, as a matter of law one should not invalidate unless the dad was removed. Note: see Magen Avraham se'if katan 9, in what he wrote: and one can say that in any case we require that all of it be removed, etc., and therefore they wrote the language 'removed,' etc. Indeed, this is implied in Yoreh De'ah, Hilchot Tereifot, in several places, that the term 'removed' implies all of it. A support for this appears from what the poet wrote: 'when he falls, he shall not be cast down, for Hashem supports his hand.' This proves that the language 'cast down' implies entirely. This is a meaningful sign, with Hashem's help; examine it.

<b>(There, se'if 11)</b> If it is half and half in one place, etc. Note: see what I wrote in the Taz, that the inferences in the mishnah are difficult because they contradict each other: at first it states that if it arose on most of it, it is invalid, implying that half and half is valid; and afterward it states that if it arose on a minority of it, it is valid, implying that half and half is invalid; see there. In my humble opinion it seemed possible to resolve this as follows. A deficient esrog is valid on the other days, and there are differing views about this below. In the view of the Ran brought in the gloss below, it is proven that he holds that specifically if most of it remains intact it is valid, but not when half and half remains. From the words of Mahari that the Magen Avraham brought, it implies that he holds that even if only a minority remains intact it is valid. In my humble opinion it would seem to decide that if most of it was removed and only a minority remains intact, it is invalid like the view of the Ran, for in that case his reason applies well, that it is not called an esrog but only pieces of esrog. That is, since most of it was removed and only a minority remains, it is called only a piece of esrog, and therefore it is invalid. But if half remains, since in any event most of it was not removed, it is not called a piece from an esrog, since the majority was not removed; in this the law follows Mahari, that it is valid. Therefore it works well to say that there is a distinction here between the first day of Yom Tov and the other days. On the other days we require specifically that it arose on most of it. But with half and half, even if from the standpoint of boil-like discoloration it invalidates even at half and half, nevertheless it is valid, for it is no worse than if it were removed, and is considered in any event as though it were absent. Since if it had been removed from the esrog it would be valid, even though it has not yet been removed it is valid, for anything in one's power is not considered lacking an act, as the poskim wrote regarding a Torah scroll on Shabbat, that if it is in his power to repair it, it is valid. So too here. But on the first day of Yom Tov, when a deficient esrog is invalid, then whichever way you view it: if we judge it as it is now, even at half and half it is not beautiful; and if we judge it as though it were removed, it is again deficient and invalid. Therefore it is valid only when it is on a minority, for then the name of boil-like discoloration is not upon it. Therefore the mishnah stated the clear case: if boil-like discoloration arose on most of it, it is invalid even on the second day of Yom Tov, for this is invalid whichever way you view it, as above; and if it arose on a minority of it, it is valid even on the first day of Yom Tov. But with half and half there is a distinction: on the first day of Yom Tov it is invalid, and on the second day of Yom Tov it is valid. Although the implication of the poskim does not appear to make this distinction, nevertheless the reasoning indicates as I wrote. It would further appear possible to say that since the laws of esrog are compared to tereifot, as stated in the Talmud and Tosafot on daf 36, if so one can say the same here: since by tereifot, two defects render it a tereifah, here too by esrog it is so. Therefore, if boil-like discoloration arose on most of it, it is invalid even without another defect joining it. But on a minority it is valid if there is no other defect. If there is another defect, it is invalid even when that joins with a minority of boil-like discoloration. Therefore at first it states that if boil-like discoloration arose on most of it, it is invalid even without another defect; and afterward it states that if boil-like discoloration arose on a minority of it, it is valid, even though there is another defect, namely that its stem was removed, for even with all these defects joined it is valid. But at half and half, if there is no other defect it is valid, but together with another defect it is invalid. Now Tosafot wrote in this sugya, daf 35b, s.v. alatah chazazit al rubo, that from the Yerushalmi it sounds somewhat that it is invalid specifically on the first day of Yom Tov, as I explained at the beginning of the first chapter. I am puzzled why they brought this Yerushalmi, since our Talmud there implies the opposite, from that which it asks: on the contrary, in two or three places it appears spotted and is invalid; and it concludes that it refers to the latter clause, etc. It is difficult: since in the end one must emend and reverse the reading from valid to invalid, why must it further answer that it refers to the latter clause? It could have remained as at first, that it refers to the first clause, only that the intent is as follows: since the mishnah mentioned specifically boil-like discoloration together with punctured and deficient of any amount, it implies that it is invalid only on the first day of Yom Tov, like punctured, and as Tosafot wrote at the end of the chapter. Therefore it properly said: they taught this only in one place, for then it is invalid only on the first day of Yom Tov and valid on the second day; but in two or three places, it is spotted and invalid even on the second day of Yom Tov. For from the language of the Talmud it is proven that two or three places are worse than one place on most of it, since it asks with the language 'on the contrary.' If so, it should have answered this way, that in two or three places it is invalid even on the second day of Yom Tov. Since it does not answer this way, it is proven that it was obvious to it that boil-like discoloration on most of it is invalid all seven days. If so, why did Tosafot bring the words of the Yerushalmi here? This requires investigation. According to the above, it appears to explain the language of the Yerushalmi, which says: Rabbi Yosi said in the name of Rabbi Shmuel: all invalidations invalidate only on the first day of Yom Tov. There they say: and most of it on one side, etc. At first glance it seems from his words that in two or three places it is better and is valid, while in our Talmud it implies the opposite. See Shiyarei Korban, who addressed this and pressed himself in his commentary there to resolve it. According to what I wrote, it works well: this continues from above, since all invalidations invalidate only on the first day of Yom Tov, but on the second day of Yom Tov they are valid even with boil-like discoloration. Therefore it properly says that when it is on most of it on one side, it is invalid only on the first day of Yom Tov; but in two or three places it is invalid even on the second day of Yom Tov, for it is like spotted. This works well and is correct. I saw that the Rosh brought the above Yerushalmi and explained it according to the Korban HaEdah's above explanation; why did he not explain more simply that the intent is that even on the second day of Yom Tov it is invalid in two or three places? I saw there that he disagrees with the Yerushalmi on logical grounds. This is puzzling: why does he not prove more strongly from our Talmud that it necessarily does not hold this way, since it does not explain the words of Rabbi Chanina in such a manner? This requires investigation.

אם אינו שוה לשאר הכתב הוי כמנומר כן ה"נ באתרוג למעלה מחוטמו כל מראה דאין דרך אתרוג להיות כן הוי כמנומר ופסול. וכן למטה משיפוע בג' מקומות נראה דפסול מטעם מנומר דלא כדעת הפני אריה וכן אנו נוהגין וכוונתינו לש"ש ויהיו לרצון לפניו ית' אמן. והנה בדין אם גזל ממנו אתרוג טוב אם די להחזיר לו אתרוג גרוע עמ"ש בזה בחיבורי לב"ק פרק מרובה (בבא קמא בדף ע"ח) ע"ש בעזה"י מ"ש ליישב דברי המהר"מ מינץ ע"ש. והנה בדין אם היה מתחלה אתרוג יפה ואח"כ נולד בו איזה שינוי מראה מחמת משמוש היד וכדומה אם נפסל מכח זה או לא. הנה כמדומה שכתבתי מכבר בענין זה ושכחתי מקומו. והנה נראה לכאורה ראיה מפירש"י בסוכה דף ל"א ע"ב בד"ה כיון דא' רבא אתרוג בימין וכו' וא"כ הוי גדול יותר מדאי שמא יפול מידו ויפסול שיארע בו נקב עכ"ל. וקשה למה נקט דוקא נקב הול"ל שיהיה בו שינוי מראה כי יתלכלך ויהיה בו שינוי מראה. ולדעתי זה ניקל יותר באתרוג להיות בו שינוי מראה מנקב ונקב אינו פוסל רק כאיסר ושינוי מראה פוסל בכ"ש ועכ"פ הו"ל למנקט תרווייהו נקב או שינוי מראה. ובע"כ נראה דס"ל לרש"י דשינוי מראה אינו פוסל רק אם בא מחמת עצמו אבל מחמת דבר אחר אינו **** ENGLISH ****
<b>(There, se'if 12)</b> Boil-like discoloration and any change of appearance invalidate in any amount, etc. Note: I saw in Sha'arei Teshuvah of the Gaon Moharaz, of blessed memory, in this siman, in the name of Responsa Pnei Aryeh, that he tends to validate a red appearance. In my opinion, Heaven forbid to say so. True, from the language of the Shulchan Aruch in se'if 16, where he used the language 'if it is black or white' and did not mention red, it implies that red is valid. But this is no proof. First, one can say that he stated it for novelty: even black or white, if not on most of it, is valid, and all the more so red; but if it is on most of it, or from its nose in any amount, even red is invalid. And even if we say that specifically black or white is invalid and not red, nevertheless it appears that this is specifically below, just as there we rule that it invalidates only on most of it or in three places, so too we rule that red is valid there. But above, from its nose, where any amount invalidates, red also invalidates. A proof is from the language of the Shulchan Aruch in se'if 12, where he used the language that any change of appearance invalidates there. From this language it implies that any change of appearance invalidates, whenever it is not the appearance of an esrog. Perhaps the Pnei Aryeh also agrees to this, that above its nose it invalidates. And what the Pnei Aryeh wrote, that redness is common in apples, what proof is that for an esrog? Because it is called an apple, should its appearances be valid? The apple's normal way is to be green, and according to several opinions that is invalid in an esrog. Also, we see that with white and black, if it is in two or three places it is invalid, even though each small amount by itself does not invalidate; nevertheless in three places it appears spotted and is invalid. If so, here too above its nose, if it is not like the bright appearance that an esrog should have, it is invalid, for it is like spotted and invalid. Indeed, with a Torah scroll, even according to the one who holds that passing the pen over the Name is effective, nevertheless with several Names it is invalid because it is like spotted; even though the writing by itself is fit to be in a Torah scroll, nevertheless if it is not equal to the rest of the writing, it is like spotted. So too with an esrog above its nose: any appearance that is not the normal way of an esrog is like spotted and invalid. Likewise below the slope, in three places, it appears that it is invalid because of spottedness, unlike the view of the Pnei Aryeh. So we practice, and our intention is for the sake of Heaven; may they be acceptable before Him, blessed be He, amen. Now regarding the law if one stole from him a good esrog, whether it is enough to return to him an inferior esrog, see what I wrote about this in my work on Bava Kamma, chapter Merubah, daf 78; see there, with Hashem's help, what I wrote to reconcile the words of Maharam Mintz. Now regarding the law if at first it was a beautiful esrog and afterward some change of appearance developed because of handling by the hand or the like, whether it is invalidated because of this or not. I seem to have written previously about this matter, but I have forgotten its place. At first glance, there appears to be a proof from Rashi's explanation in Sukkah 31b, s.v. keivan de'amar Rava esrog beyamin, etc.: if so, it is too large; perhaps it will fall from his hand and become invalid, for a hole will occur in it. End quote. It is difficult: why did he mention specifically a hole? He should have said that it may have a change of appearance, for it will become soiled and have a change of appearance. In my opinion, it is easier for an esrog to have a change of appearance than a hole, and a hole invalidates only at the size of an issar, while change of appearance invalidates in any amount. At least he should have mentioned both: a hole or change of appearance. Necessarily it appears that Rashi holds that change of appearance invalidates only if it comes from itself, but not if it comes from another matter; especially when it comes through another matter, since it can be washed and removed, it does not invalidate. However, one can reject this, for the difficulty still remains that he should have mentioned that its pitma or stem might be removed. Therefore it appears that Rashi holds that the concern is not only because of invalidity, for what of it if no prohibition will result from it? Rather, the main concern is that it will become invalid and he will not know this, and he will fulfill the obligation with it, thereby failing to perform the mitzvah and making a blessing in vain. Therefore Rashi holds that this applies only with concern for a hole, for since there we require the measure of an issar, perhaps he will imagine that it does not have the required measure and err to validate it, and he will come to prohibition and failure of the mitzvah. But where there is no concern of error, and the invalidity is open and there is no concern of mistake, we are not concerned lest it fall and become invalid. Therefore Rashi explained a hole, and there is no proof from here for this law. Examine it. See what I wrote about this in my work on Orach Chayim, year 5601, in siman 649, in a responsum to the holy community of Odessa, to Rabbi Moshe Ze'ev, may his light shine; see there and examine it.

<b>(There, se'if 21)</b> A green one that resembles the grasses of the field is invalid, etc. Note: regarding the law of a green esrog, see the poskim and the Taz. I found in Tikkunei Zohar, tikkun 21, Ostroh edition, daf 35, this language: and if she is green, she is more praiseworthy, in the image in which Esther was greenish; Esther was. End quote. One should not say that the intent is a yellow appearance, for Esther is called Hadassah after the hadas, as written in the holy Zohar, and hadas is green. And even if we explain his intent as yellow appearance, in any case, from the fact that he wrote that if it is green it is praiseworthy, it is proven that in any case if it is not green it is also valid; and it is proven at least that a green appearance is also valid. Examine this well.
