<b>Or another person plucked them, etc.</b> Maggid Mishneh's version of Rambam, however, reads: “another person plucked them for eating,” meaning a person who does not need to fulfill the mitzvah with this myrtle because he has another one, as stated in the Gemara there. See Taz and Magen Avraham. The poskim omitted the discussion there concerning when it first turned black, etc., because the Gemara asks, “Resolve it from this,” etc., and then says that Rav Pappa himself, etc. This implies that if we rule stringently, even an object disqualified from the outset remains disqualified, as in Rabbi Yirmiyah's question, because for purposes of stringency mitzvos are treated like sacred offerings, and concerning sacred offerings even an initial disqualification remains effective, as concluded in chapter 9 of Pesachim, chapter 1 of Kiddushin, chapter 1 of Zevachim, the final chapter of Kerisos, and elsewhere. He inferred from Rashi's words, “two and one,” meaning two leaves from one node and one leaf from below, etc., that Rashi disqualifies specifically in such a case. If the single leaf is above the other two, it is possible that Rashi would regard it as valid. The principal basis of the leniency he proposed, however, is that one may follow Baal HaHashlamah, who maintains that one may recite a blessing when one of the four species is missing. **** ENGLISH ****
<b>And some authorities invalidate it, etc.</b> They explain that Rabbi Tarfon's word “severed” does not mean that the top was cut off. Rather, it follows the wording of Toras Kohanim, Parashas Emor: Rabbi Yishmael says, “the fruit of a beautiful tree,” etc., and even two shoots and one that is not severed; Rabbi Tarfon says, etc. The same appears in the Yerushalmi there. In Rabbi Yishmael's statement the word “even” should not be read; rather, the text is, “and two shoots,” etc. Rabbi Abba bar Mamal asked Rabbi Ami: just as Rabbi Yishmael allows additional myrtles, should he likewise allow additional specimens of the other species? Rabbi Ami answered: Do you suppose that Rabbi Yishmael's position concerns beauty? We learned that Rabbi Tarfon says, “Even if all three are severed.” No person would say “even” unless he agreed with the preceding ruling. Rabbi Chaggai asked Rabbi Yosa: What did Rabbi Tarfon add to Rabbi Yishmael's words? He answered: Rabbi Yishmael does not regard a severed specimen as beautiful, whereas Rabbi Tarfon does regard it as beautiful. The explanation is that Rabbi Abba and Rabbi Chaggai initially thought that “severed” described a superior feature, namely the upper soft branches of the myrtle, which are its beauty and are the “shoots” mentioned by Rabbi Yishmael. Rabbi Tarfon called them “severed,” because anything broken while still soft is described as plucked. This refers to the soft branches that were plucked, as in the expression, “I shall pluck the tender shoot,” while “one that is not severed” means a branch broken from the hard trunk, for breaking is the term used when a hard object is fractured. Rabbi Ami therefore answered: Do you think Rabbi Tarfon meant this as a superior feature? If so, why did he say “even”? According to that reading, Rabbi Tarfon would require specifically all three to be severed and would not agree with Rabbi Yishmael. Rabbi Chaggai raised the same question, and Rabbi Yosa answered that “shoots” was stated as a deficiency. It means that the trunk is not whole, but a branch was cut from one side and another branch from the other side, and these are counted as two myrtles, while “one that is not severed” means that the entire trunk remains whole with all its branches. This explains the Gemara's statement that one unsevered myrtle is uncommon, meaning that it is uncommon for a myrtle to remain completely leafy in that form. According to the other commentators, however, such myrtles are very common. Baal HaMaor writes similarly. Ramban, in Milchamos, objects that according to this interpretation there is no reason for myrtle to differ from the other species. He therefore follows Rashi and Rif: Rabbi Tarfon validates a severed myrtle because its beauty lies in its leafiness. Furthermore, the Yerushalmi expressly discusses beauty, whereas the trunk itself has no greater beauty than the side branches. He explains “shoots” as branches whose tips vineyard workers customarily cut so that they will grow more, and Targum Yonasan likewise renders the phrase as having removed its top. The entire give-and-take concerning a severed myrtle addresses the preferred initial fulfillment. See there and Ran. Raam maintains that the severed myrtle mentioned in the Mishnah is valid only after the fact, as explained above in siman 645, seif 6, comparable to the conclusion in chapter 6 of Pesachim regarding remnants that became tamei, which establishes that they are invalid initially.
