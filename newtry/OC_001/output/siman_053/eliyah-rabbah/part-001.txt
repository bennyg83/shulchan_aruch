Responsa Maharam Mintz, siman 81, writes that if there is a dispute or quarrel between two householders, the chazzan must be careful to remain passive, both in speech and in action, openly and secretly. If he himself has any quarrel or enmity with individuals or with one member of the congregation, he must place the matter before the rabbi or the congregation for mediation and act in accordance with whatever they deem proper. [10] <b>On an occasional basis.</b> Bach and Nachalas Tzvi conclude that on fast days and the Days of Awe, even occasional service is forbidden. Olas Tamid writes: If the congregation wishes to waive its honor, practical determination is required as to whether they have the authority to do so. I am astonished, for Bach concludes with clear proofs that the congregation cannot waive this even according to Rashba, and that this is the view of Shulchan Aruch. Ateres Tzvi and Sheyarei Knesses HaGedolah write likewise, and this also appears to me from Chiddushei Rashba at the end of the first chapter of Chullin. See there, for I have abbreviated. Perhaps Olas Tamid is uncertain only regarding occasional service on Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur. Yet it appears that occasional service on Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur is equivalent to permanent service during the rest of the year, where waiver is ineffective, as is explicit in Bach and Sheyarei Knesses HaGedolah there. Magen Avraham writes that siman 579 implies that on fast days an actual elder is required, and a beard that has merely filled out is insufficient. Responsa Mishpat Tzedek writes that one disqualified because of a transgression may not serve even occasionally. Maharam writes: You asked whether a man whose arms have fallen away is fit to serve as chazzan. It is obvious that he is fit. Physical blemishes disqualify only kohanim, not a chazzan. See below, siman 128, where kohanim are disqualified because people stare at them, a ruling originating in the Gemara. On the contrary, appointing such a person is a particularly choice fulfillment, for the King of kings desires to use broken vessels, as it is written, “A broken and crushed heart,” etc. Maharshal and Bach cite this. Derishah concludes: This is also my view, with proof from a blind person who may lead the service. All this implies that he is fit even as a permanent chazzan. This is explicit in Yam Shel Shlomo, Chullin, chapter 1, siman 48, and in his proof from leviim, who are fit for the musical service despite bodily blemishes. This is unlike Responsa Mahari Bruna, cited in seif 3, which permits him only occasionally. See Zohar, Emor, page 173, which implies that a chazzan with such a blemish is forbidden, as reflected in certain customs. A distinction may be drawn. See Chavos Yair, siman 176, who overlooked all the sources cited above.

[16] <b>Because of burdening the congregation, etc.</b> Yam Shel Shlomo, siman 50, writes that he may not prolong the service against the congregation's wishes, even on Shabbosos and Yomim Tovim. Even with their consent, excessive length is disgraceful, for it fulfills neither “half for Hashem” nor “half for yourselves.” See Responsa Emunas Shmuel, siman 16, concerning an elderly chazzan whose voice is low and weak and who prolongs the prayer, that he may be removed. <br><b>Responsa Bach writes</b> that there is no prohibition in singing in the synagogue melodies that are sung in non-Jewish houses of worship, except for melodies specifically designated for their worship. If the melodies are not specifically designated for them, there is no prohibition. Tur writes that qualification as chazzan does not depend upon distinguished lineage, etc. Nevertheless, if two candidates are equal, one of distinguished lineage certainly takes precedence over one who is not, and this should be carefully considered, for the prayer of a righteous person who is the child of a righteous person is not comparable to that of another. Bach writes this in the name of Maharshal, and Taz and Magen Avraham cite it. I likewise saw it in Yam Shel Shlomo, Chullin, chapter 1, siman 48. Yet Responsa Maharshal, siman 20, writes that this distinction applies specifically when one prays for himself, because the merit of his ancestors assists him. One who prays on behalf of the congregation, however, is especially esteemed before Hashem when he has abandoned the deeds of his ancestors and followed Hashem's ways. Magen Avraham writes likewise, as does Yam Shel Shlomo itself in chapter Gid HaNasheh, siman 17. Taz also reaches this conclusion independently. It is surprising that these great authorities overlooked Maharshal's statement cited above. I proved the distinction in Eliyah Zuta from the interpretation in Asarah Maamaros of the account in which Rabbi Eliezer recited twenty-four supplications and was not answered, while Rabbi Akiva descended and was answered. The Gemara explains that this was not because one was greater than the other, but because Rabbi Akiva yielded regarding his personal traits. Asarah Maamaros explains that he had departed from the deeds of his ancestors, for Rabbi Eliezer possessed distinguished lineage whereas Rabbi Akiva did not. This appears to conflict with the principle that the prayer of a righteous person who is the child of a righteous person is superior. We must therefore say that the prayer of Rabbi Eliezer and Rabbi Akiva was on behalf of the congregation. In such a case, when both are righteous, the one without distinguished lineage is superior. Yitzchak, however, prayed for himself, that he should merit children, not for the congregation, and therefore a righteous person of distinguished lineage was superior, because ancestral merit assisted him. The rationale appears from Ramban's responsum concerning the statement, “The entire world is sustained for the sake of Chanina My son, while Chanina My son is satisfied with a kav of carobs from one Shabbos eve to the next.” Ramban explains that his merit was needed for others and therefore did not benefit him in this world, but it benefited the members of his generation. The same applies here. When praying for himself, a righteous person of distinguished lineage is superior, for his own merit does not benefit him but his ancestral merit assists him. When praying for the congregation, his own merit does benefit them, and a righteous person without distinguished lineage is superior because his merit is greater, since he abandoned the deeds of his ancestors. This appears correct. There is, however, some difficulty from Tur below, siman 579, regarding a chazzan for fast days, who must not have a sinner among his children, household, relatives, or anyone attached to him. It may be answered that living relatives are worse, as stated in Shevuos 39a: There is no family containing a tax collector whose members are not all considered tax collectors, because they protect him. Other resolutions are also possible. Maharshal writes in Chullin there that a chassid takes precedence over a tzaddik, provided he has a pleasant voice. Zohar, Vayechi, writes similarly on the verse, “Raise your voice, daughter of waves.” Knesses HaGedolah writes in the name of Responsa Ri Bruna that a chazzan whose voice became impaired is disqualified if it sounds trembling and broken. He further writes that when two chazzanim are equal, a kohen precedes a levi, a levi precedes a Yisrael, and a Torah scholar precedes an ignoramus even when the latter is a kohen, and certainly when he is a levi.

[23] <b>Even an individual, etc.</b> For who can offer another person’s sacrifice without his consent? This is from Beit Yosef. It accords with what Rabbi David Abudraham writes on page 47: Know that a chazzan stands in place of the kohen who offers the sacrifice, and therefore the chazzan is also called a “karov,” as stated in Midrash Shir HaShirim: When Rabbi Elazar son of Rabbi Shimon died, his generation applied to him the verse, “Perfumed with myrrh and frankincense, with all the powders of the merchant,” because Rabbi Elazar possessed mastery of Scripture, Mishnah, and the sacrificial service, and composed liturgical poems. For this reason, the liturgical poems recited by the chazzan in the midst of the prayers are called kerovos. It is also possible that they are called this because they are recited within the prayers and in their midst. This concludes his words. It follows that the common form “kerovetz” is a corruption. Beit Yosef in siman 68 explains it as an acronym for “The sound of rejoicing and salvation is in the tents of the righteous,” but this is an error. The correct term is kerovos, ending with the letter tav, as Abudraham also calls them on page 21. See Maadanei Melech, page 42, who overlooked the words of Rabbi David Abudraham.
