<b>In seif 10, in the gloss: </b>“For he has not yet reached thirteen years of age.” Note: See Magen Avraham, who writes that when they pray Maariv at night, he may lead the congregation. See also Shach, Choshen Mishpat, siman 35, Sema there, and what I wrote in my work there. At present, however, this matter requires study in light of the Gemara, Rosh Hashanah 6b, which asks: “How can a year without festivals occur?” The question is difficult. It can occur where one vowed on the first night of Pesach. Upon the arrival of the first night of Pesach the following year, a complete year has already elapsed, while three festivals have not yet elapsed, because an entire festival is required, and here the festival has only begun. This accords with Tosafos below, 7b, s.v. veharei regalim, who write that the prohibition does not take effect from the evening. Thus, whether he vowed on the first night of Pesach or on its first day, as soon as the next Pesach night begins a year has elapsed, but three festivals have not. It must therefore be that thirteen full years and one additional day are required before one is considered thirteen years old, just as one is not considered one year old until the following day. Accordingly, according to the view that the festivals need not be in their proper order, the same applies within a single festival. If part of the days of Pesach pass in the following year, once the number of days omitted from the first Pesach has passed, those days join with the earlier days of Pesach and the three complete festivals are counted. It therefore cannot occur that a year passes without the festivals. If he vowed on the first day of Pesach, the year is not complete until that day has passed, and by then three festivals have also passed. This is a clear and powerful proof. See what I wrote on this matter in my novellae to Choshen Mishpat there. Analyze this carefully.

<b>Seif 15: </b>“A regularly appointed chazzan, etc., should not wait until they tell him, etc.” Note: I saw in the Shulchan Aruch of the saintly Gaon of Vilna, of blessed memory, that he found this ruling difficult in light of Arachin 11b, where the Gemara says that even a regularly appointed functionary first seeks the congregation’s consent. See there. In my humble opinion, with due respect, these are two separate matters. Here the Mechaber means that another person who is not regularly appointed should not initiate the matter and ask to lead the prayers, because doing so appears arrogant, as though he considers himself worthy to lead. He must therefore wait until others first ask him, so that they initiate the matter with him. A regularly appointed chazzan, however, displays no arrogance and may initiate the matter by approaching the lectern. Therefore the Mechaber writes that a regularly appointed chazzan descends on his own and need not wait for others to ask him first. Nevertheless, even though he initiates and approaches first, he must still ask the congregation’s permission before beginning. Arachin discusses precisely this, that he initiates by asking them whether he should lead the prayers. This distinction is clear and correct in my humble opinion. Analyze it carefully.

<b>Seif 19: </b>“Some prevent, etc., and even an individual, etc.” Note: See Beit Yosef, who explains that prayer stands in place of an offering, and one cannot offer another person’s sacrifice without his consent. This is cited by Eliyah Rabbah. I find this difficult, because Tosafos in Kiddushin 23b, regarding whether the kohanim are agents of Hashem, imply the opposite. Their words indicate that if the kohanim were our agents, an owner could prevent a particular kohen from offering his sacrifice. It follows that because they are agents of Hashem, he cannot say that he does not want that kohen, and the kohen may offer it against his will. We rule that the kohanim are agents of Hashem, so the proof points in the opposite direction. Granted, one may answer that a chazzan is different because he is our agent. Nevertheless, Beit Yosef’s wording and rationale are imprecise, since if the chazzan were truly analogous to one who offers a sacrifice, an individual should not be able to prevent him. See the lengthy discussion I wrote, with Hashem’s help, concerning Tosafos’ words for the holy community of Lviv. It found its place in the addenda to Nedarim 35a and remains in manuscript. See there and analyze it carefully.<br><b>There: </b>In the same seif, “unless he had already consented to him initially.” Note: See my work Mishpat Tzedek on Choshen Mishpat, mahadura dalet, siman 188, and my comments to Shulchan Aruch, siman 231, in the responsum to the holy community of Kontshka. I explained that the Shulchan Aruch deliberately writes “unless he had consented initially,” rather than “unless he had not objected initially.” Mere silence may still allow him later to object and claim that he had erred in the law, believing that an individual had no right to protest, and that he has now learned that an individual may protest. Only where he expressly consented can he no longer raise any claim afterward. See there for the reasoning, with Hashem’s help, and analyze it carefully.
