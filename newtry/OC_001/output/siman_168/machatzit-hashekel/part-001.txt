מ"א ויש ט"ס בדבריו אלא שיראתי לשלוח יד להגיה וגם לא ידעתי באיזה ל' אגיה:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>(Seif katan 1) Coarse and small bread, at first glance it implies from the Tur, etc.</b> The Tur's language differs from that of the Shulchan Aruch, for the Tur writes that one blesses on the whole loaf even if it is coarse, while the pieces are clean bread, and even if the whole loaf is smaller than the pieces. Since he did not combine them and say, 'even if it is coarse and small,' as the Shulchan Aruch writes, one might say the Tur meant either one deficiency only: either it is coarse while equal in size, or it is small while equal in cleanness. But from the Gemara it appears otherwise, for it concludes, 'but slices,' etc., and speaks of large slices, since this is the case of the dispute. The Magen Avraham's proof is that, according to Rabbeinu Tam, the dispute of Rav Huna and Rabbi Yochanan is about large pieces against whole loaves; therefore the later case of a wheat slice and a barley whole loaf must also involve a larger slice. If so, the slice has two advantages, being wheat and large, yet whole barley would have been preferable were it not that wheat has precedence in the verse. Tosafot, however, also give other reasons to reject Rashi's explanation, and from the Rosh it appears that the wheat slice is smaller than the whole loaf, so the Magen Avraham's words require explanation. Still, Tosafot's wording, that even if the pieces are of clean bread and the whole loaves are coarse bread, one blesses on the whole when they are of one type, supports the Shulchan Aruch, and this seems to be the Magen Avraham's intent, though there may be a textual error in his wording.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>For according to Rashi and those who agree with him, etc.</b> According to Rashi, Rav Huna and Rabbi Yochanan disagree where the pieces are larger than the whole loaf: Rav Huna requires blessing specifically on the slice, and Rabbi Yochanan specifically on the whole loaf. Therefore a G-d-fearing person satisfies both opinions in that case, where the slice is larger and both are of the same species. But where the slice is wheat and the whole loaf is barley, the Gemara concludes that everyone agrees he blesses on the slice, and the G-d-fearing compromise does not refer to that case. Although we rule like Rabbeinu Tam, who explains differently and says that in a case of one species and a larger slice, one who blesses on the whole loaf satisfies all opinions, Rabbeinu Tam applied the G-d-fearing solution to the wheat slice only because that is stated explicitly, and we do not add further cases.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>(Seif katan 3) So that, etc.</b> But if it appears, etc., see siman 366. This means that the law comes from the Rokeach, who holds that joining helps, based on siman 366. There, although one may not make an eruv with a slice, if two halves of bread were joined and appear whole, one may make an eruv with it. The Rokeach holds the same applies here. Just as in siman 366, if the joining is apparent it is not called whole, so too here. <b>(Seif katan 4) He may, etc.</b> The Rokeach wrote in this language, etc. The Shulchan Aruch's wording implies the novelty that even on Shabbos he may join them and there is no prohibition of fixing. But the Rokeach's wording is clear that he may also join them for Shabbos so that through this it will be considered a whole loaf for lechem mishneh.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Since there is a dispute, etc.</b> Meaning, the Yerushalmi states that where there is clean impure bread and coarse pure bread, one blesses on whichever he wants. The poskim compare Jewish bread to pure bread and bread of non-Jews to impure bread, and therefore rule that with clean bread of a non-Jew and coarse Jewish bread one blesses on whichever he wants. The two opinions cited by the Agudah dispute how to understand the Yerushalmi: one holds it refers to someone who does not eat ordinary food in purity, so it can be learned for bread of non-Jews only where he is not careful about such bread; the second holds it refers even to someone who eats ordinary food in purity, and therefore even one careful about bread of non-Jews may bless on whichever he wants. **** ENGLISH ****
But there it is speaking, meaning in chapter 7 of Hilchos Chametz, where the Rambam wrote that if he kneaded with fruit juice he fulfills the obligation. This refers to where there is a little water in it, and in any case it must be speaking where there is a little water, as the Maggid Mishneh wrote there, for otherwise it cannot become chametz, and we rule that one does not fulfill the mitzvah with such matzah, as it is written: do not eat chametz with it, seven days you shall eat matzah, from which we derive that only something capable of becoming chametz qualifies for matzah. Since it contains a little water, it truly is bread and one blesses HaMotzi. This resolves the first difficulty from the Rambam and the Gemara in Berachos. But the difficulty from siman 188 remains, whether according to the first answer or the second, since the Ran wrote that on the other days one may eat rich matzah. According to the first answer, this means specifically where one mixed in one of the four items, honey, milk, etc., and one would not recite HaMotzi or Birkas HaMazon on it; according to the second, where it lacks any admixture of water, one likewise does not recite HaMotzi or Birkas HaMazon. How then can we say it is impossible not to eat bread? In truth, the Magen Avraham did not intend this difficulty from siman 188 as a challenge to the Rambam, for perhaps the Rambam does not accept the Ran's answer, but follows the poskim who say that the statement that on Yom Tov one cannot avoid eating bread refers specifically to the first night of Pesach and the first night of Sukkos. Rather, the Magen Avraham's challenge is to us, according to the Beit Yosef, who here copied the Rambam according to his interpretation, that if one mixed in honey, etc., he does not recite HaMotzi; yet below in siman 188 he seems to accept the Ran, as the Magen Avraham writes there. The Magen Avraham's answer is therefore sound: regarding the Rambam there is no difficulty, and regarding us, we may not accept the Rambam's rule that anything over which one does not recite HaMotzi and Birkas HaMazon cannot be used for matzah. Once the Magen Avraham concludes against the Beit Yosef, holding that one must recite HaMotzi even if fruit juice was mixed in, unless there is no water at all, and below in siman 471 he concludes that even a little oil makes it rich matzah so one does not fulfill the first-night obligation, then throughout Pesach one can eat rich matzah containing a little oil and nevertheless recite HaMotzi and Birkas HaMazon according to the Magen Avraham. This is unlike the Beit Yosef's view, that even if only a little oil was mixed in, if its taste is recognizable one does not recite HaMotzi; for then if the oil is recognizable, he would not recite HaMotzi, and if it is so little that its taste is not recognizable, it is not called rich matzah either, as the Magen Avraham agrees in siman 471.
