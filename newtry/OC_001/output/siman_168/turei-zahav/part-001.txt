<b>Since it is one of the seven species.</b> This appears at first to imply that spelt is not one of the seven species. This is difficult, for in siman 208 the Tur explicitly writes that spelt is included within wheat. It appears to me that at the beginning of Kilayim it states: “Wheat and zunin,” and so forth. Rash writes there: “This is difficult, for here it appears that spelt and wheat are kilayim with one another, whereas in Menachos it states that spelt is a species of wheat.” He answers that the latter statement concerns fulfilling the mitzvah of matzah, and so forth. This shows that distinctions exist in this regard. It is therefore correct to say here that spelt is not included among the seven species for all purposes in the same way as barley. Nevertheless, because for some matters it is included with wheat, the Tur writes in siman 208 that it is significant regarding Hamotzi. See also the end of siman 211.

<b>If he is not careful regarding gentile bread, etc.</b> The source of this law is plainly the Yerushalmi cited by the Beit Yosef regarding impure and pure bread. The authorities derived from there the law of gentile bread and Jewish bread, where the one reciting the blessing likewise has a choice. Tosafos conclude: “But Rabbeinu Shimshon, the officer of Coucy, instructed that the gentile bread be removed,” and so forth. Certainly the officer of Coucy had no authority to dispute the Yerushalmi. Rather, he did not compare gentile bread to the impure bread mentioned by the Yerushalmi. He held that Jewish bread is preferable and that there is no choice. Nevertheless, the matter was not entirely clear to him, so he instructed that the gentile bread be removed in order to avoid the doubt. Mordechai in the chapter Keitzad Mevarchin likewise records the words of Tosafos, but explains that according to the first opinion one has a choice regarding gentile bread only if he is not careful to avoid it. The Beit Yosef writes that this implies that if he is careful, he must recite the blessing over Jewish bread even if it is coarse. It follows that the officer's instruction applied even to one who was not careful, since Mordechai writes, “But the officer,” and so forth. According to the first opinion, the choice granted by the Yerushalmi concerning impure and pure bread likewise applies only to one who is not careful to avoid the impure bread; one who is careful must bless specifically over the pure bread. Accordingly, the words of the Shulchan Aruch are difficult. It writes that one who is not careful has a choice, while one who is careful should remove the gentile bread. This follows neither opinion. According to the first opinion, one who is careful should bless specifically over Jewish bread; according to the officer, the gentile bread should always be removed. There is a further difficulty. The Beit Yosef cites Terumas HaDeshen as understanding Raavyah in Mordechai to mean that even one who is careful, eating alone, and has before him clean gentile bread and coarse Jewish bread, has a choice. This is cited immediately afterward in the ruling, “If the homeowner is careful,” and so forth. It is clear that Raavyah does not agree with the officer. How, then, could the Shulchan Aruch first rule like the officer and immediately afterward rule leniently where one who is careful has with him someone who is not careful? In truth, the words of Terumas HaDeshen are independently difficult. He rules that it is a mitzvah for one who is careful to break bread over clean gentile bread, whereas the Yerushalmi grants a choice in such a case according to his explanation. Moreover, one would have to say that Raavyah disagrees with Mordechai's earlier statement that one who is careful specifically breaks bread over Jewish bread, which is strained. I have already written in Yoreh Deah, siman 112, that Raavyah permits gentile bread only where a guest who is not careful is eating with him. The Yerushalmi also speaks of one who is not careful. Raavyah brings proof from the Yerushalmi concerning the guest who is not careful, and the guest consequently has a choice according to the Yerushalmi. Here, where one who is not careful is eating with one who is careful, the latter must break bread over that which is preferred by the guest. When eating alone, he can say that he prefers coarse Jewish bread; when the matter depends on another person's preference, he cannot. According to this, the second difficulty is resolved. The earlier ruling to remove the gentile bread like the officer concerns one eating alone, where it is not permitted to him. The later ruling permits it only where he has a guest who is not careful, not as Terumas HaDeshen interpreted that Raavyah's mention of a guest was not specific. Nevertheless, a difficulty remains. Since one who is careful is prohibited from eating gentile bread, he has no intention of eating it; why should he remove it? Rama already rules in the name of Terumas HaDeshen that if one has no intention of eating the second type, one need not consider it at all. Terumas HaDeshen himself is not troubled by this because he follows his own view that even one who is careful and eating alone may eat it. According to my explanation, however, why did the Shulchan Aruch rule that one who is careful must remove it? This is even less relevant than a permitted food that he simply does not intend to eat. It therefore appears to me that words are missing from the Shulchan Aruch, and it should read: “If he is careful regarding gentile bread, he should bless specifically over Jewish bread even if it is coarse; and some say that he should remove,” and so forth. Everything is then clear, for it presents Mordechai's opinions accurately. Nor is there a difficulty from the later ruling concerning one who is careful and has with him someone who is not careful, for there the permission exists specifically because of the guest, as I explained above, not according to Terumas HaDeshen's interpretation. A proof is that the Shulchan Aruch writes, “If the homeowner is careful regarding gentile bread and a Jew who is not careful is eating with him.” Had it followed Terumas HaDeshen, there would have been no reason to mention that a Jew who is not careful was eating with him, since that detail would be nonessential. The Shulchan Aruch should have stated the basic novelty directly. This proves my explanation. My father-in-law likewise rules that one who is careful need not remove the gentile bread at all. Levush raises the following difficulty: Why did the Shulchan Aruch rule that where the homeowner is careful and no other person who is not careful is present, he should remove the gentile bread? Why should he not also have a choice for the same reason that permission was granted where another person who is not careful is present, as Terumas HaDeshen writes? Levush suggests that when another person is present he does not remove it because the clean bread is certainly preferred by that person and must be included in the blessing. When no other person is present and he does not wish to eat it, it is better to remove it. This is not satisfactory. If he has a choice when alone, then it is a mitzvah to give precedence to the clean bread, as Terumas HaDeshen writes and as the Shulchan Aruch immediately cites. If it is prohibited to him, then removal is unnecessary.

<b>י"מ פת כו' שממלאים כו' </b> פי' שרש"י מפרש פת הבאה בכיסנין היינו מצד המילוי כי העיסה עצמה נילושה במים אלא שמלאוהו בצוק"ר או דבש או אגוזים. ובכלל זה מה שעושין בפורים עיסה רחבה וכפולה וממלאין אותה בשומשמין ואין חילוק בין מילוי בפירות או בבשר ודגים ועמ"ש בסי' זה סי"ו ויש אומרים הוא הרמב"ם דס"ל דכיסנין הוא מצד העיסה עצמה שנילושה בדבש או תבלין ונסתפק ב"י אי נימא דוקא שנילושה בהרבה דבש והמיעוט מים. אבל אי הוה מיעוט דבש בטל לגבי רוב מים והוה פת גמור או דילמא אפילו   ע"י תערובות מעט דבש כל שניכר הטעם בעיסה מקרי כיסנין. וכתב שזה נראה עיקר. וכתב שנענין הלכה כיון דמידי דרבנן הוא יש להקל  כדברי כולם וכן כיסים הממלאים אותם בדבש משקדים בין עיסה שעירבו בה מי פירות או תבלין יש להם דין כיסנין עכ"ל ומו"ח ז"ל הקשה על זה למה אמר שהיא קולא הלא אם אינו מברך ברכה הראויה לה בין כך ובין כך היא חומרא משום שעובר על לא תשא. ולי נראה דלא קשה מידי דודאי ברכת בורא מיני מזונות פוטר הכל אפילו עיסה דהא זיין אלא שמפני חשיבות הפת קבעו עליו המוציא ושלש ברכות משום הכי כל שאין ברור שיש שם חשיבות ואינו בכלל כיסנין יש להקל שלא להצריך המוציא ויש על זה גם שם חומרא שלא לומר בחנם המוציא ושלש ברכות:

<b>Some explain that it is bread, etc., which they fill, etc.</b> Rashi explains pas haba'ah bekisnin in terms of the filling. The dough itself is kneaded with water, but it is filled with sugar, honey, or nuts. This includes the broad folded dough made on Purim and filled with sesame seeds. There is no distinction between a fruit filling and a meat or fish filling. See what I write in seif 16 of this siman. The “some say” is Rambam, who holds that kisnin is determined by the dough itself, which was kneaded with honey or spices. The Beit Yosef was uncertain whether this means specifically that it was kneaded with much honey and little water, so that a minority of honey would be nullified to a majority of water and the product would be full bread, or whether even a small admixture of honey, whenever its taste is discernible in the dough, is called kisnin. He writes that the latter appears primary. As a matter of practical halachah, since this is a D'Rabbanan matter, he rules leniently in accordance with all the opinions. Thus, pockets filled with honey or almonds, and dough into which fruit juice or spices were mixed, all have the law of kisnin. My father-in-law objects: Why is this called a leniency? If he does not recite the proper blessing, either way it is a stringency, for he violates “You shall not take.” In my view there is no difficulty. Borei Minei Mezonos certainly covers every form of dough, since it provides nourishment. Only because of bread's importance did the Sages establish Hamotzi and three blessings of Bircas HaMazon. Therefore, whenever it is uncertain whether the product possesses that importance and is outside the category of kisnin, one is lenient and does not require Hamotzi. There is also a stringent aspect in avoiding an unnecessary Hamotzi and three blessings.

<b>And some say that this is called full bread.</b> This means where it contains only a small amount of honey, even though its taste is discernible. Rather, it must contain much spice or honey and only a minority of water, so that the honey or spice is virtually primary. This is measured by taste, such that the flavor of the honey and spice is more strongly sensed than the flavor of the dough. Only then is it kisnin. Rama writes regarding this that such is the custom. It is obvious that if it contains much honey and little water, all agree that it is kisnin. The same applies if it was kneaded with milk and little water, but not to products made with only a small amount of butter or fat, even though their taste is discernible, for those are full bread. Therefore, the practice of baking shemaltz cakes for lechem mishneh on Shabbos is proper, since they contain only a small amount of fat. It appears that Rama disagrees only with this point in the Shulchan Aruch, that discernible taste alone creates the status of kisnin. He does not disagree with the earlier definition based on filling, nor with the later definition, “Some explain,” and so forth. Otherwise he should have written that all of those are full bread, especially if he disputed everything cited by the Shulchan Aruch. My father-in-law cites Maharshal that one should not recite Borei Minei Mezonos even over lekach unless the spice and honey are primary and there is only a small amount of flour. It appears that one need not be concerned for this, since it is an individual opinion. My father-in-law also writes that Purim kreplach are kisnin, and that the principal matter depends on the kneading: if kneaded with mostly honey and little water, they are kisnin.

<b>And if he eats them during the meal, etc.</b> This law also applies to pas haba'ah bekisnin mentioned immediately before, as Beit Yosef explicitly writes in the name of Shibolei HaLeket. The Tur and Shulchan Aruch state it here regarding lachmaniyos as an even greater novelty. It is more reasonable that lachmaniyos should be regarded as part of the meal than pas haba'ah bekisnin, since they are called bread, yet even so the blessing over the bread does not exempt their initial blessing. All the more so does this apply to pas haba'ah bekisnin. Mordechai in Keitzad Mevarchin presents the opposite reasoning in the name of Maharam. Regarding lachmaniyos he derives by a kal vachomer from kisnin that they require a blessing during the meal: if kisnin, over which one recites Hamotzi when establishing a meal, is nevertheless not exempted by the bread, and so forth. This appears difficult, for the Gemara also states regarding lachmaniyos, understood as nilush, that if one establishes a meal upon them he recites Hamotzi. It appears that regarding nilush there was doubt whether it is truly the meaning of lachmaniyos. Maharam therefore derives the rule by a kal vachomer from kisnin, regarding which all agree that an established meal is effective. The reason kisnin was clearer to him than lachmaniyos is that Rif explicitly states the blessing of Borei Minei Mezonos regarding kisnin and derives lachmaniyos from them. I will quote Rif: “The conclusion is that when one eats them in the manner of kisnin, he initially recites Borei Minei Mezonos and afterward nothing; when he eats them as an established meal, he recites Hamotzi and the three blessings of Bircas HaMazon, for Rav Yehudah said in the name of Shmuel that one recites Hamotzi over lachmaniyos, and we establish this as referring to one who established a meal upon them.” His meaning is that if one eats a small quantity, not as an established meal, it is by definition eaten in the manner of kisnin, so one recites Borei Minei Mezonos rather than Hamotzi. If one establishes an independent meal upon them, he recites Hamotzi, as the Gemara states regarding lachmaniyos. It follows that if one eats a small quantity even during a meal, he recites Borei Minei Mezonos, because it is unrelated to the meal and has the same law as fruit in siman 177. This law does not depend upon the eater's subjective intention, but upon the ordinary manner of eating among most people, as with kisnin. I write this because I saw in a gloss to Emek Berachah that it distinguishes between lachmaniyos and kisnin on one side and fruit on the other. Regarding fruit, even if one eats it during a meal as an established food rather than dessert, it is considered unrelated to the meal and requires an initial blessing. Regarding kisnin and lachmaniyos, however, it makes the law depend on the eater's intention: if eaten as dessert they require a blessing, while if eaten for satiation they are part of the meal and require no blessing. Its primary basis is that the Tur here says “if he eats them,” while in siman 177 concerning fruit it says “brought before him.” Such a structure need not be refuted, for it is merely homiletic. Beit Yosef, in the name of Shibolei HaLeket, actually uses the expression “brought” regarding these items as well. We have already demonstrated that there is no such distinction during the meal. Rif's essential distinction is between establishing an independent meal upon lachmaniyos without bread and not doing so. A distinction within a bread meal based on the eater's intention never existed. In truth, regarding fruit too, if it is eaten as part of the meal and constitutes his primary food, it requires no blessing. Likewise, if these products are his primary meal, they certainly require no blessing. The true distinction in both cases is whether he eats a small amount in circumstances where ordinary people would or would not establish a meal upon it. One should not listen to a view that causes an unnecessary omission of a blessing, and all the more so one should not make an unnecessary blessing over fruit merely because it is eaten as established food rather than dessert. <b>Furthermore,</b> I saw in that work that specifically kisnin and waflatkes require Borei Minei Mezonos during a meal, but hoizen blasen, which are extremely thin, fried in oil, and always require only Borei Minei Mezonos because they lack the appearance of bread, do not establish a blessing for themselves when served during a meal. Since their original batter was thick and they are not filled with anything, the bread exempts them. This does not appear correct to me. These hoizen blasen, called fritlach in other lands, certainly have the law of pas haba'ah bekisnin because they are fried in fat or another liquid. For that reason one recites Borei Minei Mezonos, not because they lack the appearance of bread, for they certainly do have the appearance of bread. It is therefore clear that one must recite Borei Minei Mezonos over them during a meal, like other kisnin. See what I will write, with Hashem's help, in seif 13 of this siman.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>If it was cooked, etc.</b> The Beit Yosef cites Rambam: “Bread that one broke into pieces and cooked in a pot, or kneaded with broth, if the pieces contain a kezayis or are recognizable as bread and their form has not changed, one recites Hamotzi. If they do not contain a kezayis or the appearance of bread was lost through cooking, one recites Borei Minei Mezonos.” His wording appears self-contradictory. At first, one favorable factor suffices, either a kezayis or the appearance of bread; at the end, two favorable factors appear necessary. The Beit Yosef cites explanations from great authorities and offers his own. My father-in-law also explains that the concluding clause concerns uncertainty: if there is no kezayis and it is uncertain whether the appearance of bread remains, or conversely if the appearance is certainly gone and there is uncertainty whether there is a kezayis, we rule stringently. I do not understand what uncertainty has to do with this, for no special expertise is needed to determine whether the appearance of bread remains, and all the more so whether there is a kezayis. It appears to me that Rambam holds that cooking creates a stronger bond than broth. The case is that initially no individual crumb contained a kezayis, but through cooking the crumbs adhered and became one. Adhesion through cooking is stronger than adhesion through broth, since pieces joined by broth can be separated again. Therefore, in cooking the only distinction is whether the appearance of bread remains. Regarding broth the reverse is true: there is no relevant change in the appearance of bread merely because broth joins the crumbs. Although the next ruling states that when crumbs are placed in water and the water whitens, this changes the bread, here it does not concern soaking in broth but merely crumbs joined by broth. There the law depends upon whether a kezayis existed before the adhesion. Rambam derived this from the Gemara. Initially the Gemara makes the law depend on whether the pieces remain intact, meaning whether they contain a kezayis, as Tosafos explains from the Yerushalmi, and it does not mention the appearance of bread. Later, in the statements of Rav Sheshes and Rava, the matter depends upon appearance and not upon a kezayis. One must therefore say that the discussion of intact pieces concerns broth, where a change of appearance is irrelevant, while Rav Sheshes and Rava concern cooking, where size is irrelevant because cooking creates a strong bond and the law depends upon appearance. Rambam thus properly states at first that there are two kinds of adhesion, one through cooking and one through broth, each with its own criterion. With broth there must be a kezayis, after which a change of appearance is irrelevant. With cooking the appearance must remain recognizable, while a kezayis is unnecessary. He then concludes that if the broth-bound pieces do not contain a kezayis, or if cooking destroyed their appearance, meaning that each case contains the defect relevant to it, one does not recite Hamotzi. This is why he writes at the end “or its appearance was lost through cooking,” which otherwise appears superfluous, in order to identify the context in which loss of appearance is relevant. This is the correct explanation of Rambam. As practical halachah, however, we have only the words of Rabbeinu Yonah copied here in the Shulchan Aruch.

The dispute here is whether Hamotzi has any relevance to it at all. If one eats it in the manner of kisnin, its blessing is plainly Borei Minei Mezonos. If one establishes a meal upon it, the law depends on the dispute cited at the beginning of this seif. Rama and the Beit Yosef already testify that the custom is to be lenient. I therefore know of no remaining doubt regarding eating a small quantity, except where it was fried in oil or honey and he wishes to eat enough to constitute an established meal, or where it was kneaded with water and therefore is not kisnin, in which case this law applies even to a small quantity. A God-fearing person should not eat an established quantity except during a meal. See also seif 17 below for more on this matter.

<b>ודוקא שאפאו בתנור כו'. </b> נראה דכתב זה מפני שקשה לו למה כ' כאן דמברך במ"מ בהך פשטיד"א ולעיל מסיק דירא שמים לא יאכל אלא תוך הסעודה וניחא ליה דכאן מיירי באפיה בלא משקה ובזה כ"ע מודים  דמברך המוציא וק"ל הא מסיק בש"ע לעיל ס"ז דאם יש מילוי בעיסה הוה פת כסנין ואין לך מילוי גדול יותר מפשטי"ד. ובס' עמק ברכה ראיתי שמחלק בין מילוי בשמי' למילוי בשר ודגים מטעם דבשר ודגים באים ללפת הפת וע"כ הוה טפל לפת משא"כ בפירו' ולא כוון אל נכון דהא הב"י כ' זה הדין בשם האגור וז"ל אע"ג דבשר ודגי' וגבינ' עיקר מברכין עליה המוצי' דמין דגן חשיב וסיים דבתר חמש' המינים אזלי' ש"מ דלא חשבי' לבשר ודגים לטפל דא"כ לא הוצרך לטעם דחשיבו' דגן וה' מינים וע"כ נלע"ד דלא נתכונו יפה בזה אלא דודאי יש בזה דין כסנין ואי קבע סעודה מברך המוציא וע"ז קאמר דה"א דפשטיד"א זו  אפי' קבע סעודה לא יברך המוציא מכח דבשר עיקר קמ"ל דלא ואע"ג דלא זכר כאן בש"ע דמיירי שקבע סעודה אין בכך כלום דאי בא הש"ע להשמיענו דאין בזה דין כסנין ויש חילוק בין מילוי בשר למילוי בשמים ה"ל להשמיענו זה לעיל בדין כסנין במקום שכ' דיש דין כסנין מחמת המילוי אלא ברור דאין חילוק דכמו שבשר ודגים באים ללפת הפת כן באים פירות ללפת את הפת כדאי' בסי' קע"ז בפירות שאוכל עם הפת וכאן ודאי הוה פת בכיסנין ויש בהן ברכת המוציא אם קבע עלייהו וקמ"ל דאפי' לאותה דיעה שכ' לעיל מזה דאפי' בקבע סעודה לא מהני אבל בזה מהני קביעות סעודה דיש בו שייכות המוצי' ובהא סליקנא ונחיתנ'  דטיגון במשקה הוה כסנין כדלעיל וה"ה במילוי בשר ודגים רש"י דנקט מילוי צוק"ר ובשמי' אורחיה דמלתא נקט ובפשטיד"א אורחיה במילוי בשר וכיוצא בזה ואפי' אפאו בתנור הוי כסנין כמו שאר פת של כסנין דאפאו בתנור:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Specifically when he baked it in an oven, etc.</b> It appears that this was written because of the difficulty why the ruling here states that one recites Borei Minei Mezonos over this pie, whereas above it concludes that a God-fearing person should eat it only during a meal. The answer given is that here it concerns baking without liquid, where all agree that one recites Hamotzi. This is difficult, for the Shulchan Aruch concludes above in seif 7 that dough containing a filling has the law of pas haba'ah bekisnin, and there is no greater filling than that of a pie. I saw in Emek Berachah that he distinguishes between a filling of spices and a filling of meat or fish, because meat and fish are eaten as an accompaniment to bread and are therefore secondary to the bread, unlike fruit. This is not correct. The Beit Yosef cites this law in the name of Agur: “Although the meat, fish, or cheese is primary, one recites Hamotzi over it, because grain is significant.” He concludes that we follow the five species. This shows that meat and fish are not regarded as secondary, for otherwise there would have been no need for the reason of the importance of grain and the five species. It therefore appears to me that they did not explain this properly. Certainly this has the law of kisnin, and if one establishes a meal upon it he recites Hamotzi. The teaching here is that one might have thought that even if he establishes a meal upon this pie he should not recite Hamotzi because the meat is primary; it therefore teaches that this is not so. Although the Shulchan Aruch does not explicitly state that it concerns one who established a meal upon it, that is no difficulty. Had the Shulchan Aruch intended to teach that this does not have the law of kisnin and that there is a distinction between a meat filling and a spice filling, it should have taught this above in the laws of kisnin, where it stated that a filling creates the law of kisnin. It is therefore clear that there is no distinction. Just as meat and fish may accompany bread, so fruit may accompany bread, as stated in siman 177 concerning fruit eaten with bread. Here it is certainly pas haba'ah bekisnin and requires Hamotzi if one establishes a meal upon it. The teaching is that even according to the opinion cited immediately above, that an established meal is ineffective for certain fried items, here it is effective because this item has a connection to Hamotzi. My final conclusion is that frying in liquid produces the law of kisnin, as stated above, and the same applies to a filling of meat or fish. Rashi mentioned a filling of sugar and spices only because that is the ordinary case, while pies are ordinarily filled with meat and similar items. Even when baked in an oven, it is kisnin like any other pas haba'ah bekisnin baked in an oven.
