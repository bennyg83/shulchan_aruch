**** ENGLISH ****
<b>It does not involve “you shall make.”</b> The remaining tzitzis have no invalidity. Beit Yosef explains in the name of a responsum of Rashba: If you were to say that a tallis that was partially torn becomes invalid unless all its tzitzis are removed, because some of the original corners that made it valid have disappeared and the later attachment of another tzitzis to complete the four would render the earlier tzitzis “already made,” that is incorrect. It was taught that all agree one may not bring even a piece measuring one cubit by one cubit with techeiles already attached and sew it on. This implies that all the other corners do not become invalid because of “you shall make,” and not something already made, even though part of the garment was torn away. If so, what difference is there whether part of it tore or the garment was divided into two? Everything remains valid.

This makes clear that just as the remaining tzitzis do not become invalid when part of the garment is removed, even if another piece of fabric is later placed beneath the missing section, so too when a tallis is divided into two, one may complete either half into a full tallis. He may attach two new tzitzis to the newly formed corners. If he wishes, he may also join another piece of fabric to that half and attach new tzitzis to the new corners. The original tzitzis do not become invalid.

I saw that my father-in-law, Bach, wrote concerning this responsum that the validation applies only when one wraps himself in each piece independently. If he joins another piece to either half, even if the added piece has no tzitzis, the original tzitzis become invalid because of “already made.” Once he begins sewing the additional piece onto one corner, that corner becomes nullified and only three corners remain. When he completes the sewing, the garment becomes four-cornered again, and the original tzitzis are then “already made.” These are his words.

In my humble opinion, Rashba explicitly writes that the original tzitzis have no invalidity at all. His proof clearly addresses this point: one may not bring a piece measuring one cubit by one cubit with techeiles already attached, which implies that if no techeiles is attached, he may sew it onto the garment and afterward attach tzitzis, without any concern of “already made.”

I found an even stronger statement in Darkei Moshe, siman 10: “A tallis that has four tzitzis and was divided into two is permitted, and there is no concern of ‘you shall make,’ and not something already made,” citing responsa Rashba, siman 217. This is the same responsum Rashba cited by Beit Yosef. I am greatly surprised by his understanding of Rashba. Rashba intended only that the original tzitzis should not become invalid, but that they become valid once new tzitzis are later attached. The tzitzis that were on the piece removed from the garment became nullified from the garment. The same applies to a tallis divided into two: the tzitzis on each half became nullified relative to the other half. How, then, can sewing the halves together restore those tzitzis? This is certainly a case of “you shall make,” and not something already made, even more than the baraisa's case of a piece measuring one cubit by one cubit brought from elsewhere with techeiles already attached.

Rather, when the tallis was divided into two, he must remove the tzitzis from one of the pieces. After sewing the pieces together, the piece without tzitzis is treated as an addition to the first piece, which already contains a section large enough for wrapping. He then attaches new tzitzis to the second piece, which is now an addition to the first. This appears correct.
