<small>Shulchan Aruch, seif 2.</small> “And this corner was not there.” Know that there are two categories within “you shall make,” and not something already made. The first, which is the primary meaning of the verse, is that the tzitzis must come into being upon the garment through an act. One may not take already completed tzitzis and simply hang them upon the garment, because that is something already made.

The second category is what the Sages meant by “and not something made through an invalid state.” Even though he attached them to the garment through his own act, if they were invalid at that time, the act is treated as nonexistent. When they later become valid, they are something already made. This second category applies specifically where their initial making was invalid. If they were initially made validly, later became invalid, and then returned to validity, they remain valid, unless the invalidity affected the tzitzis themselves. In that case the original act is nullified, and it is as though they were never made validly. This is explained in Magen Avraham, siman 23, and in our words above in siman 12, letter 4. The distinctions do not apply to the first category, because no act was performed upon them at all.

I shall now explain the general laws of this siman according to Taz. Seif 2 is based upon the statement in the chapter HaTecheiles that all agree one may not bring a piece measuring one cubit by one cubit from elsewhere with techeiles attached and hang it upon the garment. Rosh gives the reason, cited by Taz in seif katan 4, from the verse “upon the corners,” as the Mechaber writes. Taz maintains that this invalidity is not based upon the second category, namely that the corner lacked the minimum garment size and its tzitzis became invalid. That cannot be the reason, because they were initially made validly and the invalidity did not affect the tzitzis themselves. Rather, when the corner is attached to another garment it is invalid because of Rosh's reason: relative to the second garment, the tzitzis are something already made.

Therefore Taz rules in seif katan 4 that even if the corner was entirely severed from this tallis outside three fingerbreadths, it may not be sewn back, unlike Beit Yosef, because of “you shall make,” and not something already made. Rosh's additional reason is needed because this is the first category. Although the tzitzis were attached to the original garment through an act, they came to this garment without an act. Once this is so, even if the piece measuring one cubit by one cubit contained enough material for wrapping, or many cubits, so that the tzitzis never became invalid, they would still be invalid when attached to the other garment.

This is not the second category, because they were initially made validly. It is the first category, according to Rosh. Taz maintains that even though the tzitzis were originally made upon this very garment, once the corner was completely severed it is as though it came from another garment. This is what Taz writes in seif katan 3, where he invalidates even when the tallis was divided into two and each half retained the minimum garment size. The tzitzis on each half are treated, relative to the other half, as though they had been upon a different garment at the time of their making.

According to Taz, Rosh's phrase “at the time of making” does not mean only the moment when the tzitzis were first attached. It means that while the tzitzis remain attached, they must continuously remain upon this garment. Once the piece is completely severed, it is treated as another garment, and sewing it back creates the first category of something already made. The baraisa's phrase “from elsewhere” is therefore not exact. Even a piece from this garment has the same law once it was completely severed.

The practical conclusion according to Taz is that whenever a piece was entirely severed, even if the piece itself contains the minimum garment size, sewing it back creates something already made, because of Rosh's reason and the first category. Some portion must remain attached. The required attached portion is specifically three fingerbreadths, as explained by Taz in seif katan 4.

According to this, seif 2 is entirely superfluous as practical law, because even if the corner remains attached to this very garment by less than three fingerbreadths, it may not be sewn. The baraisa says that all agree only to teach that even according to the opinion that a remaining connection of less than three fingerbreadths suffices, if the corner came from another garment, or was entirely severed from this corner, it may not be sewn. Taz explains in seif katan 4 that the baraisa is needed only according to the view that three fingerbreadths are unnecessary. According to the practical ruling that three fingerbreadths are required, seif 2 adds nothing. Beit Yosef disputes Taz throughout and follows the approach of Magen Avraham, which I shall explain in my comments to Magen Avraham, seif katan 3. Consider this carefully.

<small>Magen Avraham, seif katan 3.</small> “This implies that the principal point.” Magen Avraham follows Beit Yosef and disagrees with Taz in two respects.

First, Rosh's reason alone does not invalidate the tzitzis, and there is no first category of something already made here, because the tzitzis were attached through an act. Even if he takes the corner and attaches it to another garment, that is not inherently problematic. The second garment is merely an addition to the original corner. Surely it is permitted to sew a patch onto a tallis. Although no act was performed with the tzitzis relative to that patch, the patch is treated as an addition to the tallis. The same applies here. According to Taz, it is not treated as an addition because the piece itself bears tzitzis, as he writes at the end of seif katan 3.

According to Magen Avraham, if the corner itself contains the minimum garment size, it may be attached even to another tallis, because its tzitzis were never invalid. The baraisa speaks of a corner measuring one cubit by one cubit, which lacks the minimum garment size. Its tzitzis therefore became invalid, and sewing it onto another garment creates the second category of something already made. Why, then, did Rosh need his reason? Without it, one could ask why a tear outside three fingerbreadths may be sewn, since the tzitzis were initially made validly and the invalidity did not affect them. Rosh answers that where their original making was upon another garment, their present placement on this garment is considered an initial making in an invalid state. It is therefore invalid under the second category. If, however, the corner contains the minimum garment size, it remains valid even when taken from another garment, because it was never invalid. This is Magen Avraham's first disagreement with Taz.

Second, if the corner was entirely severed from this very garment outside three fingerbreadths, one may sew it back. Although the tzitzis became invalid, they were initially made validly. Magen Avraham does not accept Taz's reasoning that once the piece was severed it is treated as though it came from another garment.

One might ask why the Mechaber writes in seif 3 that if each half contains enough material for wrapping, one may rejoin the pieces. According to Magen Avraham's explanation, this is no novelty at all. There is no problem of something already made because the tzitzis never became invalid, and Rosh's reason alone does not invalidate. Moreover, since this piece came from the same garment, Rosh's reason does not apply at all. Even if it lacked the minimum garment size it should be permitted, just like a tear outside three fingerbreadths that may be sewn even after complete severance.

It appears that the minimum garment size is itself the novelty. One might have thought that because the piece has the status of an independent garment, its tzitzis are treated as though they came from another garment, and Rosh's reason would apply, as Taz maintains. This would make it worse than a strip torn outside three fingerbreadths, which retains only the status of a corner, not of an independent garment. The Mechaber therefore teaches that it is nevertheless valid. Rosh's reason alone does not invalidate. It serves only to create a case of something already made when combined with actual invalidity. Here, because the piece retained the minimum garment size, there was no invalidity.

This is why Magen Avraham concludes, “and the same appears from seif 3.” At first glance the statement is unclear. He brings proof for his explanation that Rosh's reason alone does not invalidate, but only when combined with something already made. If Rosh's reason alone sufficed, then in seif 3, where each half has the minimum garment size, the tzitzis would be as though they came from another garment, and Rosh's reason would apply. This differs from the case of a tear outside three fingerbreadths, where the torn strip never acquired the status of an independent garment. Since seif 3 nevertheless validates it, Rosh's reason alone cannot invalidate. It invalidates only when combined with something already made.

The practical result of Magen Avraham's approach is as follows. A piece containing the minimum garment size may be attached even if it came from another garment, because there is no invalidity of something already made. A piece from this same garment, even if smaller than the minimum garment size, may be reattached so long as it is at least three fingerbreadths wide. In seif 3, the ruling concerning a tallis divided into two is not exact, because even pieces from two different talleisim may be joined. Consider this carefully.

<small>Seif katan 4.</small> “When he begins to sew.” Meaning, when he has sewn less than three fingerbreadths, the pieces are not yet considered joined, as explained in seif 4. Nevertheless, the corner at the place of sewing is nullified and the garment has only three corners. Although the tzitzis were initially made validly and the invalidity does not affect the tzitzis themselves, here, because the piece contains enough material for wrapping, it is treated as another garment. The second category of something already made therefore applies together with Rosh's reason, as explained in the preceding letter.

Magen Avraham refutes this from the law that a tear outside three fingerbreadths may be sewn. Although we explained that a piece containing the minimum garment size is worse than a tear outside three fingerbreadths, the proof remains because the statement “outside three fingerbreadths” implies any distance beyond three fingerbreadths, whether small or large, even enough to contain the minimum garment size. It is nevertheless valid. The same should apply to a tallis divided into two, which may be rejoined.

He further refutes Bach from siman 10, seif 3. There, although the garment was sewn, its corners are not nullified because the sewing will certainly later be undone, and we follow its eventual state even now. All the more so here: when he begins sewing, he does so with the intention of sewing the entire garment. It is therefore considered as though the sewing were already complete, because we follow the ultimate result. Consider this carefully.
