**** ENGLISH ****
<b>(Se'if Katan 1) We begin early.</b> Tur writes, etc. This is Beis Yosef's language. Know that Rabbi Yehudah and the Rabbis disagree about the time for Minchah. The Rabbis hold that its time extends until evening, meaning until tzeis hakochavim, whereas Rabbi Yehudah holds that its time extends until plag haminchah, one and one-quarter hours before tzeis hakochavim. The time for Maariv consequently begins when the time for Minchah ends. According to the Rabbis, Maariv begins at tzeis hakochavim; according to Rabbi Yehudah, it begins one and one-quarter hours earlier. Berachos 27a asks which view is the halachah. It argues that since Rav prayed the Shabbos prayer on Erev Shabbos - that is, he prayed the Friday-night Maariv from plag haminchah onward - we may infer that the halachah follows Rabbi Yehudah. It responds: On the contrary, since Rav Huna and the Rabbis did not pray until evening, we may infer that they follow the Rabbis. It concludes that since the halachah was stated neither in accordance with one master nor the other, one who acts in accordance with either has acted properly. Shulchan Aruch therefore writes here that 'we begin early,' etc., earlier than on weekdays. This means that even those who follow the Rabbis during the week and pray Maariv specifically at tzeis hakochavim should pray early on Erev Shabbos because of the mitzvah of adding to Shabbos. Beis Yosef then cites Tur in the name of Ritz Gias, who distinguishes based upon the availability of wine, etc. That is, even if by praying during the day from plag haminchah onward he would have wine for Kiddush or Havdalah, while waiting to pray until tzeis hakochavim would leave him without wine - for example, he must travel before night to a place where wine is unavailable, as discussed below in siman 293 - he nevertheless should not pray until night.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>It appears to me to provide another sound reason - meaning that besides the mitzvah of adding to Shabbos described above, there is another reason.</b> For, etc.: Maariv corresponds to the limbs and fats of the afternoon Tamid, since the prayers were instituted to correspond to the daily offerings. Shabbos Maariv therefore corresponds to the limbs and fats of Friday's afternoon Tamid. It is consequently improper to pray Maariv at night, when it would no longer have been permissible to burn Friday's limbs and fats. Tosefes Shabbos challenges this: If it is improper to pray at a time when the offering replaced by the prayer could not be brought, what is gained by praying early while it is still day? Once the congregation has said Barchu, it has accepted Shabbos, as stated above at the end of siman 261. How may one then pray Maariv, which corresponds to the limbs and fats? In my humble opinion, speech itself involves no Shabbos desecration. The reason not to pray Maariv on Friday night is only a symbolic remembrance that the limbs and fats were not burned then. A remembrance concerning the essential period of Shabbos suffices, even if there is no such remembrance during the added period. Moreover, in the period added to Shabbos it would have been permissible to burn the limbs and fats. The essential addition, which is required by Torah law, has no prescribed measure, and slightly more than the smallest amount suffices. Any further period voluntarily added is prohibited only rabbinically, and in the Temple it would have been permitted in order to perform the mitzvah of burning the limbs and fats at its proper time. I wrote similarly above in siman 253, Magen Avraham 26. I have now found support in Talmidei Rabbeinu Yonah, chapter Tefillas HaShachar, concerning Rav's praying the Shabbos prayer on Erev Shabbos: 'Rabbi Yitzchak of Orleans would say that although the slightest amount suffices for adding to Shabbos, if one adds much more, it is nevertheless included in the addition to Shabbos and he is bound by it. Although one could say that this is only rabbinic, he may nevertheless perform no labor, just as during the slightest addition,' etc. Thus, Rabbi Yitzchak of Orleans is not certain about the matter and allows the possibility that the period one adds beyond the minimum is forbidden only rabbinically. One may similarly say that the poskim who require praying Maariv early on Erev Shabbos for Magen Avraham's reason - that it corresponds to the limbs and fats - also hold that the voluntarily added period is only rabbinic. The matter is therefore resolved.

<b>He was imprecise, for if we calculate, etc. - two fifths of an hour.</b> Terumas HaDeshen holds that a mil is one third of an hour less one thirtieth of an hour; this is the correct text. Rambam, however, writes in his Commentary on the Mishnah that a mil is two fifths of an hour. Maadanei Melech, Berachos 1, explains their positions. Terumas HaDeshen calculates the day from alos hashachar until tzeis hakochavim, and therefore a mil is one third of an hour less one thirtieth. The chapter Mi SheHayah Tamei states that on an equinoctial day, whose daylight is twelve hours, an average person walks ten parsaos. A parsah is four mil, so he walks forty mil in a day. If a mil were a full third of an hour, three thirds making an hour, forty mil would total thirteen and one-third hours. Subtract one thirtieth of an hour from each mil. The forty thirtieths removed total one and one-third hours: thirty thirtieths make an hour and the remaining ten make one third. In equal sixty-minute hours, one thirtieth is two minutes; thirty times two is sixty minutes and ten times two is twenty minutes, one third of an hour. Rambam, however, agrees with Levush that the day is calculated from sunrise until sunset and with the opinion that the walk from alos hashachar to sunrise is five mil and from sunset to tzeis hakochavim another five. Since walking ten parsaos means from alos hashachar to tzeis hakochavim, only thirty mil remain within the day from sunrise until sunset; five mil are walked before the day begins and five after it ends. Dividing the twelve-hour day into thirty parts yields two fifths of an hour per mil. Thirty mil are sixty fifths of an hour, or twelve hours, since twelve times five is sixty. Yet I am perplexed and do not know what practical difference exists between Terumas HaDeshen and Rambam beyond terminology, since hours are always seasonal hours; Rambam's day is no shorter or longer than Terumas HaDeshen's. In any event, if a mil is two fifths of an hour and, according to Olas Shabbos, sunset to tzeis hakochavim is four mil, the interval is eight fifths of an hour, the measure Magen Avraham states. Using equal hours for illustration: one fifth of an hour is twelve minutes, so two fifths are twenty-four minutes. Four mil are eight fifths. Five fifths form one hour, leaving three fifths, or thirty-six minutes - one-half hour plus six minutes. Those six minutes are one tenth of a sixty-minute hour. Thus the four mil equal one and one-half hours plus one tenth of an hour. Add the one and one-quarter hours from plag haminchah to sunset, and the total is two and three-quarter hours plus one tenth. The calculation is nevertheless imprecise. If a mil is two fifths of an hour, one must follow the opinion that alos hashachar to sunrise is five mil and sunset to tzeis hakochavim is five mil, as explained. If each interval were four mil, a mil would not be two fifths but three eighths of an hour - one quarter plus half a quarter. Subtracting four mil before sunrise and four after sunset, a total of eight, from the forty mil leaves thirty-two mil in the day. At three eighths of an hour per mil, thirty-two mil equal twelve hours: thirty-two quarters equal eight hours, and thirty-two half-quarters equal another four, totaling twelve. Since Magen Avraham assigns two fifths of an hour per mil, he necessarily follows the view that there are five mil from alos hashachar to sunrise and likewise from sunset to tzeis hakochavim. Yet he then writes, 'It therefore totals two and three-quarter hours plus one tenth.' That result assumes four mil from sunset to tzeis hakochavim. Chok Yaakov also raises this point. Furthermore, how can Rabbi Yehudah - whose position we are following - be assigned a mil of two fifths of an hour, which depends on there being five mil from alos hashachar to sunrise and from sunset to tzeis hakochavim? Rabbi Yehudah states explicitly in Pesachim 93b that each interval is four mil; see there. Rather, since the only poskim who assign a duration to a mil are Terumas HaDeshen, who gives one third of an hour less one thirtieth, and Rambam, who gives two fifths, while no posek explicitly gives three eighths of an hour, Magen Avraham did not wish to introduce a new measure absent from the poskim. The true calculation is nevertheless that if each twilight interval is four mil, a mil must be three eighths of an hour. Since Olas Shabbos's words are refuted either way, Magen Avraham uses the two-fifths measure explicitly found in Rambam. He nevertheless records a total of two and three-quarter hours plus one tenth according to Olas Shabbos's own assumption and Rabbi Yehudah's explicit position in Mi SheHayah Tamei that the interval is four mil. Since Olas Shabbos's words are rejected in any event, this is why Magen Avraham presents the measure in that manner.
