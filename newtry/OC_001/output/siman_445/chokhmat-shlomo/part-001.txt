<b>Se'if 1.</b> How is chametz destroyed? He burns it or crumbles it, etc. Note: see Magen Avraham s.k. 1, who brought the language of the Rambam at the end of Hilchos Pesulei HaMukdashin, that all items that are buried should not be burned, for although you are stringent by burning them, you are lenient with their ashes, etc. This is difficult to me from Pesachim folio 27: why did the Rabbis say to Rabbi Yehuda, any inference whose beginning is stringent and whose end is lenient is not an inference, for if he did not find wood to burn it, etc.? Without this they should have said more strongly: granted, you are stringent by burning them, but you are lenient with their ashes, and the end is therefore lenient. Perhaps they said this to Rabbi Yehuda according to his own view, for he holds at the end of Temurah that if one wants to be stringent and burn it, he may do so, which proves that he holds it is not considered a leniency that one may come to benefit from its ashes; or he holds that even the ashes of items that are burned are forbidden. Therefore, according to him, they could not say that for this reason its end is lenient. One can say further that with this Rashi's difficulty is resolved, namely that at the time of its destruction, its destruction may be by any means. According to the above, one can say as follows: certainly at the time of its destruction, granted that its destruction may be by any means, nevertheless since according to Rabbi Yehuda it is among the items that are burned, its ashes are permitted, and that is a leniency; whereas according to the Rabbis it is among the items that are buried and its ashes are forbidden. If so, it is a leniency. Only there was room to say that Rabbi Yehuda's intent is before its time, for then even if it were among the items that are buried, their ashes are permitted, since it was charred before its time, etc. If so, burning is properly stringent, for even among buried items their ashes are then permitted. Therefore they said: if you judge regarding before its destruction, then there it is a leniency if he did not find wood to burn it, for he will sit idle. In any event, its end is lenient; examine this, and it works well. I was later told that in Sefer Or Chadash he noticed my above difficulty and answered as I wrote above, that Rabbi Yehuda follows his own view. What appears to my humble opinion is as I wrote, with the help of Hashem. Another difficulty occurs to me regarding what the Talmud says there: Rabbi Yehuda returned and derived another inference, nosar has bal tosiru, etc. This is difficult: how does he compare chametz to nosar? Granted, nosar too is a negative commandment rectified by a positive commandment, nevertheless with nosar the positive commandment applies only if he transgressed the negative commandment; but to leave it initially in order to fulfill the positive commandment is forbidden. With chametz, however, Tosafos wrote in Pesachim folio 29 that one who keeps chametz in order to destroy it does not transgress, and it is proven there that initially it is permitted to pass over the negative commandment in order to fulfill the positive commandment. If so, chametz is more lenient than nosar; what proof is there from one to the other? This requires examination. A learned person asked me: what proof is there from kodshim to chullin? Kodshim are more severe. At first glance he said well. But it appears that it works well, for we derive leniency from kodshim, as the Taz wrote in Yoreh Deah siman 3. If so, Rabbi Yehuda says in any event: if burning is stringent, then there is an a fortiori inference from kodshim, and we can properly derive chullin from kodshim by an a fortiori inference. If they will say that its end is lenient, then in any event, if burning is a leniency, we again derive it by a common feature, for we derive a leniency in chullin from kodshim, as above in the name of the Taz. Examine this well, for it is correct. There, in the same se'if 1. Note: see Magen Avraham s.k. 2, who wrote that one must say chametz is not called a positive commandment of the public, etc. See Hilchos Megillah, siman 696 se'if 7, where it is explained that meat and wine on Purim are called a positive commandment of the public; this is the opposite of the Magen Avraham's words here. See my chiddushim to Pesachim folio 92, in my work on Orach Chayim from the year 5612, folio 4a. What seemed to me to answer this is to pay attention to the language of the Shulchan Aruch in Hilchos Megillah, where he wrote that the positive commandment of rabbinic aninus does not come and override the positive commandment of words of Kabbalah, which is a positive commandment of the public. Why did he not write the reverse, that the positive commandment of the public, which is from words of Kabbalah, comes and overrides the rabbinic positive commandment of the individual, as we generally say that a positive commandment overrides a negative commandment, or that a severe positive commandment overrides a lighter positive commandment? Rather, the matter is as follows. Where the mitzvah requires intent for the sake of the mitzvah, according to the halachic decisors who hold that mitzvos require intent, or even according to those who hold that intent is not required, that is only because an ordinary act is considered with intent; but if he intends for an optional matter, he certainly has no fulfillment of the mitzvah. In such a case, if he intends for the sake of the mitzvah, the positive commandment overrides the negative commandment or the lighter positive commandment, and the overriding is called by the name of the positive commandment that he comes to fulfill. But where the act is such that even if he does not intend for the sake of the mitzvah, but intends for an optional matter, it also helps and is considered a mitzvah, and you come only to forbid it because of another positive commandment or negative commandment that prevents him from doing it, then the overriding is considered to be on account of the positive commandment that prevents the act, which overrides the other positive commandment. Therefore it works well. On the contrary, from there there is a proof for the Magen Avraham's words: it is not called an actual positive commandment of the public, since the individual does not discharge the public's obligation. Therefore, for the severe positive commandment to override the lighter positive commandment, it must be extremely severe; and where there is only a slight severity, it has no power to override the other positive commandment. But where it is the reverse, that the positive commandment that prevents the act is called the one that overrides the other positive commandment that obligates the act, then whenever the positive commandment of the act is somewhat severe in any degree, the second positive commandment has no power to override the slightly more severe one. Set this one against that one, and it remains optional. Granted, this one has the advantage of being passive inaction, but the other has another advantage, that it has some slight stringency; therefore neither overrides the other, and it remains optional. And where it is optional, it is permitted to do this act as an optional act. But where, as an optional act, he does not fulfill his obligation, and he must do it for the sake of the mitzvah, there one cannot say: set this against that and it becomes optional. Rather, one must say that the positive commandment that obligates the act overrides the positive commandment of passive inaction; for this, it must be very severe, and without that it does not override. Therefore, regarding Purim, since it is not an actual mitzvah of the public, for the individual does not discharge the public's obligation, one cannot say that this positive commandment overrides the positive commandment of aninus, although it is somewhat a mitzvah of the public and words of Kabbalah are like Torah words. Rather, since on Purim one does not require doing the act for the sake of the mitzvah, and if he eats and drinks as an optional act he has also fulfilled it, it is enough if we say that rabbinic aninus has no power to override this positive commandment. Granted, aninus has the advantage of passive inaction, but Purim has the advantage that it is somewhat public and, together with the fact that words of Kabbalah are like Torah words, therefore neither overrides the other, and it remains optional. Therefore the Shulchan Aruch was precise in its language, that he is permitted in meat and wine, meaning that it is optional; to forbid it is impossible, for then the positive commandment of aninus would override the positive commandment of Purim, and that is impossible. But with chametz, where he must intend for the mitzvah of destroying the chametz, as an optional act it is certainly forbidden to destroy it on Shabbos; rather, we would say that the positive commandment of chametz overrides, in the reverse direction, the positive commandment of Shabbos. In this, as long as it is not an actual positive commandment of the public, it is not considered a positive commandment of the public; and since the positive commandment of Shabbos also has the advantage that it is passive inaction, they remain equal, and it is forbidden to destroy it for the sake of the mitzvah, for as an optional act this does not apply. This works well with the help of Hashem; examine it. In this way one may answer his difficulty in Pesachim folio 92, which is in my above-mentioned work, why the Talmud does not say that for this reason an onen eats the Pesach offering, because the positive commandment of the public in Pesach comes and overrides the positive commandment of aninus. According to the above it works well, for with Pesach, where it must be specifically for the sake of the mitzvah, the overriding is called by the name of the positive commandment of Pesach; and since it is not actually public, because the individual does not discharge the public's obligation, it is not strong enough to override the positive commandment of aninus, etc., and it works well. However, the sugya there is still not settled, for we say in that tractate, folio 59, that the positive commandment of Pesach, which has kares, should come and override the positive commandment of completion, which does not have kares. If so, there too on folio 92 it should have said the same. Perhaps there, regarding offering, where there is actual kares, it is considered severe, for if he does not bring the offering he is liable to kares. But here, where it speaks of eating, since it follows the view that eating does not prevent fulfillment, even if he does not eat he will not be liable to kares; therefore it is not considered severe. Nevertheless, regarding the rule that the Sages did not uphold their words, it properly says in a place of kares, meaning that since in any event the Pesach offering has a severity that includes an aspect of kares in its performance, therefore even regarding eating they did not uphold their words. But regarding aninus, which has no aspect of kares at all, they did not uphold their words at all. This works well with the help of Hashem; examine it well.

<b>There, se'if 2.</b> And if he gave it to a non-Jew before the sixth hour, he need not destroy it, etc. Note: I saw in Chok Yaakov and Baer Heitev that they brought in the name of Responsa Bach that it is forbidden to give it to his worker or to his non-Jewish servants whose sustenance is upon him if it is a large amount of chametz, because it is like paying his debt with prohibitions of benefit; see there. His words are very astonishing. Paying one's debt with prohibitions of benefit means where at the time of payment it is forbidden for benefit. But if at that time of payment it is permitted, what concern is there that afterward it will become forbidden for benefit? Would a person not be allowed to pay a debt to a non-Jew with chametz, even a debt whose time has not yet arrived before Pesach? Is that forbidden? So too here: since the non-Jew receives it and exempts him from his sustenance before its prohibition, and if he sold it to the non-Jew and the non-Jew became obligated to him, it would be permitted, here too it is as if he sold it to the non-Jew, and the non-Jew became obligated to him for money, and with that debt-money he exempted him from his sustenance. It is like giving him money to buy his sustenance for him; even though he buys chametz for him, it is permitted, as written in siman 450. Furthermore, there are those who permit in siman 450 saying to a shopkeeper: give chametz to my workers and I will pay, and it is not considered paying his debt with prohibitions of benefit, as the Magen Avraham wrote there, even though his sustenance is upon him. It is not considered paying his debt with prohibitions of benefit. Even those who are stringent would agree here, for there he gives on Pesach itself, but where he gives before Pesach, it is certainly permitted. Therefore it appears that whenever the non-Jew acquired it in a manner that is generally effective, it is effective here too. One cannot say that it remains under a Jew's responsibility, for if it is lost he will need to give him other sustenance; for if so, even with a small amount of chametz, if it remains for him into Pesach, the same would be true. Furthermore, it is like accepting responsibility for a non-Jew's chametz in the non-Jew's domain, which is permitted. Furthermore, if so, why is it permitted with a worker to give him a dinar to buy chametz with it, and according to those who permit, even to tell a shopkeeper to give him chametz is permitted? When the chametz comes into the hands of the non-Jewish worker, it would be under the Jew's responsibility, for if it is lost he will need to pay him another. Perforce, this is like accepting responsibility for a non-Jew's chametz in the non-Jew's domain, which is permitted. Furthermore, and this is the main point, as I wrote in my chiddushim long ago: responsibility for chametz is forbidden, and likewise wanting its preservation, only where the primary responsibility arises because of the chametz, such as one who accepts the chametz of others under responsibility, so that now the obligation of responsibility begins; and likewise wanting its preservation, where one buys chametz for a non-Jew or hires himself as a worker for chametz or for yayin nesech. But where the obligation is not because of the chametz, but from another source, only that through the chametz he is exempted from his debt, and if the chametz will not be there, another prior obligation that he already owed from another source and not because of the chametz will take effect, this is not considered responsibility and not considered wanting its preservation. The body of his benefit or responsibility does not begin from the chametz, but from elsewhere, and the chametz is only removal of damage, as I wrote in my chiddushim long ago with this proof; see there. If so, here too it is like this, and it is only like driving away a lion, which is not benefit but only removal of damage, as Tosafos wrote in Bava Kama regarding an animal that fell into a garden. Although paying his debt is permitted only without his knowledge, but with his knowledge it is forbidden, that is only where he gives it to him by hand and it is also after it has already become forbidden. But if he does not give it to him by hand, but only says to a shopkeeper to give it to him, or where he gives it to him by hand but it is still the time of permission, then at the time of giving it is permitted, and afterward, although it becomes forbidden for benefit, at that time he is not giving it to him; he benefits automatically, and it is no worse than paying his debt without his knowledge, which is only driving away a lion. Therefore the words of the Bach are astonishing. My heart therefore tells me that the Bach speaks only where the non-Jew eats in the Jew's house, and these are the words of the Agudah that the Magen Avraham brought in siman 450, that he should not eat in his house because of suspicion. What is the suspicion? That they will say he is paying his debt with prohibitions of benefit. This too is the intent of the Bach: there is a concern that people will say it is paying his debt with prohibitions of benefit, for they will think he gave it to him on Pesach. But if it is not in the Jew's house, it is certainly permitted; only in the Jew's house is it forbidden, because people will say it is like paying his debt with prohibitions of benefit. Why did he not state the reason that people will say the Jew transgressed bal yera'eh? One can say that since according to Torah law mere nullification is enough, presumably he nullified it, for one does not abandon the permitted and eat the forbidden. Although it remains forbidden rabbinically, nevertheless it would then be permitted in private chambers, for with a rabbinic prohibition, it is permitted in private chambers where there is concern of maris ayin, as the Magen Avraham wrote in siman 301. Therefore the Bach stated the reason because of paying his debt with a prohibition of benefit, for that is a Torah prohibition and is forbidden even in private chambers. This appears correct to my humble opinion in the Bach's intent. Without this, his words have no explanation. In any case, as a matter of law it appears to be completely permitted; only in his house he should not eat because of suspicion, like the words of the Agudah above. This is clear to my humble opinion; examine it. Afterward Responsa Bach came into my possession, and it is not in siman 147 as the Chok Yaakov wrote, but in siman 143. In truth, it all follows the stringent view in siman 450; but according to the permissive authorities, certainly all the more so it is permitted here too. Therefore the words of the Chok Yaakov are astonishing, that he brought this without qualification. The substance of the Bach's words is also astonishing, for he brought proof to this, that even greater than this is written in the Agudah, that it is forbidden to give a worker a dinar to buy chametz; see there. In my opinion it is not comparable at all, for there it is during the time of prohibition, but in our case it is still during the time of permission. Can a person not pay before its time if the creditor agrees to accept it to exempt him from his debt? In order not to leave the matter smooth without proof, I will bring a proof from the Talmud in the first chapter of Pesachim, where Rab Gidal said in the name of Rav: one who betroths with Kurdani wheat from six hours and onward, we do not take the kiddushin into account. This is difficult: why did he not teach a greater novelty, that even if he betrothed before, during the time of permission, and stipulated that the kiddushin not take effect until the sixth hour, we do not take the kiddushin into account, since when the time of kiddushin arrives the chametz has already become forbidden, especially according to those who explain that he speaks after the sixth hour, when it is according to Torah law? Why did he not teach this? Perforce, in such a case she is betrothed, for Rav follows his own reasoning in Kiddushin folio 59, that even if the money was consumed, she is betrothed, because it was consumed in her domain. If so, here too with chametz, the chametz has already become hers, and what remains upon him is only a monetary debt; if so, he is not benefiting from a prohibition of benefit, but from the debt, which is permitted, and she is betrothed with the debt and not with the chametz. Although the chametz is then physically present in her hand, it is as though it were not present. If so, all the more so one who gives chametz to a worker during the time of permission is not considered to be benefiting from prohibitions of benefit. This is clear and correct with the help of Hashem. Therefore the Bach's words in this matter do not stand; examine it. There, in the same se'if: But if he burned it from the sixth hour and onward, since it is forbidden for benefit, he may not heat an oven or stove with it, etc. Note: an actual case came before me where a woman threw the challah into the fire on Shabbos HaGadol before Pesach and thought it had burned. Afterward they swept the coals from the oven and left them for Pesach, and they took the coals to cook pots with them, and found the challah among the coals. I ruled to permit the cooked dishes; only the pot that was adjacent to the challah, where it was likely that the challah touched that pot, I forbade. My reason was that since if he charred it before its time it is permitted for benefit after its time, then if we knew that the challah had been well charred before Pesach, there would be no concern at all. Only since we did not know, we are concerned perhaps it was not thoroughly charred, as the Rosh wrote regarding muryas. Nevertheless, that which we require that it be fit for a dog applies only when it is present as itself; but through a mixture, it is enough if it became unfit for human consumption, as is proven in siman 442. If so, here too, granted it was not well charred, in any event it was certainly unfit for human consumption. Since this and this cause it is only like through a mixture, and the reason this and this cause it is permitted with prohibitions of benefit is because of nullification, it is proven that it is a kind of mixture. If so, it is chametz through a mixture, and it is enough if it became unfit for a dog's consumption. What the Rosh was concerned about, perhaps it was not thoroughly charred, follows his earlier words, where he wrote that Rabbi Shimon permits only where it became mixed by itself, but not to mix it and keep it intentionally. Therefore, regarding that, he properly wrote that since the Rosh holds that intentionally mixing is as though it were present as itself, and regarding something present as itself we require that it become unfit for a dog's consumption, he is concerned perhaps it was not thoroughly charred. But where it became mixed by itself, since it is through a mixture and it is enough that it became unfit for a dog's consumption, this is considered certain. Furthermore, the Tur permits muryas even for benefit; if so, it is proven that he holds leniently regarding benefit, and we are not concerned that perhaps it was not thoroughly charred. Especially since there are views that even with chametz, this and this cause it is permitted, therefore in any event one may rely on this in such a case, for it is a double doubt: perhaps this and this cause it is permitted, and perhaps it was thoroughly charred. But where it is likely that it touched the actual body of the challah, there is concern for flavor that imparted flavor, and this does not fall under the law of this and this cause it; there one should properly be concerned that perhaps it was not thoroughly charred. Some of the above reasons do not apply there, and the Magen Avraham also forbids in such a case even for benefit. Therefore in that matter my heart did not allow me to be lenient, and I forbade that cooked dish; the rest I permitted. This is correct with the help of Hashem; examine it. Since in this siman I spoke at length, with the help of Hashem, in my work Sefer HaChaim regarding the law of this and this cause it, and regarding where something is not nullified even in a thousand and even this and this cause it is forbidden, I will copy here what arose in my thought with the help of Hashem when I studied the Rambam in Hilchos Temurah. This is the language of the Rambam in chapter 3 of Hilchos Temurah, law 5: one who consecrates an animal with a passing blemish, etc., and it is redeemed, and he substituted another animal for it after it was redeemed, this is a temurah but is not offered, etc. I saw in Lechem Mishneh that he wondered: since we say in chapter 2 of Bechoros that the reason their offspring are forbidden is specifically where it conceived before its redemption and was born after its redemption, etc.; but if it conceived after its redemption, they are chullin. If so, here too regarding temurah it should be proper to say this. After all, the offspring of kodshim is more severe than temurah, for the offspring of partners is sanctified, whereas they do not make a temurah; see there. In my humble opinion there is no difficulty at all, since we say in Pesachim that although this and this cause it is permitted, nevertheless with hekdesh it is forbidden, because it is not nullified even in a thousand. If so, here too, the fact that the offspring of a korban is permitted when it conceived after its redemption is because of this and this cause it. Granted, where it conceived before its redemption, it is not considered this and this cause it, since it has a permitted outcome through redemption and therefore is not considered this and this cause it. But where it conceived after redemption, since it no longer has a permitted outcome, it is this and this cause it; therefore it does not have the status of the offspring of a korban and is permitted. But with temurah, since in any event it is the temurah of a korban and is not this and this cause it, even after its redemption it is forbidden. With a partners' korban, since they have redemption, it is not this and this cause it, and it is considered the offspring of a korban; therefore the offspring of a korban is more severe than temurah. But where it is this and this cause it, it is not considered the offspring of a korban at all, and in this temurah is more severe. This works well; examine it. While dealing with this, I was uncertain regarding these laws of the Rambam, where he wrote that it is a temurah: does one also receive lashes or not? This requires examination; examine it. See Magen Avraham here, who wrote that where a mashehu is forbidden, even this and this cause it is forbidden. As for the difficulty against him, how this differs from a davar hama'amid, where we hold that even in a thousand it is not nullified, and nevertheless this and this cause it is permitted, see what I wrote in my work on tractate Avodah Zarah, Avodas Avodah, folio 68b, to resolve this; examine it.
