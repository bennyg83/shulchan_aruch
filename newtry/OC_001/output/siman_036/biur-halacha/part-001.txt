<b>ולנקודה התחתונה תהיה לכתחילה וכו' -</b> וידע המעיין בקונטרס הזה דבכמה מקומות לא ביארתי בפנים וגם בשער הציון אם הדין הוא רק לכתחילה או דיעבד והיא אחת משלשה סיבות או דהוא פשוט דהוא לכתחילה או דיעבד או דיש בזה פרטים המתחלפים דיש בזה לפעמים לכתחילה ולפעמים דיעבד כגון מה שכתוב דצריך האות לעשות כה וכה כדי שיהיה יוכל להסמיך אות אצלו דפשוט דאם לא עשה כן ועי"ז נעשה הפסק בתיבה שנראה כשני תיבות דפסול ואם זה האות הוא בסוף תיבה כשר בדיעבד ועוד הרבה כיוצא בזה ולא רציתי להאריך ע"כ סתמתי הדבר או דהיה אצלי ג"כ ספק והנחתי הדבר כמות שהוא ע"כ אל ימהר האדם להקל בדבר או להחמיר ולהפסיד לאחרים אם לא שימצא ראיה ברורה:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And the lower point should initially, etc.</b> The reader of this booklet should know that in several places I did not clarify, either in the text or in Shaar HaTziyun, whether a ruling applies only initially or also after the fact. This is for one of three reasons.

Sometimes it is obvious that the ruling is only initial or, conversely, indispensable after the fact. Sometimes the matter has differing details, applying initially in one case and after the fact in another. For example, where a letter must be formed in a particular way so that another letter may be placed next to it, then if it was not formed that way and the resulting gap divides one word so that it appears like two words, the writing is invalid. If that letter stands at the end of a word, however, the writing may be valid after the fact. There are many similar cases, and I did not wish to lengthen the discussion.

At other times I myself remained uncertain and left the matter without decision. Therefore, one should not rush either to be lenient or to rule stringently and cause another person loss unless he finds clear proof.

<b>And if.</b> Know that this doubt arises only according to what we established as halachah in Biur Halachah, siman 32, seif 25, following Rabbi Akiva Eiger, that repair helps where a letter does not appear as two letters even when the separation is visibly recognizable. Peri Megadim holds that repair does not help in such a case in tefillin and mezuzos.

For example, where the yod of an alef does not touch the alef, even though that detached yod has the independent form of another letter, the remainder of the alef has no independent letter name. Repair may therefore help so that the detached yod is no longer read as a yod but as part of the alef. Peri Megadim himself mentions this reasoning, though according to him it helps only where the separation is not clearly recognizable.

We also established there that if the hump above a ches does not connect the two sides and the separation is clearly visible, repair does not help because it appears as two zayins. We do not say that the hump preserves the name ches.

Our present doubt is whether, when the hump itself is continuous but does not touch one of the zayins beneath it, the form of a ches is still lacking because it is not attached on that side, or whether the continuous hump retains its status and prevents the zayin beneath it from being read independently, just as with the yod of an alef. This requires study.

<b>It should not be bent too greatly.</b> What is considered too greatly bent? Baruch She'amar, in his first alphabet, and Agur imply that this means when the bend touches the base of the tes.

This requires study. Beit Yosef explains that it should not be bent too greatly because it would then resemble a peh entirely. Sefer HaTerumah and Hagahos Maimoniyos cite this language. They refer to the right half of the tes, for although the left side, a zayin, proves that it is not a peh, their view is that if part of a letter resembles another letter it is not complete writing. Sefer Yere'im interprets the baraisa in this manner regarding alefs made like ayins and similar forms. Maaseh Rokeach cites Maharanach, siman 1, likewise. Many authorities disagree, but this is not the place to elaborate.

Yet regarding a peh, Baruch She'amar and Agur themselves write, and the law below follows them, that if the inner point touches either side, the letter is invalid. If so, the less the tes is bent inward, the more closely it would seem to resemble a peh. It must therefore be that even without touching the base, a very great bend is forbidden, and certainly if it touches. In either case, at least part of it resembles a peh.

This also explains why Beit Yosef, Levush, and all the commentators on Shulchan Aruch did not define the phrase “bent too greatly.” Had they accepted Baruch She'amar's narrow definition, they would not have remained silent.

Regarding the after-the-fact law, Shulchan Aruch HaRav writes “it should not initially be bent too greatly,” implying leniency after the fact. LeDavid Emes writes: “One should not bend the tes too deeply inward lest it entirely resemble a peh, for if its point touches its body inside it is invalid.”

If Shulchan Aruch HaRav interprets “too greatly” as Baruch She'amar does, this would be a dispute: Shulchan Aruch HaRav validates and LeDavid Emes invalidates.

The principal after-the-fact ruling, whether according to our explanation or that of Baruch She'amar and Agur, remains difficult. Sefer Yere'im, Sefer HaTerumah, and Hagahos Maimoniyos write that in this way it entirely resembles a peh, and explicitly explain this as the baraisa's statement not to make tesin like pehin. The baraisa concludes regarding all such cases that the writing must be placed in genizah.

According to Rosh, however, who writes in Hilchos Sefer Torah in the name of Rabbeinu Tam that the right side of the tes should be bent so that if the letter is turned to the left it resembles a peh, the baraisa proves nothing concerning a slight or great bend. Its point is merely that although a tes resembles a peh from a certain angle, one should not actually make it a peh.

Rosh also copied almost all of Sefer HaTerumah's language but omitted this distinction concerning a great bend, indicating that he disagrees. Tur and Rabbeinu Yerucham likewise merely say that the tes should be bent, without mentioning this issue.

It follows that a greatly bent tes is disputed. Sefer HaTerumah, Yere'im, and Hagahos Maimoniyos invalidate it even after the fact, while Rosh, Tur, and Rabbeinu Yerucham permit it. Since this concerns a Torah law, one should be stringent.

According to our explanation, however, “greatly bent” includes even where it does not touch below, and it is very difficult to invalidate after the fact against Baruch She'amar and Agur, who imply that without contact it is valid.

Initially, it appears that one should repair it wherever possible. In a Sefer Torah, if it is found during reading, one should not remove another scroll because of this and may rely upon the lenient authorities. Afterward, however, he should erase enough of the tes to cancel its letter form and rewrite it, because otherwise the repair would be engraving rather than writing.

In tefillin and mezuzos, where writing out of order invalidates, one should rely upon the lenient authorities.

If the inward bend touched the base of the tes, then even in tefillin and mezuzos it is invalid, following LeDavid Emes. One should not be lenient against Sefer HaTerumah and Hagahos Maimoniyos. Perhaps Rosh also agrees that it is invalid because its form has changed.

Even if a child reads it as tes, the letter is not surrounded by parchment at the point of contact, as explained below concerning the shin. Because of that alone, scraping at the point of contact would ordinarily help. But according to Sefer HaTerumah and Hagahos Maimoniyos, this is included in making tesin like pehin, and in every case the letter's form must first be nullified. That cannot be done in tefillin and mezuzos because of writing out of order.

<b>Peri Megadim left it unresolved.</b> A contemporary scholar brought proof for those who validate from Shabbos 104b: if one intended to write one letter and two emerged, he is liable.

According to Rabbeinu Chananel, cited by Hagahos Maimoniyos and Beit Yosef under this letter, the case concerns a tes: he intended to write a tes but did not attach its leg on the left, and it became a nun and zayin. If a nun whose head was made like a vav is not considered a nun even after the fact, why would he be liable for writing two letters? Presumably the nun within the tes was formed as other tesin are formed, with the neck extending from its side rather than its center.

This proof may be rejected. According to the Gemara's conclusion, which says that the letters require crowns, the baraisa must refer to a case where he made the nun within the tes upright above and crowned it, unlike an ordinary tes, where the nun is unadorned and bent slightly inward. We may therefore also say that he extended the neck from the center of its head like an ordinary nun, not like the nun within an ordinary tes. That is why he is liable for the nun as well.

Regarding repair of a bent nun whose head was made like a vav, I wrote in the text that repair helps even in tefillin and mezuzos. There is no concern of engraving because scraping alone does not validate it, and no concern of writing out of order because its essential form remains. Everyone commonly recognizes such a form as a nun.

This follows from Peri Megadim's ruling regarding a final kaf made square. Even according to the authorities who invalidate it after the fact because they do not distinguish between final and bent kaf, they agree that adding ink to round it helps even in tefillin and mezuzos. It is still considered to retain the form of a kaf because people commonly write such kafin.

Our case is all the more lenient. The Gemara explicitly requires the bent kaf to be rounded, and according to those who apply that form even to final kaf, a square top would seem to erase the name kaf. Nevertheless, Peri Megadim says repair helps because the letter is commonly written that way.

Concerning a final nun with a vav-shaped head, at least there is no explicit source in the Gemara that repair should not help. All the more so, one should not be stringent concerning a bent nun whose head was made like a vav.

I wrote that one should scrape only a little, because if he scrapes too much the form will be lost and later repair will be writing out of order.

I also wrote that afterward he should add ink. If he first adds ink and makes the letter very broad, it may lose its essential form because people are not accustomed to such a shape, and later scraping from the left may not help.

Know further that Peri Megadim treats final and bent nun alike even after the fact, evidently because both carry the name nun. He likewise tends to invalidate a final kaf made square. According to his reasoning, the same concern may apply to an open mem whose bottom was made rounded, since both open and closed forms bear the name mem. A closed mem made that way is certainly invalid because it falls under the warning not to make memin like samechin. Perhaps the same applies to the open mem. This requires practical study.

Repair nevertheless helps even in tefillin and mezuzos, because its essential form remains even before repair.

<b>And its right leg, etc., like a bent kaf.</b> After the fact, even a slight inward bend appears sufficient. See Beit Yosef. Rashi on Shabbos 104a, s.v. “let him enter through this,” likewise implies this, as do Rabbeinu Yerucham and Agur, who write that the leg of the kof should be lengthened so that it not resemble a heh.
