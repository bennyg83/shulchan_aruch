<b>There.</b> “A ches made according to Rashi in this form is valid.” Rabbeinu Tam requires a ches shaped like two zayins with a hump above them. Rashi requires a simple roof, with an upright staff-like crown upon the left side.

Sefer Yere'im therefore writes that one should satisfy both views. The teaching in the Letters of Rabbi Akiva, that no crown is attached to the ches, means only that such a staff-like crown is not obligatory. It does not forbid adding it, provided the upright staff is made at the end of the roof on the left, so that it appears to be the head of the left leg and not a separate crown upon the roof.

Beit Yosef's phrase “as a crown upon its roof” may mean that if one draws the staff toward the right upon the roof of the ches, it appears as a separate crown upon the roof.

Magen Avraham's instruction not to lengthen the roof means that one should not make a broad roof between the two zayins, because it would then no longer resemble a sloping hump.

Magen Avraham earlier writes that this case must be one in which the hump remains in its proper place. The source states that if the letter was shaped like a dalet and zayin, one need not invalidate it, but may scrape the dalet until it becomes a zayin without violating the requirement of writing in order. This is because the hump already stands above the point where the zayin ends. Once the dalet is scraped into a zayin, no further writing is needed.

If the hump were above the end of the dalet, and after scraping one would have to extend the hump diagonally, the letter would become invalid the moment it was scraped and the later extension would be writing out of order. In Olas Tamid, siman 33, seif katan 44, the hump is illustrated at the end of the dalet, but that is a scribal error.

Magen Avraham further writes that if we know an expert wrote it, etc. This initially requires study. What difference does the writer's expertise make? Once it is invalid, repair should not help because it would be out of order.

Olas Tamid writes: “We learn from his words that if it was initially made validly and afterward became damaged, one should not invalidate it. This requires study as practical halachah.” Peri Megadim likewise leaves it unresolved.

It appears to me that the reason is as follows. If the tag was initially written separately, it immediately acquired the status of a small letter. Later drawing it down and connecting it to the main letter does not help, because this is like attaching another letter that was written invalidly to the intended letter. It does not revert to the status of a tag merely by being joined.

If, however, it was originally attached validly and separated only afterward, it already acquired the status of a tag. Even though it now requires repair by drawing it back to the letter, the status of a tag does not depart from it merely because it separated, and the repair helps.

Accordingly, where the taggin did not touch the letter from the outset, one should not repair them by merely drawing them to the letter. Rather, one should erase those taggin entirely and make new ones. There is then no concern of writing out of order, because this is no worse than disconnected yods in an alef where a child can read the letter.

If one already repaired them by drawing the taggin to the letter, Olas Tamid appears to regard the letter as invalid. It requires study whether erasing them and writing them anew would help, for perhaps the act of joining them invalidated the body of the letter by making it appear like two letters connected together. Once the body of the letter itself became invalid, erasure might not help.

Nevertheless, it appears that erasure does help. This is no worse than two actual letters attached to one another, which may be separated after writing and is not considered writing out of order so long as the form of each letter remains proper, as explained in seif 7.
