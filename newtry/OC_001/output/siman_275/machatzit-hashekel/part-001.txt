<b>(ס"ק ב) בעששית כו'</b> וב"ח אוסר משום לא פלוג. וטעם המתירים כתב בס' א"ר דוקא בגבוה י' קומות דל"ל הכירא דהא גם בחול כשצריך להשתמש במקום גבוה מניח הנר במקום גבוה בזה אמרי' לא פלוג משא"כ מפתח דאין דרכו בכך דבחול וא"ל הכירא לא אסרו משום לא פלוג ע"ש. ונלענ"ד ע"י קשר משונה שרי כמו ע"י מפתח לדעת המתירים דהא דברים שמשלחים ע"י גוי וצריכים חותם קשר משונה עדיף ממפתח כדאיתא בי"ד סי' קי"ח וסי' ק"ל ע"ש:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Paragraph 2: In a glass case, etc.</b> Bach forbids it under a uniform decree. Eliyah Rabbah explains the permissive view: when a lamp is ten stories high, no reminder exists, since even on weekdays one places a lamp high when needing to use that place; the uniform decree applies. A lock is different, since this is not the weekday manner and provides a reminder, so no uniform decree was made. It appears to me that an unusual knot likewise permits it according to the lenient view. Regarding items sent through a gentile that require a seal, an unusual knot is superior to a lock, as in Yoreh De'ah 118 and 130.

<b>For this entails no Torah prohibition.</b> Tur rules like Rabbi Shimon that one is exempt for labor not needed for its primary purpose. Extinguishing, unless done to char the wick or make charcoal, is such labor. Tevuos Shor writes that if a wax candle and oil lamp stand beside one another, reading by them is plainly forbidden. This differs from siman 264, where Magen Avraham permits use when a valid lamp stands beside an invalid one. There the other uses require no close examination, and a valid lamp initially suffices; the invalid lamp will not be tilted because the valid one suffices. Here the activity requires close examination, which is forbidden even by a valid lamp lest he tilt it. A wax candle beside it does not help: although a wax candle cannot be tilted, he may tilt the other lamp. This is very clear.

**** ENGLISH ****
See se'if 12. Raavan's ruling that repulsiveness eliminates concern that one may tilt depends upon the dispute in se'if 12 among Rashi, Rif, and Rosh. Under their text, a servant may inspect utensils by a naphtha lamp. Rashi explains that because it is repulsive, we do not fear he will tilt it, agreeing with Raavan. Although naphtha may be more repulsive than tallow, Rashi indicates generally that no decree applies when it is repulsive. Rif and Rosh give two reasons: its light is strong and it is repulsive. Beis Yosef says both are necessary: strong light removes concern of tilting, because no tilt is needed; repulsiveness removes concern he may take some fuel, as with olive oil. Since repulsiveness alone does not suffice for them, they evidently still fear tilting despite it, contrary to Raavan.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Paragraph 6: To recite piyyutim.</b> The same applies to zemiros, etc. Tosefes Shabbos asks: Magen Avraham 3 cites Bach that Maharshal noted the custom to inspect tzitzis and utensils and read the Haggadah by wax candle, which must rely on authorities permitting wax. Certainly a tallow candle should also be permitted, since it shares only the concern of trimming and adds the leniency of being repulsive. Why then refrain from piyyutim, when synagogue candles are wax or tallow? I did not find Bach permitting Haggadah reading by wax. Bach says: Rashi forbids a regular servant to inspect utensils by olive oil but permits an irregular servant, though this is not publicly ruled; Rif, Rosh, and Rabbeinu Tam hold the reverse. Thus, with olive oil neither servant may be permitted amid a serious Shabbos dispute. The present custom of leniency follows great authorities who say a wax candle creates no concern of tilting. For this reason people inspect tzitzis at dawn by wax candle, as found in Maharshal's name. Bach mentions only inspecting tzitzis and a servant inspecting utensils, not Haggadah reading. Those two have independent grounds for leniency: utensil inspection is itself disputed between Rashi and Rif, Rosh, and Rabbeinu Tam, so the permissive wax opinion is combined with it. Tzitzis inspection was prohibited by Magen Avraham 1 in the name of Mateh Moshe and Maharshal; these three great authorities needed to state the prohibition because one might have thought it permitted, perhaps because it requires little examination, like the regular servant permitted in se'if 12.

When the wick is in the oil, he knows to avoid taking any. But if he brings his hands near, we fear he may take oil even where the wick is inside it.
