In some versions this gloss is written later, concerning eating. Meaning, after Beit Yosef writes, “But in order to eat, he washes even outside,” Rama adds, “If he did not rub, he does not wash.” According to those versions, Rama's purpose is to remove the Levush's objection based on suspicion. One could still say that suspicion applies. Rama therefore places the gloss regarding eating, where suspicion is not relevant, and for that reason one who washes outside need wash only where he is legally obligated. Therefore, if he did not rub, he need not wash. Regarding drinking, however, where suspicion does apply, Magen Avraham would agree that even if he did not rub he must wash inside, as Levush writes. Magen Avraham rejects this version, however, because Rama holds that even for drinking one need not wash where he did not rub, and this is also the wording in the Agudah, for the reason Magen Avraham gave above, that in such a case there is no suspicion.

<b>Seif katan 5: He washes, etc.</b> I do not know why Beit Yosef reversed their wording. The Gemara in Yoma 30a states: One who leaves to urinate washes one hand and returns. If he conversed with his fellow and remained away for an extended period, he washes both hands and returns. When he washes, he should not wash outside and then enter, because of suspicion; rather, he enters, sits in his place, and washes both hands. Thus, the law that he must wash inside is taught after both cases, implying that it applies to both urination and prolonged absence. So too in the Tur and Rambam. Beit Yosef, however, reversed the order and wrote first, immediately after the law of urination, that he must wash inside, and only afterward wrote the law of prolonged absence. This implies that after a prolonged absence he may wash even outside.

**** ENGLISH ****
See siman 212 regarding what Shelah proved. Although we rule there that the principal food exempts the secondary food even where the secondary food is bread, so that he need not recite HaMotzi, the same applies to those who sit drinking and customarily eat a little bread to sweeten the drink. They need not recite a blessing over the bread, because it is secondary to the beverage, certainly if he expressly intended to eat bread only to sweeten the drink. Magen Avraham cites this ruling in the name of Shelah below in siman 212. Shelah further proved from the words of Rabbeinu Yonah cited above that although the beverages, as the principal item, exempt the secondary bread from HaMotzi, he must nevertheless wash his hands for that bread. So too wherever bread is exempted as secondary, netilas yadayim is still required. Here, one who wishes to drink and not eat has withdrawn his hands from eating, which constitutes the end of the meal. If he then wishes to eat, he would ordinarily need to recite a blessing over the bread. But because the meal has ended and he must recite a blessing over the wine he now drinks, since wine during the meal does not exempt wine after the meal, he need not recite a separate blessing over the small amount of bread eaten with the drink, because the bread is secondary to the beverage and the blessing over the wine exempts it. Even so, he must wash for that small amount of bread, because its secondary status does not dispense with washing. See there at length.

<b>אית' בתוס' דבכורות כו'</b> אמרי' התם א"ר אבא בריה דרחב"א משתינים מים מפני רבים (מפני הסכנה) ואין שותין מים בפני רבים וכתבו התוס' וז"ל פי' בקונטרס דרך ת"ח להיות צנוע באכילה ושתיה והא דאמרי' בפסחים דף פ"ו דרב הונ' בדר"נ אשתי' כו' ולא אהדר אפיה וקאמר התם הטעם כלה תנן (ר"ל דקתני התם במתניתין בשעת אכילת פסח והכלה הופכת פניה בשעת אכילה ואוכלת משום דהיא מתבייש' לאכול) אבל איש א"צ להפך פניו שמשמע התם שדרך לשתות בפני רבים בלא חזרת פנים היינו בשעת סעודה דומי' דכלה ובהלכות דרך ארץ אמרי' דברבים הופך פניו לצד אחר וישתה מים נראה דנקט מים למי שאין רגילים לשתות מים כולם אלא הצמא אבל שאר דברים רגילים לשתות יחד עכ"ל התוס' ומ"ש התוס' ובה' ד"א אמרי' כו' צ"ב כוונתם אם לדייק דנקט מים ע"כ למעט שאר משקין אתי דזה ה"מ להוכיח גם מדברי הש"ס (דדוחק לומר דבש"ס נקט מים איידי דמשתינים מים) אלא י"ל לענ"ד דכוונתם להקשות על תירוצם דתוך הסעודה א"צ להפך דהא בהלכות ד"א אמרי' דברבים הופך פניו כו' משמע:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Tosafos in Bechoros states, etc.</b> There the Gemara says in the name of Rabbi Abba son of Rav Chiya bar Abba: One may urinate in the presence of many because of danger, but one may not drink water in the presence of many. Tosafos writes that Rashi explains that a Torah scholar should be modest in eating and drinking. Yet in Pesachim 86, Rav Huna son of Rav Nachman drank and did not turn his face, and the Gemara explains, “We learned this only concerning a bride,” meaning that the Mishnah states that at the eating of the Pesach offering the bride turns her face and eats because she is embarrassed, but a man need not turn his face. This implies that it is normal to drink before many without turning away. Tosafos answers that this refers during a meal, like the case of a bride. In Hilchos Derech Eretz it is stated that among many one turns his face aside and drinks water. It appears that water is specified because not everyone is accustomed to drink water, only one who is thirsty, whereas other beverages are normally drunk together. The intent of Tosafos in citing Hilchos Derech Eretz requires explanation. If they meant merely to infer that the text specifies water in order to exclude other drinks, they could have inferred the same from the Gemara itself. It is difficult to say that the Gemara mentioned water only incidentally because it had spoken of urination. It therefore appears that Tosafos intended to challenge their own distinction.

The teaching should have said only, “One may not drink water before many,” as the Gemara states. It must therefore refer to one sitting at the table during a meal, where he cannot do otherwise except by turning his face. The fact that Tosafos in Pesachim inferred that the case was during a meal from its similarity to the bride, rather than from the wording about turning the face, may be because the comparison to the bride is a stronger inference. This proves that even during a meal one must turn away. Tosafos therefore answers that there is a distinction specifically between water and other beverages, and the incident in Pesachim involved another beverage.
