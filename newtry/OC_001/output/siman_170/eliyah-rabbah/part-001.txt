[4] <b>He washes both, etc.</b> Perishah writes that although we rule that leaving the meal does not require washing the hands and reciting HaMotzi anew, as the Tur writes in siman 174 and in Hilchos Sukkah, nevertheless the Tur cites this passage in order to derive another rule from it regarding a definite complete diversion of attention. This is strained. In my humble opinion, the Tur distinguishes and holds that when one left for an extended period, washing without a blessing is nevertheless required. See Bircas Avraham, page 94. This is likewise implied by Agudah, first chapter of Yoma, unlike Olas Tamid.

[5] <b>[Levush] And regarding this too they said, etc.</b> I say that were it not for the words of the great poskim, I would explain the Gemara in Yoma 30a as referring only to the latter case, where he left for an extended period. After urinating, however, there is no suspicion, for we may assume that he urinated in a manner that did not require rubbing. A proof is the Gemara’s wording: “Rather, he enters, sits in his place, and washes both hands,” which implies that it refers to one who left for an extended period, since after urinating one hand would suffice. However, Rif and Rosh do not quote the words “and washes both hands,” and it appears that these words were absent from their text. As a matter of law, although my explanation appears correct, it is not sufficient to rely upon against all the great poskim. I found an even greater novelty in Toras HaBayis HaAroch and Mishmeres HaBayis, page 157, that it is likely that even one who left for an extended period must wash inside, even before eating, because of suspicion, for what fastidiousness is involved when he merely left to speak? However, Toras HaBayis HaKatzar there rules this way regarding one who left for an extended period and does not mention the law of urination at all, as though the Gemara does not refer to urination, in accordance with my explanation. This requires extensive study.

[6] <b>[Levush] And he may choke, etc.</b> Malbushei Yom Tov writes: In my opinion, it is prohibited even without this concern, as Rama writes in Choshen Mishpat, siman 176 and siman 72. This is somewhat difficult, for if so, it should be prohibited even with three people, since Rama’s wording is that when members of a group eat together and one eats a great deal, the others may remove him. The Levush’s reason is also strained, and Magen Avraham likewise writes that “drinking” is not meant specifically. It appears that the Levush derived his view from Beit Yosef, who writes: “It appears to me that the meaning is that when one person drinks, the other waits for him,” etc. The Levush understood that the rule applies specifically when he drinks, because of danger. But he overlooked the words of Rabbeinu Yonah in the chapter Sheloshah SheAchlu: “While one person drinks, cuts bread, or waits to receive a piece from the one who recited HaMotzi, his fellow should wait for him because of proper conduct. Here the teaching concerns only proper conduct. It does not address the requirement to leave him his share when he interrupts for other matters and delays for a long time.” Beit Yosef too requires explanation for writing, “It appears to me,” without mentioning Rabbeinu Yonah’s words. Tzeidah LaDerech, page 59, writes that two people wait for one another, provided that the delay is not prolonged and that he does not interrupt with idle conversation.

**** ENGLISH ****
[9] <b>He should do, etc.</b> This is stated in Pesachim 86b. Magen Avraham asks: When they asked him, “Why, when they gave you a cup,” etc., he should have answered that it was because of the rule, “Whatever the host tells you,” etc. He answers that it is nevertheless improper manners to comply immediately before he is urged. This is difficult, for the Gemara implies that he immediately sat upon the bed. I saw that Maharsha explains that he preferred to answer from the formal rule that one does not refuse a great person. Yet the question remains: why did they ask, when they knew the rule that one must do whatever the host says? In my humble opinion, the answer is that the phrase “they gave him a cup” implies that they merely handed him the cup and did not tell him to drink. This is therefore not included in “Whatever the host tells you.” Examine this carefully. “Except leave”: Bach explains that one must perform every service within the house, except that if the host tells him to leave the house in order to serve him outside, he need not do so. Perishah, however, explains: Whatever the host merely tells you to do, do it, except if the host tells you to leave, do not leave unless he strikes you or throws out your belongings, because they said that a guest who leaves at once both disgraces and is disgraced. Lechem Chamudos, page 504, writes that it simply means leaving the house, and for this reason the poskim omitted it. The Gemara states that even if the host throws something, the guest may not deviate from his instruction.

[12] <b>Small, etc.</b> This means smaller than a revi’is. Shelah. With our beer, the measure is greater than a revi’is. Magen Avraham. Malbushei Yom Tov writes: The wording of Shulchan Aruch is “very small,” and regarding a large person too it says “very large.” This appears primary to me, for the Arizal said that the Sages did not state the rule unless one’s cup is small, his wine sweet, and his stomach broad, implying that all three conditions are required. Shulchan Aruch therefore carefully writes “very small” and “very large.” The Tur and Shulchan Aruch did not cite the Arizal’s words because it is uncommon for all three conditions to occur together. See Magen Avraham regarding this. Kesubos 65a states that a woman whose husband is not with her should not drink wine. A guest may not drink wine even when her husband is with her, because marital relations are forbidden to guests. See below, siman 240, seif 13. If she is accustomed to drinking wine when with her husband, she may drink a small amount when he is absent. When she is with her husband, however, she may drink even a large amount, even if she is not accustomed to doing so. Magen Avraham writes that the same applies to other intoxicating beverages. It is possible to distinguish, however, because wine gladdens the human heart.

[18] <b>[Levush] Even to place them, etc.</b> The Shulchan Aruch does not mention this here, and one must say that it relied on seif 10, where it had already written the rule. In truth, it requires study why these laws were not written in a single clause, as the Tur wrote them. It is also difficult why it was necessary to state that one may not place the piece in his fellow’s bowl, for this follows by a kal vachomer from the preceding rule that one may not place it on the table. In my humble opinion, the explanation is as I wrote in my commentary to Shulchan Aruch. Maseches Derech Eretz states: “A person should not bite from a piece of bread and return it to the bowl. Likewise, he should not bite from a piece of bread and give it to his fellow, nor drink from a cup and give it to his fellow.” It appears to me that this does not refer to the portion that he bit, for that may not even be placed on the table. Rather, it refers to the remaining portion, similar to the case of one who drinks from a cup and gives the remainder in the cup to his fellow. This is likewise evident from Rokeach, siman 329, who writes: “A person should not eat from a piece of bread and return it to the bowl.” Since he uses the term “eat” in place of “bite,” it implies that he eats what he bit and returns only the remainder. Shulchan Aruch therefore divided the cases: seif 10 discusses the portion that he actually bit. I likewise understand the Tur. Its statement that one may not place it on the table refers to the portion that he bit, while its statement “or in the bowl” refers to the remainder, even though both were written in one clause. This also resolves the question how the Tur derived the prohibition against placing it on the table, which is not mentioned in Maseches Derech Eretz. The answer is as explained. This is unlike Lechem Chamudos, page 224, who was forced to write that the Tur’s version also read, “and he should not place it on the table.”
