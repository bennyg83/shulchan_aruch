<b>ומברך שהחיינו. </b> ואם לא אמר אותו ביום א' מברך ביום ב' או באחד משאר ימים כשיזכור ואם אין לו כותח של יין בשעת הקידוש מברך זמן בלא כוס ריא"ז בש"ג פ' בכל מערבין וכ"כ המ"א והוא פשוט בש"ס פ' לולב וערבה וכה"ג מתבאר בהל' חנוכה סימן תרע"ז ע"ש וכ' עוד המ"א ונ"ל דה"ה אם שכח לומר זמן בליל ב' מברך כל ז' עכ"ל ונראה דה"ה בעצרת אע"ג דהוא רק שני ימים מ"מ יש לה תשלומין כל שבעה. ועוד נ"ל פשוט דאם שכח לומר זמן ליל ראשון ובליל ב' אמר זמן דא"צ שוב לומר זמן מכח לילה ראשונה להשלמה דאם יום ראשון קודש א"כ השלים הזמן ביום ב' ואם יום שני קודש הא אמר זמן ואם שכח לומר קידוש בלילה דינו כדלעיל סי' רע"א סעיף ח' וכ"כ הע"ש לקמן ס"ס תפ"ה: <b>ואם </b> שכח לומר ההגדה פשיטא שאין לו תשלומין כלל דכתי' בעבור זה בשעה שמצה ומרור מונחים לפניך וכ"כ המ"א לקמן סי' תפ"ה והשיג על הע"ש בזה שהבין בדבריו דקאי על ההגדה וזה אינו דהוא לא כ' רק דין אם שכח הקידוש וכמ"ש:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And he recites Shehecheyanu.</b> If he did not recite it on the first day, he recites it on the second day, or on one of the other days when he remembers. If he does not have a cup of wine at Kiddush, he recites Shehecheyanu without a cup. Riaz in Shiltei HaGibborim, chapter Bameh Me'arvin, and Magen Avraham write likewise. This is also explicit in the Gemara, chapter Lulav VaAravah, and a similar ruling is explained in the laws of Chanukah, siman 677; see there. Magen Avraham further writes: “It appears to me that the same applies if he forgot to recite Shehecheyanu on the second night, for he may recite it throughout all seven days.” It appears that the same applies to Shavuos. Although it lasts only two days, its offerings may be made up throughout all seven days. It also appears obvious that if he forgot to recite Shehecheyanu on the first night and recited it on the second night, he need not recite it again as compensation for the first night. If the first day was sacred, he made up the blessing on the second day; and if the second day was sacred, he recited it at its proper time. If he forgot Kiddush at night, the law is as stated above in siman 271, seif 8. Olas Shabbos writes likewise at the end of siman 485. <b>And if</b> he forgot to recite the Haggadah, it is obvious that there is no possibility of making it up, for the verse states, “Because of this,” meaning at the time when matzah and maror are placed before you. Magen Avraham writes likewise below in siman 485 and challenges Olas Shabbos, who understood his words as referring to the Haggadah. This is incorrect, for Olas Shabbos wrote only the law of one who forgot Kiddush, as stated.

<b>אומר יקנה"ז. </b> ואין מברכין על הבשמים בכל מוצאי שבת לי"ט ונאמרו בו הרבה טעמים עיין בתוס' והרא"ש פ' ע"פ: <b>וכתב </b> במ"א שאומרין ההבדלה מיושב כמו הקידוש וכן נוהגין ליישב ונ"ל הטעם כמבואר לעיל סי' תכ"ב דהלל אומרין מעומד דוקא אפ"ה בליל פסח אומרין אותו במיוש' דרך היסבה וחירות א"כ ה"ה לענין קידוש והבדלה ודלא כה"ג שכת' דיש לעמוד כשחל במ"ש:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>He recites Yaknehaz.</b> We do not recite a blessing over spices when Motzaei Shabbos falls on Yom Tov. Many reasons have been given; see Tosafot and Rosh in chapter Arvei Pesachim. <b>And Magen Avraham writes</b> that Havdalah is recited while seated, like Kiddush, and the custom is indeed to sit. It appears to me that the reason is as explained above in siman 422: although Hallel is generally recited specifically while standing, on the night of Pesach it is recited while seated, in a manner of reclining and freedom. The same therefore applies to Kiddush and Havdalah. This is unlike Knesses HaGedolah, who writes that one should stand when the first night falls on Motzaei Shabbos.

<b>ישלים ההגדה. </b> הלבוש כ' וז"ל ולא יפסיק בהגדה משמע דהטעם הוא שאסור להפסיק תוך ההגדה אבל דבר זה לא מצאתי בשום פוסק וגם למה לא יפסיק בסיפור ההגדה במצות הבדלה (וע"ל סי' תע"ב ס"ק ב') ובפרט בין הפרקים אלא נ"ל הטעם פשוט כי הבדלה צריכה כוס א"כ אם יבדיל תוך אמירת הגדה יפסיק אח"כ בין הבדלה לברכת הכוס באמירת ההגדה וא"ל שיברך על הכוס וימזוג לו כוס אחר לאמירת הגדה משום דזה נראה כמוסיף על הכוס כיון שנוטל כוס קבוע להבדל' והואיל ואפשר בענין אחר עבדינן וע' בסעיף ג':
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>He should complete the Haggadah.</b> Levush writes, “He should not interrupt the Haggadah,” implying that the reason is that one may not interrupt during the Haggadah. I have not found this in any posek, however. Why should he not interrupt the telling of the Haggadah for the mitzvah of Havdalah, especially between sections? See above, siman 472, seif katan 2. Rather, the reason appears simple. Havdalah requires a cup. If he were to recite Havdalah during the Haggadah, he would afterward interrupt between Havdalah and the blessing over the cup by continuing the Haggadah. Nor can one say that he should recite the blessing over that cup and then pour another cup for the Haggadah, because this would appear as though he were adding to the prescribed cups, since he took a designated cup for Havdalah. Since the matter can be arranged otherwise, we do so. See seif 3.

<b>ואח"כ יבדיל. </b> וכת' המ"א ואם נזכר באמצע סעודתו שלא הבדיל מבדיל מיד על הכוס ויברך בפה"ג אם לא היה דעתו לשתות יין תוך הסעודה ולא ימתין להבדיל עד אחר הסעודה דהוי כמוסיף על הכוסות דאין לומר ההבדלה והלל על כוס אחד ואם שכח עד אחר ברכת המזון יאמר ההלל והבדלה על כוס א' כיון דא"א בע"א עכ"ל מ"א וע"ל סי' רצ"ו:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And afterward he should recite Havdalah.</b> Magen Avraham writes that if he remembers in the middle of the meal that he did not recite Havdalah, he should immediately recite Havdalah over a cup. He should recite Borei Pri HaGafen if he had not intended to drink wine during the meal. He should not wait until after the meal, because that would appear as though he were adding to the cups, since Havdalah and Hallel should not be recited over one cup. If he did not remember until after Birkas HaMazon, he should recite Hallel and Havdalah over one cup, because there is no other possible arrangement. Thus far the words of Magen Avraham. <b>כדי שלא ישתכר. </b> ולפ"ז משמע דשאר משקין שאינם משכרין מותר לשתות אכן לפמ"ש המרדכי הטעם דבין ראשון לשני מותר לשתות דחמרא מגריר גריר ויאכל מצה לתיאבון אפשר דשאר משקים אסור לשתות בין ראשון לשני כיון שאינם גוררים אכן לא מצינו לשום פוסק חשש איסור בזה רק ביין הוצרך להתירו שלא יהא כמוסיף על הכוסות וגם שלא תימא חמרא מסעיד סעיד וע"ל סימן תע"א:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>So that he will not become intoxicated.</b> This implies that one may drink other beverages that are not intoxicating. However, according to Mordechai, the reason wine is permitted between the first and second cups is that wine stimulates the appetite, so he will eat matzah with appetite. It is therefore possible that other beverages are forbidden between the first and second cups, because they do not stimulate the appetite. Nevertheless, no posek expresses concern about a prohibition in this respect. Wine alone required an explicit permission, so that it should not appear as though he were adding to the cups, and so that one should not say that wine is filling. See above, siman 471.

<b>וחרוסת. </b> והם יהיו יותר קרובים. בש"ע נרשם כאן בשם מהרי"ל ובאמת במהרי"ל לא מצאתי כן רק שכ' וז"ל ירק האיפך נכון להניח שיהא סמוך אליו יותר מן המצות דאי המצות סמוכים לא היה צריך לבצוע אפיקומן מראשונה כדי שלא יהא מעביר על המצות עכ"ל אבל חרוסת ובשר וביצה לא הוזכר כלום כיון דאין מברכין עליהם רק לזכר בעלמא מביאין אותם על השלחן לא שייך גבייהו כלל אין מעבירין על המצות ואיכא מ"ד אין חרוסת מצוה כדאיתא במתני' פ' ע"פ ודוקא גבי מצה הקפיד מהרי"ל דהוא מצוה שמברכין עליה וצונו וגם הוא מוקדם לברכה ע"כ יש להקפיד לקרב אליו הירקות יותר מהמנה ואפילו המרור לא הוזכר כלל במהרי"ל לרחק או לקרב אע"ג שמברכין עליה וצונו ואפשר דכיון שאינו חביב על אדם ואין לו דין קדימה בברכה לכן לא הקפיד מהרי"ל על המרור גם הראיה מנר מערבי שהביא מהרי"ל שם יש לחלק יע"ש:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And charoses.</b> “And they should be closer to him.” Shulchan Aruch attributes this here to Maharil. In truth, I did not find this in Maharil. He writes only: “It is proper to place the eppich vegetable closer to him than the matzos, for if the matzos were closer, he would not need to break the afikoman from the upper matzah, so that he should not pass over the mitzvos.” Charoses, meat, and egg are not mentioned at all. Since no blessing is recited over them and they are brought to the table only as a remembrance, the principle that one may not pass over mitzvos does not apply to them. Moreover, there is an opinion that charoses is not a mitzvah, as stated in the Mishnah in chapter Arvei Pesachim. Maharil was particular only regarding matzah, which is a mitzvah over which we recite “and commanded us,” and whose blessing precedes that of the vegetable. Therefore the vegetables should be placed closer to him than the matzah. Even maror is not mentioned by Maharil as needing to be placed nearer or farther away, although we recite “and commanded us” over it. Perhaps, because people do not favor it and it has no priority regarding blessings, Maharil was not particular about the maror. The proof Maharil brings there from the western lamp may also be distinguished; see there.

<b>Chazeres.</b> Since not everyone is familiar with these vegetables and their names, the terminology varies from country to country, and not every vegetable is found in every place, I have therefore decided to copy here all the explanations of these vegetables that I found in the poskim, so that each person may understand the matter clearly according to the language and country familiar to him. The Gemara in Pesachim explains that chazeres is lettuce. Rashi calls it latuga; Aruch, latuka; Hagahos Maimoniyos, lektika or latich. Levush writes that it is what we call zersich in these countries. My brother-in-law, the author of Sefer Eliyah Zuta, cites a manuscript that challenges Levush and says that it is instead called latich in German and lactuca in Latin. Medical books list seven varieties of it, and it has no connection to the common zersich of these countries. In Berachos, chapter HaRoeh, regarding Bar Hedya's interpretations of dreams, Rashi explains the phrase “your affairs will be doubled like lettuce” as referring to a vegetable that is folded and thick. I heard from one who speaks the Romance language that in the warm countries they have chazeres during Nisan, and it is folded and thick, like what we call head lettuce in these countries, and this is what they eat as maror. See Beit Yosef, who writes that the mitzvah is fulfilled with chazeres even though it is sweet and has no bitterness. Yet in chapter HaRoeh, Bar Hedya said to Rava, “Your affairs will be bitter like lettuce.” One must therefore say that it nevertheless has some bitterness. Aruch writes that mature chazeres is bitter lettuce. Agudah writes: “If latuga is unavailable, one should take horseradish.” Olsheen is called hindivi in the Gemara. Rashi calls it kereshpila; Aruch explains it as a certain wild endive, called sinzoni in the vernacular, and some call it krespinio. Shibbolei HaLeket cites that it is astrola, and some call it in the vernacular damtika endiva. Rav Ovadiah of Bartenura likewise identifies it as endive. It is common among us and is cooked and eaten with sugar and vinegar to reduce its bitterness. Hagahos Maimoniyos identifies it as kerbel, which is also common among us. Maharil, however, writes that since the siddurim prescribe kerbel for the first dipping, it follows that one does not fulfill the mitzvah of maror with it. Tamcha is identified by Rashi as marubya; Aruch calls it kardavya or merayu. The later authorities write that it is what is called horseradish in German, and Tosafos Yom Tov writes likewise; see there where he challenges Rav Ovadiah of Bartenura. The custom has therefore spread in these countries to take horseradish for maror, reciting the blessing over the leaves and taking the stalk for korech. Charchavina is identified in the Gemara as the ivy of the palm tree. Rashi calls it vidla, and Rokeach calls it agrorin. Maror is called merarita in the Gemara. Rashi calls it amerfoyl in the vernacular, and Agudah identifies it as vermouth, meaning wormwood.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>But not pickled.</b> Rashi explains this as pickled in vinegar. The later authorities write that Rashi follows his own view, cited in his name in Yoreh Deah 105, that soaking in water does not have the law of pickling, only soaking in vinegar or brine. We, however, rule that an item soaked in water for twenty-four hours is considered pickled like cooked food. They therefore write that one should protest those who soak the maror for twenty-four hours, because the mitzvah cannot then be fulfilled with it. In my humble opinion, however, the custom of Israel has the force of Torah, and those who soak it so that the leaves remain moist and do not wilt have authorities upon whom to rely. Even the poskim who rule regarding prohibitions that soaking in water for twenty-four hours is considered pickling for purposes of absorption and release would agree here that only vinegar makes the maror halachically pickled. The Gemara on Pesachim 39 states regarding maror that the general principle is that one fulfills the mitzvah with anything that retains the taste of maror. As long as it was not pickled in vinegar, it retains the taste of maror, as can be plainly seen and tasted. There is also proof from the wording of the Mishnah and baraisa there, which teach: “but not pickled, scalded, or cooked.” Tosafot explain that scalding is more intense than cooking, otherwise the sequence would be unnecessary. The poskim agree. Yet the opening phrase, “but not pickled,” would still appear unnecessary if ordinary water-pickling is less intense than cooking, while the sequence “not scalded and not cooked” would proceed from the stronger to the weaker. Rather, the pickling mentioned there must mean pickling in vinegar, as Rashi explains. Vinegar changes and nullifies the bitterness more than scalding or cooking in water. The Mishnah's sequence therefore proceeds throughout from the less obvious case to the more obvious. The Gemara's general principle then includes soaking in water, since the maror retains its bitter taste. Apart from all this, I wrote in my work Minchas Yaakov that several poskim hold that soaking in water for twenty-four hours is not considered pickling. Although we rule stringently regarding prohibitions, maror in our time is D'Rabbanan, and this stringency can itself lead to a leniency, because people may eat the leaves when wilted. No early posek explicitly forbids soaking maror in water. There is therefore clear reason to permit it, and one should not be stringent. A similar point may be made below in siman 521; see there.

The reason we wash on this night is as one of the other unusual practices performed so that the children will ask.

<b>ואינו מברך אחריו. </b> אף ע"ג שאכל כזית והב"ח כ' וז"ל ונראה הרוצה לצאת ידי חובת כל הגאונים יאכל בטבול זה כזית שלם ויהיה דעתו בשעת ברכת בפה"א שלא לפטור המרור בברכה זו וממילא חייב לברך אחר טיבול ראשון בנ"ר לד"ה ואחר כך כשיברך המוציא יכוין נמי שלא להוציא המרור בברכת המוציא ויחזור ויברך בפה"א על א"מ ובנ"ר דאז יוצא לכל הדעות ואין כאן משום ברכה שאינה צריכה שעושה כן לאפוקי נפשיה מפלוגתא עכ"ל ועדיין לא יצא לדעת הסמ"ג סימן ר"כ דאין לברך על המרור כיון שלא בא להנות ממנו רק לזכר בעלמא לכן נ"ל עיקר כהסכמת רוב ראשונים ומהרי"ל סי' נ"ט והמ"א דאין לברך כלל:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And he does not recite an after-blessing.</b> This applies even if he ate a kezayis. Bach writes that one who wishes to satisfy all the Geonim should eat a full kezayis at this first dipping and intend when reciting Borei Pri HaAdamah not to exempt the maror with that blessing. He will then be obligated, according to all opinions, to recite Borei Nefashos after the first dipping. When he later recites HaMotzi, he should likewise intend not to exempt the maror with HaMotzi, and should again recite Borei Pri HaAdamah over the eating of maror and Borei Nefashos afterward. In this manner he satisfies all opinions, and there is no issue of an unnecessary blessing because he acts to remove himself from a dispute. Nevertheless, he has still not satisfied the opinion of Semag, siman 220, who holds that no blessing is recited over maror because it is not eaten for enjoyment but only as a remembrance. It therefore appears to me that the primary ruling follows the agreement of most early authorities, Maharil, siman 59, and Magen Avraham, that no after-blessing is recited at all.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Who recites the Haggadah.</b> See Beit Yosef, who rules that a blind person is also obligated to recite the Haggadah, unlike Rabbeinu Yerucham and Responsa Radbaz, siman 59. This is also the conclusion of the Gemara in Pesachim 115, that he is obligated in reciting the Haggadah and may discharge the obligation of others. See Shaar Ephraim, siman 27, who reconciles the words of Rabbeinu Yerucham but agrees in practice with Beit Yosef's conclusion. Although the Gemara's conclusion may permit a distinction, nothing is lost by saying the shorter formula, “The matzah that we eat,” and so forth. See Bach in siman 460, who writes in the name of Maharshal concerning one who was imprisoned and did not know how to recite the Haggadah but possessed a Chumash, that he recited all the passages concerning the Exodus from Egypt.
