**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And He created.</b> See Taz. The view of Beit Yosef is explained in the Shulchan Aruch. The word “with wisdom” follows Rashi on Berachos 60b, who explains that the wonder itself is the wisdom, or Tosafos there, who explain the phrase “Who fashioned.” The Tur omitted both explanations, and Beit Yosef explains that the creation of man is certainly an act of wisdom. It is difficult, however, why the Tur chose an independent interpretation and omitted both Rashi and Tosafos. Beit Yosef further explains that “if one of them were to close” refers to the one special opening, the mouth, at the moment of birth, for if it remained closed even briefly one could not survive. The phrase is in the future tense because even afterward, if it were to close excessively, one could not survive. “If one of them were to open” refers to the many organs, any one of which, were it to open even after birth, would make survival impossible even briefly. Taz asks why Beit Yosef says that one should not say “hollow spaces” because creation does not apply to them, while the same objection should apply to “openings.” Bach further asks that Scripture says, “Who creates darkness,” although darkness is merely the absence of light. It appears to me that creation can indeed apply to something without tangible substance, such as openings and darkness, because they nevertheless constitute a created function: through openings substances enter and leave, and the removal of light produces darkness. Hollow spaces differ, because the cavity comes into being automatically through the formation of the surrounding organ. This explains why Beit Yosef and the Shulchan Aruch mention “openings,” such as the mouth, and “hollow organs,” such as the internal organs. With regard to openings they do not mention organs, because the reference is to the openings themselves, to which creation applies. Accordingly, the wording “He created within him,” rather than “He fashioned,” is fitting, because fashioning applies only to something tangible, whereas creation may apply even to something tangible, as in “He created man.” Taz further asks that the Tur cites the Gemara in Niddah, which states that in the womb the navel is open and the mouth is closed. This implies that “if one were to open” refers to the navel at birth, just as “if one were to close” refers to the mouth remaining closed. If the reference were to internal cavities that might open later, why use the future tense? Taz therefore explains that the Tur does not follow Rashi’s division. Rather, “hollow spaces” refers to the mouth and navel themselves, while “openings” refers to the intestines. In the womb the navel and intestines must be open, while the cavity of the mouth must be closed; after birth the arrangement must be reversed. This is the wondrous wisdom of the Creator expressed in the words “Who fashioned with wisdom.” The doubled wording “openings, openings” teaches that the two are not open at the same time. At one stage one is open and the other closed, and at another stage the reverse is true. The same organs are thus described both as openings and as hollow spaces, depending on their condition. It is therefore accurate to say, “If one of them were to open or one of them were to close,” both in the womb and after birth. The author of the Shulchan Aruch, however, explains the Tur’s “hollow spaces” like Rashi and “openings” according to the Gemara in Niddah, referring only to the mouth. This yields the difficulty that “one of them” has one meaning in the clause concerning closing and another meaning in the clause concerning opening. See Rashi on Berachos there, who explains that opening refers to the hollow spaces and closing to the openings. His point is to exclude the reading that “hollow spaces” means the organs themselves. Just as “closing” refers to the openings, not to pierced organs, so “opening” refers to the cavities, not to the organs. I have elaborated according to my limited understanding, though to comprehend the intent of the holy authorities I still require clarification. See what I wrote in Mishbetzos Zahav.

<b>אפי׳</b> עט״ז תראה שהאדון ז״ל כיון כוונה מיוחדת במ״ש ואחרים פירשו דקאי אשעת יצירה כי הב״י פירש לטור דקאי איציאה לאויר העולם אבל הוא ז״ל פירש איצירה במעי אמו שאם יסתם הטבור ויפתח הפה וכמ״ש באות א׳ אפי׳ רגע א״א ומאן דלא גרס שעה פי׳ כרש״י. ואולי באבודרה״ם או בכל בו מצא כתוב ל׳ ואחרים פירשו ואני לא מצאתיו כעת:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Even.</b> See Taz. The master intended a specific point when he wrote that “others explain” the phrase as referring to the time of formation. Beit Yosef explains the Tur as referring to emergence into the air of the world, whereas Taz explains it as referring to formation in the womb: if the navel were closed and the mouth open, survival would be impossible even briefly, as explained in paragraph 1. One who does not have the reading “for one hour” explains it like Rashi. Perhaps Taz found the phrase “others explain” in Abudarham or Kol Bo, though I have not presently found it.

<b>רופא</b> עט״ז ב׳ גירסאות יש להם מקום ובד״מ נמי כתב שאומר רופא חולי כל בשר שאדם בריא יכולה הנפש להשכיל במושכלות שאל״כ היסורין מבטלין משמע שי״ל חולי כל בשר יע״ש:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Healer.</b> See Taz. Both textual readings have a basis. Darkei Moshe also writes that one says, “Who heals the illnesses of all flesh,” because when a person is healthy the soul can engage in intellectual matters, whereas suffering prevents this. This implies that the wording “the illnesses of all flesh” is appropriate. See there.

<b>יש</b> עט״ז: עב״י בשם מהרי״א ענט״י עובר לעשייתן קודם הניגוב ובאמת לסעודה שפיר דניגוב מעכב דכאלו אוכל לחם טמא משא״כ שחרית נגוב אין מעכב עיין מ״א ד׳ ו׳ וקנ״ח סי״ב אלא דמ״מ כל שיכול להסמיך הברכה סמכינן ואף ברכת השבח ראוי להיות תיכ״ף אחר הראייה והיכא דלא בירך תכ״ד מברך אח״כ כמ״ש הט״ז ברכ״ז ב׳ ירושלמי (עיין ר״ן ספ״ק דפסחים) וא״כ שכוי בינה וכדומה דא״א תוך כ״ד תו לא נתנו חז״ל שיעור תכיפה ומסדרם בבה״כ להוציא אחרים משא״כ ענט״י מיד כשיטול קודם ניגוב יברך דהוא שיעור תכיפה וכ״ש להר״ב סימן מ״ו דמברך על מנהגו של עולם פשיטא ענ״י יברך מיד ולכן נהג הוא כבני ספרד ועב״י יישב זה דבני אשכנז נוהגין ע״פ הרשב״א ענ״י נמי שבח הוא יע״ש עט״ז ד׳ א׳ ו׳ א׳ והבן:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Some.</b> See Taz and Beit Yosef in the name of Mahari Abuhav, who writes that the blessing over washing the hands should be recited before drying, so that it precedes the completion of the act. Regarding washing for a meal this is correct, because drying is indispensable, since one who eats without drying is considered as though he ate impure bread. Morning washing differs, because drying is not indispensable. See Magen Avraham, siman 4, seif katan 6, and siman 158, seif 12. Nevertheless, whenever the blessing can be placed close to the act, it should be. Even a blessing of praise should ideally be recited immediately after seeing the event that prompts it. If one did not recite it immediately, however, one may still recite it later, as Taz writes in siman 227, seif katan 2, based on the Yerushalmi. Consequently, with blessings such as “Who gives the rooster understanding,” where immediate recitation is impossible, the Sages did not establish a precise measure of proximity, and they may be arranged in the synagogue in order to discharge others. The blessing over washing the hands, by contrast, can be recited immediately after washing, before drying, and should therefore be recited then. This is especially clear according to Rama in siman 46, who holds that the morning blessings are recited over the general order of the world. Therefore he followed the Sephardic custom. Beit Yosef explains that Ashkenazic communities follow Rashba, who regards the blessing over washing the hands as a blessing of praise as well. See Taz, seif katan 4, Magen Avraham, seif katan 1, and siman 6, seif katan 1, and understand.
