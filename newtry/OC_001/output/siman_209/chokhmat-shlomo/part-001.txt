**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Seif 2.</b> If one took a cup of beer or water, and within the time required for speech remembered that he had erred and said, “that everything came into being through His word,” and this was the exact wording he used, etc. Note: The phrase “and this was the exact wording he used” appears superfluous. Once it has already stated that within the time required for speech he remembered his error and said Shehakol, the matter is self-evident. Why add an explanation of what he said? In the laws of Pesach, siman 487, the Shulchan Aruch writes only briefly that he remembered within the time required for speech and concluded with the Yom Tov formula; there it does not add the phrase “and this was the exact wording he used,” as it does here. It therefore appears that the Mechaber intended something significant with this wording. Magen Avraham already noted why the Mechaber did not explain here that the person knew the cup contained beer or water, as he explained there in the laws of Pesach. It therefore appears as follows. Some poskim write that even in matters concerning which we rule that a retraction within the time required for speech is ineffective, if the correction forms literally one continuous utterance, retraction is effective. This is cited by Beit Yosef in Even HaEzer, siman 38, to reconcile Rambam’s ruling concerning an act performed before stating a condition. He distinguishes between a retraction within the time required for speech and one continuous utterance: in one continuous utterance, retraction is effective according to all opinions. See there and Bach there. Accordingly, the same may be said here. If the person did not say everything as one literal utterance, then although he corrected himself within the time required for speech, he must at least have known that it was Yom Tov; otherwise he has not fulfilled his obligation. Here, however, the case is one continuous utterance. There, where the Mechaber explains that the person knew it was Yom Tov, he may therefore state simply that if he corrected himself within the time required for speech he fulfilled his obligation; it need not be one literal utterance. Here, however, the case is one in which he did not know it was beer. Therefore, a mere correction within the time required for speech does not help if there was even a slight pause. The Mechaber consequently explains that when he says the person remembered within the time required for speech, he means that his very utterance was “Borei peri hagefen, Shehakol nihyah bidvaro.” Since it was one literal utterance, he fulfilled his obligation even though he did not know it was beer or water. This may also resolve Magen Avraham’s question why the Mechaber did not write here “according to some opinions,” since according to the first opinion the law depends upon his intent. In my view, the poskim cited above may hold the opposite distinction. We follow the person’s intent only where he did not conclude at all in accordance with his original intent. There the defect lies in the blessing itself, because its end does not correspond to its beginning and its beginning does not correspond to its end. At first he intended to recite Borei peri hagefen, but afterward concluded Shehakol. Even if the beverage were actually wine, as he originally intended, he still would not have recited its proper blessing in the ideal manner. Although he would fulfill his obligation, that would only be by virtue of the separate rule that one who recited Shehakol over anything has fulfilled his obligation. Nevertheless, Shehakol is not wine’s proper blessing in the first instance. Where the conclusion of the blessing was not what he originally intended, we therefore follow his intent. But if he concluded the blessing in accordance with his original intent, the blessing itself was properly recited. Had the cup truly contained wine, it would have been an excellent blessing from beginning to end. The defect arises only from an external fact, namely that this cup was not wine. Where the impediment comes from an external circumstance, we do not consider his original intent. Therefore, since he concluded Shehakol within the same utterance, he fulfilled his obligation. This may be further clarified by the rule that mitzvos require intent only for a mitzvah in which one is obligated. Where one is not obligated in the act, intent is not required, as I wrote in my work Sefer HaChaim, siman 60. I was later informed that Ran writes this in his novellae to Chullin. Accordingly, where the defect lies within the blessing itself, because its conclusion does not correspond to its beginning and he did not initially intend that conclusion, since he is eating or drinking and is therefore obligated to recite a blessing, the blessing is treated as an obligatory mitzvah and requires intent. But where there is no contradiction within the blessing itself, because the blessing was properly recited from beginning to end with one consistent intent, and the defect arises only from the food or drink not matching the blessing, the eating and drinking themselves are optional. For an optional matter, intent is not required. It is therefore better where he completed Borei peri hagefen in accordance with his intent and then immediately added Shehakol, because if the beverage had been wine, as he intended, the blessing would have been entirely proper from beginning to end. By contrast, if at first he intended wine but concluded only Shehakol, then even if it had been wine, as he originally intended, the blessing would not have been the proper blessing in the first instance. The defect would lie in the very blessing, and the absence of proper intent would therefore invalidate it. Since in fact it was beer rather than wine, he did not fulfill his obligation. But where he completed Borei peri hagefen, the blessing itself would have been excellent had the drink been wine, and the defect lies only in the beverage not matching the blessing. There, intent is not required, only the recitation of a blessing, and since he added Shehakol within the same utterance, he fulfilled his obligation. This also explains the differing language of the Shulchan Aruch. The effect of retraction within the time required for speech is that the correction uproots the first utterance and renders it as though it had never been said. Proof appears from Nazir and Temurah, cited in Ketzos HaChoshen, siman 405: where one may seek annulment, one may also retract within the time required for speech; where annulment is ineffective, retraction within the time required for speech is likewise ineffective. This clearly shows that just as annulment uproots a vow or consecration retroactively as though it had never been uttered, so too retraction within the time required for speech uproots the initial utterance retroactively. Otherwise, what comparison would there be between them? This applies, however, only where there was at least some pause, in which case the correction works only through retraction and treats the first utterance as nonexistent. Where it is one literal utterance, it works even without retroactively uprooting the first words. This is the distinction in those matters where retraction within the time required for speech is generally ineffective. A retraction of that type would uproot the first utterance retroactively, and in weighty matters one cannot do so. As Taz writes in Even HaEzer, siman 38, regarding a gift, since an act has been performed he cannot retract. But where it is one literal utterance, the correction works without uprooting the first utterance, and therefore it is effective even in those cases. This explains the Shulchan Aruch here and in the laws of Yom Tov. There, the case is that he did not immediately conclude with the Yom Tov wording but paused slightly. Although the correction was within the time required for speech, it works only by uprooting the first utterance as though it had never been said. If the first utterance is uprooted, it is as though he concluded only with the Yom Tov wording while originally intending Shabbos, which according to Rambam and those who agree with him is ineffective because intent is decisive. Therefore, he must have known that it was Yom Tov, and then the correction within the time required for speech is effective. Here, however, the case is one literal utterance, which does not require uprooting the first words. It is as though he said Borei peri hagefen and nevertheless concluded in the same utterance with Shehakol. Since he also said Borei peri hagefen, the absence of intent is not decisive. Therefore the Mechaber requires one literal utterance. If it were not one literal utterance but merely within the time required for speech, it would not help where he did not know that it was beer. According to Rambam’s view that intent is required, either way he would fail: if Borei peri hagefen is treated as still present, the later Shehakol was not intended and followed a pause; if Borei peri hagefen is retroactively uprooted, then under Rambam’s view the blessing is still ineffective because he originally intended wine. Therefore, one continuous utterance is required. This correctly explains the Shulchan Aruch’s rulings. Another explanation may be offered based on what I wrote above, distinguishing between a blessing one is obligated to recite and one in which one is not obligated. Here the case concerns a blessing that is not obligatory, and the Shulchan Aruch therefore rules primarily not in accordance with Rambam: we disregard his original intent, and since he concluded properly he fulfilled his obligation. Later, regarding the Yom Tov blessing, which is obligatory, the halachah may follow Rambam that intent is required. If he originally intended Shabbos, concluding with the Yom Tov formula does not help; therefore there he must have known it was Yom Tov and merely misspoken. According to this, where the cup is obligatory, such as a cup of Kiddush or one of the Four Cups, if he intended Borei peri hagefen because he thought it was wine, then learned it was beer and concluded Shehakol, he would not fulfill his obligation, because in an obligatory matter we rule like Rambam that intent is required. Nevertheless, where it was one literal utterance, it appears that even in an obligatory matter he has fulfilled his obligation. This appears correct to me as practical halachah. Indeed, according to Magen Avraham, the Shulchan Aruch should have explained here that he knew the actual nature of the beverage, just as he explains in the laws of Pesach. This would be all the more necessary, because there, in the laws of Yom Tov, the person did not initially intend the opposite, yet the Shulchan Aruch explains that he knew; here, where he did initially intend the opposite, the Mechaber does not explain so. According to my explanation, however, there was no need to say that he knew, because the first clause itself concerns one who did not know, and this clause plainly continues that case. Examine this carefully.<br><b>There, in the gloss.</b> “And if others were also drinking and wine was before them.” Note: See Magen Avraham below, siman 213, who questions this. See what I wrote in my novellae to the laws of Rabbeinu Tam, in my work on Orach Chayim from the year 5599, in the laws of siman 599, where I reject Magen Avraham’s answer and resolve the matter differently. Rama did not mention without reason that wine was before them. Specifically in such a case, where their intent is evident from their actions, they fulfill their obligation and he fulfills his obligation through them. Otherwise, they depend upon him, and since he did not fulfill his obligation, they too do not fulfill theirs. Only where their intent is evident from their actions are they considered as though they themselves recited the blessing, and then he depends upon them. See there, where with Hashem’s help I explained the reasoning fully. Examine this carefully.
