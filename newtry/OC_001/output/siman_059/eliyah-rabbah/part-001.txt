[2] <b>And he remembered immediately and said, and so forth.</b> This implies that if he did not remember immediately, but only after the time needed to utter a brief phrase, he has not fulfilled his obligation. Bach explains that the opening formula, ‘Blessed are You, Hashem our God, King of the universe,’ does not apply to his later words ‘Who forms light,’ because he did not say them immediately. Therefore, even if he concluded with ‘Who forms light,’ he has not fulfilled his obligation, since the conclusion lacks mention of kingship. According to this, if he concludes, ‘Blessed are You, Hashem our God, King of the universe, Who forms the luminaries,’ he has fulfilled his obligation. I saw, however, that Derishah explains that every blessing requires wording related to its opening close to its conclusion, and all the more so the conclusion itself must relate to the opening. If he does not say it within the time needed to utter a brief phrase, it is as though he did not say it as part of the blessing. See there. According to this, it is understandable that even if he concludes, ‘Blessed are You, Hashem our God, King of the universe, Who forms the luminaries,’ he has not fulfilled his obligation, because the conclusion does not relate to the opening. Magen Avraham writes: It appears to me that if he did not remember until after completing Ahavah Rabbah, he should not interrupt, but should recite Yotzer Or after the Amidah. This is not convincing, because all agree that Yotzer Or was initially instituted before Krias Shema. Rather, they should interrupt and recite Yotzer Or and Ahavah Rabbah, as stated in siman 60.

They therefore instituted the plea ‘In Your abundant mercy,’ and so forth, in the blessing over the luminaries. ‘God, blessed, great,’ and so forth, follows the alphabet, through whose letters heaven and earth endure. [4] <b>And when they respond, and so forth.</b> Meaning, in public. This also appears from Levush, for regarding an individual it is explained in siman 132 that he says it quietly. See above, siman 61. Meaning, Rama writes there that the custom is to answer. The Tur likewise writes that this is no worse than responding to a greeting, for which one may interrupt. Shelah, page 253, writes that this does not appear correct to him. Rather, the Sephardic custom is not to answer Amen and interrupt. He adds as their reason that even if one recites Krias Shema with awe, fear, trembling, and perspiration throughout all 248 limbs, he must nevertheless pass from fear to love, so that the joy of faith in Hashem's unity enters his heart, and he is prepared to surrender his life and all his possessions. The blessing therefore concludes with love both in the morning and evening, followed immediately by accepting the yoke of the Kingdom of Heaven and then ‘You shall love,’ and so forth. One should therefore not interrupt. Rather, it appears that he should prolong his recitation so as to conclude the blessing together with the chazzan. Consequently, if he finished before the chazzan, it is proper to answer Amen in accordance with the Tur's view.
