**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Seif 2: If he erred, and so forth; and the same applies, and so forth.</b> The Tur's words are explained as requiring two conditions. First, in the opening portion of the blessing he must mention ‘Who forms light,’ either at the beginning or in the middle. Second, the conclusion must be proper. If either condition is lacking, he has not fulfilled his obligation. This requires study, for the Rosh's words do not appear to indicate this, nor does the Gemara appear to say so. The Gemara answers, ‘There it is different,’ and so forth, which indicates that the conclusion alone suffices. Even according to the final answer, ‘Rather, since,’ and so forth, it likewise appears that the conclusion suffices. His interpretation, however, follows the ruling that the unresolved question is decided leniently, and reads the baraisa in its straightforward sense because he returned and said ‘Who forms light,’ according to the Rif's explanation. Yet the baraisa's wording, ‘Its general principle is this,’ and so forth, implies that this refers to the conclusion of the blessing. Therefore, the primary ruling is that if he concluded ‘Who forms the luminaries,’ then even though he initially said the entire text of ‘Who brings on evenings,’ he has fulfilled his obligation according to every interpretation in the Gemara.

<b>Seif 3: Some say, and so forth; and some say, and so forth.</b> This also appears from the Mishnah in Megillah 23b; see Rashi there, s.v. ‘One may not divide,’ and so forth. Seemingly, the first blessing does not require ten except because of Kedushah, and Ran writes likewise there. Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe already addressed this in siman 69; see there. All their explanations are strained. This is also the view of the Geonim and Rambam, as Ran proves there in Megillah. The students of Rabbeinu Yonah further prove this in the third chapter of Berachos from the Yerushalmi's statement, ‘They began with ten and some departed,’ and so forth. This implies that they began with dividing Krias Shema, namely the blessing. What then does ‘they complete’ mean? Although he rejects that proof there, as cited in Beit Yosef there and as I write below, the first opinion supported itself from the end of chapter 16 of Maseches Soferim, cited by Mordechai in the third chapter of Berachos. It states there: ‘A minor who may not divide Krias Shema cannot say the Kedushah of Yotzer when alone, but in public he responds with them. An adult who divides Krias Shema may say Kedushah, because it is part of the order. But the Kedushah of the Amidah, since one must say, “We shall reverence You and sanctify You,” and so forth, should not be recited with fewer than ten.’ Yet that source appears to prove the opposite. At first glance it is difficult, for when it says, ‘An adult who divides,’ and so forth, it necessarily refers to ten. Why then does it add, ‘because,’ and so forth, but contrasts this with the Amidah? Rather, its meaning is as follows. Although six who did not yet hear suffice for dividing Krias Shema, as stated there at the end of chapter 10, the Kedushah of the Amidah requires ten who did not yet hear it, as stated in the Mishnah in Megillah and in Maseches Soferim there. Kaddish and Barechu may not be recited with fewer than ten. Our teachers in the West say seven, and give a reason for their position. This implies that according to the first view, precisely ten who have not heard are required. Even according to our teachers, this applies only to Barechu, which they derive from a verse, whereas Kedushah remains governed by the original rule. Thus, when it says that a minor cannot, and so forth, it means that since he cannot divide Krias Shema, he also cannot say Kedushah. In public, however, and so forth. An adult, and so forth, may likewise say Kedushah with seven or six, because it is part of the order of dividing Krias Shema, unlike the Kedushah of the Amidah. The Zohar states this explicitly, and Beit Yosef cites it. Darkei Moshe, however, writes that since the matter is explicit in Maseches Soferim, one need not be concerned for the Zohar. This is the meaning of Rama's gloss, ‘It has already,’ and so forth. Yet I have already written that Maseches Soferim itself maintains the opposite. The earlier sages likewise instituted reciting baraisos in place of Kedushah because they accepted the latter opinion. According to the first opinion, there would be no need for this, since it is no better than the Kedushah of U'Va LeTzion and Yotzer.

**** ENGLISH ****
“Quietly, etc.” This is implied by the Tosefta cited above according to the Hagahot Maimoniyot, which explains “they respond” to mean that they say it aloud. The Rosh, however, does not explain it this way, for his words imply that were it not for the aforementioned concern, the individual would not recite the blessing. This indicates that in his time individuals did not recite the blessings; rather, everyone listened to the chazzan, and the concern he mentioned caused our custom to develop. The Tosefta is understood according to its plain meaning, as stated there: “He would say all these,” etc., implying that the others did not recite them. The reason he recited all of them is as I wrote above in siman 49 in the name of the Rosh: one cannot discharge another’s obligation in an oral recitation in that manner, as stated in the Tur there. He nevertheless recites it quietly, because the primary rule is to listen to the chazzan.

**** ENGLISH ****
“Nevertheless, etc., the blessings, etc.” As stated in the Tosefta. Even R’ Yehuda would recite only Kedushah. The Ran writes there in Megillah in the name of the Geonim that “one may not divide, etc.” means that one may not begin the blessings of Keriat Shema in a manner in which one person recites the blessing and the others fulfill their obligation through his blessing unless ten are present. This is unlike other blessings, such as Kiddush, Havdalah, and similar blessings, where even among two people one may discharge the other’s obligation, except for Birkat HaMazon, which requires three; see there for the reason. The requirement here is because of the Kedushah contained in these blessings. The Sages did not distinguish in their enactment even at Maariv, etc. This means that one who knows the blessing may discharge one who does not know it with fewer than ten, except for the Kedushah within it. This is like what is stated regarding Birkat HaMazon in the seventh chapter of Berakhot. Rabbeinu Yonah likewise writes in the third chapter of Berakhot that, because of the difficulty stated above, he holds that an individual may recite this Kedushah. He therefore explains the rule to mean that the listeners fulfill their obligation only by answering Amen, and that one person may discharge the others only in the presence of ten. This is also alluded to by the statement in the third chapter of the Jerusalem Talmud: “It is not reasonable regarding Keriat Shema that each person should review it with his own mouth.” See above, siman 594, in the Rama’s gloss, and the Magen Avraham there.
