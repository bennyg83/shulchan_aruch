[1] <b>After he has donned, etc.</b> Knesses HaGedolah, Hagahos Beis Yosef, writes: I heard a Scriptural support from the verse, 'The wrapped ones were Lavan's, and the bound ones Yaakov's.' Levush and Beis Yosef explain that the mitzvah of tzitzis is equivalent to all the mitzvos; moreover, it is more frequent, since it applies also on Shabbos and Yom Tov, and a frequent mitzvah takes precedence. For this reason, Shiyurei Knesses HaGedolah, Hagahos HaTur 1, writes that one who cannot afford both tzitzis and tefillin should give precedence to tzitzis. Olas Tamid 1 objects that this is incorrect and that he should buy tefillin, for our Sages said, 'The sinners of Yisrael with their bodies' refers to a skull that did not put on tefillin. I say that this reason is insufficient, for Tosafos, s.v. Karkafsa, write that this applies specifically when one does not put on tefillin out of contempt; when he refrains because the mitzvah is debased in his eyes. Beis Yosef likewise writes in siman 37, in the name of Mordechai, siman 707, and Ran, 4a, s.v. Beinonim. Even according to Rosh, Hilchos Tefillin 28, cited there by Beis Yosef, it appears that one is not called a 'skull that did not put on tefillin' in this case, since he is purchasing another mitzvah. Nevertheless, since we do not find the designation 'skull' concerning tzitzis, but only concerning tefillin, there is evidence that tefillin are more stringent. Yet Beis Yosef there cites one interpretation under which tefillin is not meant specifically, and the same applies to all mitzvos. In any event, it appears to me that, as a matter of law, he should buy tefillin, because tzitzis is not an absolute obligation, as stated above in siman 24:1. Moreover, the poskim call tefillin an obligation upon one's person, whereas they do not call tzitzis such an obligation, as Abudraham writes on page 30. We rule below, at the end of siman 38, that tefillin precede mezuzah because an obligation upon one's person is superior. After Beis Hillel was printed, I found that in Yoreh Deah 285:2 he also rules this way, for a different reason: tefillin are sacred articles, while tzitzis are not sacred articles. The Yerushalmi, Peah 1:1, teaches that one is not obligated to go begging from door to door to purchase tefillin and tzitzis. Bach 1 writes that certain people in our time who own no tefillin and rely on friends to lend them tefillin after those friends have fulfilled their own obligation incur a great punishment.

[2] <b>He should put on tefillin, etc.</b> Sefer HaChinuch, Vaeschanan, mitzvah 421, writes: Because man is a material being, he is necessarily drawn after desire, for the nature of matter seeks every pleasure, like a horse or mule without understanding. Were it not for the soul with which God graced him, restraining him from sin to the extent of its strength, he would succumb. Because the soul dwells within his boundary (the earth) and is distant from its own boundary (the heavens) it cannot always overcome him, while his material force overcomes it. It therefore requires many guards: tefillin, tzitzis, mezuzah, and that words of Torah not cease from our mouths day or night. These serve to remind us so that we cease the oppression of our hands. Our Sages further said: Why is entry into the Land juxtaposed to the passage of tefillin? The Holy One, blessed is He, said: Perform this mitzvah, through whose merit you will enter the Land and merit life in the World to Come. They also said that if a person's transgressions and merits are evenly balanced and he bears the sin of neglecting tefillin, it tips the scale toward guilt. Baruch She'amar 26 writes: In the three verses containing the word 'letotafos' (one in Vehayah Ki Yeviacha, one in Shema, and one in Vehayah Im Shamoa) and the word 'lezikaron' written in Kadesh Li Chol Bechor, all the letters of the alef-beis appear except gimel and samech. This teaches that a gas ruach, an arrogant person, should not wear tefillin; rather, they are for one whose spirit is humble and who takes no credit for himself when he studies or performs a mitzvah. Sefer Chassidim 273 writes that if the tefillin are in their case, one should not shake them out but grasp them with his hand and remove them. I found it written in Emek Berachah 15 that it is proper to wash the place where tefillin are put before putting them on.<br>[2*] We learn in Berachos 14b: Anyone who recites Krias Shema without tefillin is as though he bears false testimony against himself. Levush writes that some explain this as false testimony concerning God. That is, he says in Krias Shema, 'If you will diligently heed,' and, 'I shall give rain,' yet he does not put on tefillin, while the Holy One, blessed is He, does give rain. Rashi indeed explains it as false testimony concerning God, but Levush's explanation of Rashi (that this is because he says, 'If you will diligently heed,' etc.) is strained. Moreover, the verse does not say 'all My mitzvos'; it says 'My mitzvos.' Would rain not be given merely because he fails to fulfill the mitzvah of tefillin? I saw that Maadanei Melech, page 13, chapter 2, siman 10, note 9, explains Rashi to mean that, had he truthfully testified that 'Hashem is our God, Hashem is One,' he would have fulfilled His mitzvos. Bach 2 inclines to this, but adds that by binding tefillin one testifies to His unity; if he does not put on tefillin, he appears to testify that He is not One. I wonder why they do not mention Levush's words. Tosafos, Berachos 14b, s.v. UManach, write: This means false testimony concerning the Master Who commanded him, for he recites, 'They shall be frontlets' and 'a sign upon your arm,' yet they are absent. This appears to mean that he testifies falsely concerning God by implying that He did not command him to put on tefillin. I found that Abudraham, page 30, explains Rashi this way as well. Apart from this, it is evident to me (with due respect) that Levush did not carefully examine Tosafos. What he writes, 'It appears to me that this is no difficulty,' etc., was already stated explicitly by Tosafos, and Abudraham there asks and answers the same question. Perhaps Levush found their position difficult because, according to it, the Gemara should have said that anyone who wears a four-cornered garment without tzitzis is as though he bears false testimony. To answer this, he may have concluded, 'People are not accustomed to scrutinize,' etc. That is, even if one wears a four-cornered garment without tzitzis, people do not ordinarily scrutinize it, so no falsehood is apparent. This is strained and requires further study. I presented these words to my master and father, the Rav, may his light shine. He said that there is further reason to wonder at Levush's understanding that the false testimony arises because rain comes: Sanhedrin 113a, in the incident involving Achav, implies that withholding rain applies specifically to idolatry. Maharsha there also explains that 'there will be no rain' relates to 'you will swiftly perish,' following 'when you will have grown old.' Moreover, Vehayah Im Shamoa was addressed to the community, as Rashi explains in Parashas Eikev. The condition concerning rain would therefore apply only when the community does not put on tefillin. I replied that this could perhaps be resolved by saying that people at large will err and will not derive these interpretations, just as Achav did not derive them; it therefore appears like false testimony. Nevertheless, I have already clarified Rashi's true meaning.

[3] <b>For one ascends in matters of sanctity, etc.</b> At the end of this se'if, Levush writes that some say tefillin precede because they possess greater sanctity. One must say that the two positions dispute this very rationale: whether the item of greater sanctity should be put on first, or later, because one ascends in sanctity. In truth, I do not know who the 'some say' whom he cites below are; Beis Yosef does not mention them. The rationale of ascending in sanctity appears in Likkutei Mahariv, Dinim VeHalachos 20. It appears to follow the mystical teaching that a person proceeds from a lesser sanctity to a greater sanctity, as stated in the Shulchan Aruch of Mahari Luria, Hilchos Tzitzis 20, as well as in Zohar and Makor Chaim 1. Megillah 21b similarly states that when the last reader reads four verses he is praiseworthy, because one ascends in sanctity and does not descend. Moreover, the principal reason for the 'some say' below is, as Levush concludes, that the mitzvah of tzitzis was deferred when he dressed because his hands were not clean. One cannot object that Levush himself writes in siman 8:11 that the garment can be donned without a berachah. Nevertheless, at the beginning of that siman he implies that, ideally, it is better to don it after washing. Levush asks why Shulchan Aruch needed to give a reason for tzitzis preceding tefillin: the obligation of tzitzis takes effect immediately when one dresses, while tefillin are not put on when naked; for this reason, he says, the authorities did not need to mention it. In my humble opinion, this poses no difficulty. We rule that tzitzis is not an obligation upon one's person; one may, if he wishes, make all his garments without four corners and tzitzis. Only during Krias Shema and prayer must even one who does not remain wrapped in tzitzis all day take care to wear them, as stated above in siman 24:1. Shulchan Aruch therefore needed to give these reasons. Regarding Levush's statement that the authorities did not need to mention it for this reason, see Eliyah Zuta 1.

This also appears to me from Responsa Menachem Azaryah 59, and from Magen Avraham 43, at the end of subparagraph 11. The Zohar, Vaeschanan, cited by Levush, begins: 'When he leaves the entrance of his house wearing tzitzis and tefillin, four holy angels join him, leave the entrance of his house with him, and escort him' (meaning that they escort him to the synagogue) 'and proclaim before him: Give honor to the image of the King!' It seems to me that Knesses HaGedolah did not examine the beginning of this Zohar passage; see there, and there is no need to elaborate. In truth, Makor Chaim's view is reasonable, since before the time for tzitzis arrives no obligation rests upon him at all. After Responsa Nachalas Shivah 6 was printed, I found that it accords with my words and states this in the name of Totzaos Chaim, paragraph 154.

[8] <b>On the arm, etc.</b> Another reason is that placement on the arm corresponds to the sense of touch, which depends upon the hand; it therefore has only one compartment. The four compartments upon the head correspond to the four senses located in the head: sight, hearing, smell, and taste. This is intended to arouse a person through all five senses to remember God's kindnesses and His unity, and that He watches over all His creatures. As an allusion to this, the right strap extends to the navel, indicating that the influence upon the lower world, by its Creator's command, extends to the navel of the world. This excludes the opinion of those who say that Divine providence reaches only as far as the sphere of the moon and no farther. We also find that the hand-tefillin commemorate the mighty hand, while the head-tefillin commemorate the Exodus from Egypt, which they saw with their eyes. [9] <b>To put on tefillin, etc.</b> We do not recite the berachah 'to bind,' although the verse says, 'You shall bind them,' because the essence of the mitzvah of tefillin is that they remain upon him all day. Were one to say 'to bind,' it would imply that tying them for a single moment and then removing them suffices. The Sages therefore instituted the wording 'to put on,' which implies that they remain upon him for at least some period of time. Thus writes Bach 5; see below, se'if 12. Makor Chaim, page 46b, writes that because the berachah 'to put on tefillin' also exempts the head-tefillin, they instituted the wording 'to put on,' which connotes a beginning, since he must still put on the head-tefillin. Lechem Chamudos writes that the word 'tefillin' should be pronounced emphatically, indicating its doubled form, for 'tefillin' is related to an expression of judgment. If one transgressed and put on the head-tefillin first, see siman 684, Eliyah Rabbah 8.

[10] <b>It is proper always to say, 'Blessed is the name of the glory of His kingdom forever and ever,' etc.</b> Levush's omission of this practice appears to follow Beis Yosef, who writes: It does not seem proper to me for a person to bring himself into a possible berachah in vain and rely upon saying, 'Blessed is the name of the glory of His kingdom forever and ever.' Lechem Chamudos writes that Rama was not concerned with Beis Yosef's words: since our practice is to recite both berachos, it is proper, merely as an added precaution, to say 'Blessed is the name,' not because we place ourselves in any doubt of a berachah. Malbushei Yom Tov 2 likewise writes in the name of Derashos Maharash 171 that we are not uncertain; we consider Rabbeinu Tam's view definite. Nevertheless, Levush appears to hold that one should not say 'Blessed is the name,' lest a harmful error result and people mistakenly recite another doubtful berachah in reliance on saying 'Blessed is the name.' The general practice, however, follows Rama. I saw Olas Tamid 7 go even further, writing: It is better to intend not to fulfill the obligation through the berachah 'to put on tefillin'; the berachah recited over the head-tefillin will then not be in vain, as stated in Responsa Menachem Azaryah 102. This is astonishing. We rule that if one spoke between the two tefillin, or if he has only the head-tefillin, he recites two berachos. If he intends not to discharge the head-tefillin through 'to put on tefillin,' he will have to recite that berachah again over the head-tefillin. To the contrary, he must intend that 'to put on tefillin' apply also to the head-tefillin, for it was instituted for the head-tefillin as well, as Levush explains. Responsa Menachem Azaryah affords no proof, because he holds that only one berachah is recited over both tefillin, and that if one spoke between them he recites only 'concerning the mitzvah of tefillin.' For us, who recite two berachos, his reasoning is inapplicable. Rama's words are therefore primary. Lechem Chamudos explains that Rama's phrase, 'It is proper always to say it after the second berachah,' includes even one who spoke between the two tefillin. According to Rashi, he does not repeat 'to put on tefillin,' while according to our practice he repeats that berachah as well. Because one may not interrupt between the two berachos by saying 'Blessed is the name,' Rama says that he always says it after the second berachah, even in this case. When he spoke between them, however, he must intend when saying 'Blessed is the name' that it relate to the repeated berachah 'to put on tefillin.' It appears that the same law applies when he has only the head-tefillin.

[12] <b>With a kamatz, etc.</b> Matzas Shimurim, page 64, in the name of HaMaarich in Sefer HaAruch, writes that Shulchan Aruch erred in ruling that 'lehaniach' is pronounced with a kamatz, for that form denotes rest. Here it should be pronounced with a patach and a dagesh, meaning to place tefillin, like the statement concerning the Chanukah lamp: 'It is a mitzvah to place it at the entrance of his house.' It is astonishing that he had the temerity to dispute Shulchan Aruch, whose ruling comes from Agur 37. He also failed to see Levush's words, which reject this position from Scriptural verses. The Talmudic wording affords no proof; perhaps the Talmud's text concerning Chanukah should also be vocalized with a kamatz. Tashbetz Katan 271 likewise accords with Shulchan Aruch. He also overlooked Radak's words in Sefer HaShorashim, where these appear under two separate roots: the form with a kamatz under the root nuach, and the form with a patach under the root yanach; see there. Further proof comes from the verses, 'Efrayim is joined to idols; leave him,' 'Now leave Me,' 'I shall leave him upon his land,' and 'to leave them in the wilderness,' all of which denote leaving. I also saw that Olas Tamid 9 writes: Levush and Lechem Chamudos disagree with Shulchan Aruch, and their words appear correct; that the berachah should be pronounced with a patach. This is exceedingly astonishing, for Levush, to the contrary, upholds the pronunciation with a kamatz, as does Lechem Chamudos, page 94, and Malbushei Yom Tov 4. Their objection concerns only Beis Yosef's statement that there is no difference in meaning; on that point they write that there is a great difference, and that it must specifically be pronounced with a kamatz. This is the primary ruling. I found in Piskei Tosafos, Menachos 131: 'He should recite lehaniach softly, and not lehaniach with a dagesh.' This evidently means that he should pronounce it with a kamatz, for the patach form has a dagesh. Lechem Chamudos 55 writes that in the berachah 'al mitzvas tefillin,' the vav of 'mitzvas' may be pronounced with a cholam or with a patach; one may choose. I am accustomed to pronounce it with a patach, because the two tefillin are considered a single unit. Shiyurei Knesses HaGedolah, Hagahos HaTur 2, writes that this is the universal practice; Or Chadash, Berachos HaTefillin, rules likewise, unlike Taz 10. If one has only the head-tefillin, all agree that the vav is pronounced with a patach.

[13] <b>After placing it upon the biceps, etc.</b> Bach 6 likewise writes that one should not begin the berachah until after placing the hand-tefillin upon the biceps, before fastening it. For the head-tefillin, he should not recite the berachah until after placing it around his head, before tightening it upon his head. This is also the tradition I received.
