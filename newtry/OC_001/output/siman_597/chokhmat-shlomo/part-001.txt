<b>Seif 1.</b> They eat, drink, and rejoice, and one does not fast on Rosh Hashanah, etc. Marginal note: See the Beis Yosef and Taz, who brought two opinions about this. In my humble opinion there is proof from the Talmud that this depends on a dispute of Tannaim. It is from what is written in tractate Taanis, chapter 3: that city fasts and cries out with the shofar, and all its surroundings fast but do not cry out; Rabbi Akiva says, they cry out but do not fast. The Tosafos Yom Tov brought in the name of the Yerushalmi that it explains their dispute: the first Tanna compares it to Yom Kippur, when they fast but do not cry out, and Rabbi Akiva compares it to Rosh Hashanah, when they cry out but do not fast. End quote. If so, it is explicit in the Yerushalmi that on Rosh Hashanah there is no mitzvah to fast, for this is Rabbi Akiva's reason, that he learns from Rosh Hashanah that they cry out but do not fast. But in truth one must understand the reason of the first Tanna: why does one master compare it to Yom Kippur and the other compare it to Rosh Hashanah, and what reasoning do they dispute? It therefore appears that in truth everyone agrees that it would be more fitting to compare it to Rosh Hashanah than to Yom Kippur. But the first Tanna holds that on Rosh Hashanah there is a mitzvah to fast; if so, on Rosh Hashanah they fast and cry out, and we have not found crying out without fasting. The reverse, fasting without crying out, we do find on Yom Kippur, and therefore he holds that they fast but do not cry out. But Rabbi Akiva holds that there is no mitzvah to fast on Rosh Hashanah; if so, one finds crying out without fasting, and therefore he holds that they cry out but do not fast. One can further say that according to the first Tanna there is a kal vachomer that it is a mitzvah to fast on Rosh Hashanah. For according to the first Tanna, crying out is less weighty than fasting, since he holds that they fast but do not cry out. If so, there is a kal vachomer for Rosh Hashanah: if in a place where they do not cry out, they fast, in a place where they do cry out, namely Rosh Hashanah, all the more so they fast. Therefore there is a kal vachomer that on Rosh Hashanah one should fast. Conversely, according to Rabbi Akiva one cannot learn a kal vachomer and say: since according to him fasting is less weighty than crying out, for he holds that they cry out but do not fast, let us learn a kal vachomer that if in a place where they do not fast, they cry out, then in a place where they fast, namely Yom Kippur, all the more so they should cry out, and we should cry out on Yom Kippur. This is not so, for regarding Yom Kippur, since the Torah states explicitly that only on the Yom Kippur of Yovel do they blow, it is clear that on an ordinary Yom Kippur they do not blow. But according to the Rabbis there is no explicit verse that one does not fast on Rosh Hashanah. Therefore one may properly learn a kal vachomer: if in a place where they do not cry out, they fast, then in a place where they cry out, is it not logical that they fast? Therefore, according to the first Tanna there is an obligation to fast on Rosh Hashanah, but according to Rabbi Akiva there is no obligation to fast on Rosh Hashanah. Since we rule there like the first Tanna, it is more reasonable to follow the view of those who say it is a mitzvah to fast on Rosh Hashanah. So it appears to me in my humble opinion; examine this carefully. Some bring proof from the verses of Ezra and from Elisha that there is no obligation to fast on Rosh Hashanah. In my opinion there is no proof from this at all, for it is known that a fast stands in place of an offering. Therefore, certainly in the time of the Temple, when we had the Beis HaMikdash and actual offerings, there was no obligation to fast; according to Torah law there is neither an obligation to fast nor a prohibition. On the contrary, by reasoning it was proper to eat and rejoice, as Ezra said. But for us, when there is no Beis HaMikdash and we have no offerings, one may say it is a mitzvah to fast so that the fast should stand for us in place of an offering, and all the above proofs are rejected. With this it appears one can explain what is written in Koheles: Rejoice, young man, in your youth, and let your heart gladden you in the days of your youth, and know that for all these God will bring you into judgment. This refers to Rosh Hashanah, for only through joy and trust do they become similar to Him, may He be blessed, and fit for Him to enter into judgment with them. We will explain what is written in Midrash Emor, that when the Holy One, blessed is He, ascends and sits on the throne of judgment, He ascends in judgment. Sometimes, when Israel are not worthy, the Holy One, blessed is He, as it were lowers Himself below and judges Israel below, as stated, I am with the crushed one. But if they are worthy, then the Holy One, blessed is He, raises them upward, in accordance with the statement of the other Tanna, He comes to the crushed one. With this the words of the Talmud in Rosh Hashanah 26 are understood properly, where the Talmud says: Regarding what do they disagree? One master holds: on Rosh Hashanah, the more a person bends his mind, the better, and on Yom Kippur, the more a person straightens his mind, the better. The other master holds: on Rosh Hashanah, the more a person straightens his mind, the better, and on fast days, the more a person bends his mind, the better. At first glance there is no understanding of what reasoning they dispute. It appears to say as follows. Rashi explained that the more a person bends his mind, the better, because it is written, My eyes and My heart will be there all the days. Regarding the more a person straightens his mind, Rashi explained because it is written, Let us lift our hearts to our hands. In Yevamos, and it is brought in the Sefer Turei Even, it says there that one authority says one should direct his eyes downward, as it is written, My eyes and My heart will be there all the days, and one authority says one who prays must have his heart above, as it is written, Let us lift our hearts to our hands, etc. Thus it is explicit that from the verse My eyes and My heart it is proper to look downward, and from the verse Let us lift our hearts to our hands it is proper to look upward. With this we understand the matter. We have already written that without trust we would not be fit for Him, may He be blessed, to enter into judgment with us. If He did enter, it would be only kindness, as the Baal Akeidah says, and then He, may He be blessed, lowers Himself, as it were, below. But if they have trust in Him, may He be blessed, they become like Him, and then they are fit for Him to enter into judgment with them, and then He, may He be blessed, raises them upward. In every matter there must be an act and a proof; thought does not suffice. One must bring everything from potential into actuality. Something like this applied to the tests of Avraham Avinu: although everything is revealed before Him, may He be blessed, nevertheless the Holy One, blessed is He, wanted to bring the matter from potential into actuality. If so, here too regarding trust, it is not enough to trust in the heart; one must bring the trust from potential into actuality. Therefore, if they eat and drink on Rosh Hashanah, this indicates the greatness of their trust in their hearts, as Ezra said, Eat rich foods, etc., for the joy of Hashem is your strength, as I wrote there in my explanation. But if they fast on Rosh Hashanah, there is no joy and no allusion to trust. On the Yom Kippur of Yovel, however, although they fast, since then they return the fields and their servants go free, and there is no greater monetary loss than this, if they did not have trust in Him, may He be blessed, they would be concerned for their monetary loss. It must be that they trust in Him, may He be blessed, and this too is an allusion to trust. With this the entire sugya is well understood. The one who says that on Rosh Hashanah, the more a person bends his mind the better, means that they fast on Rosh Hashanah, and there is no remembrance of trust; consequently they are not fit to enter judgment except through kindness, as the Baal Akeidah says. If so, the Holy One, blessed is He, lowers Himself below, and he holds like the view I am with the crushed one. Therefore he can say that the more a person bends his mind the better, corresponding to the verse My eyes and My heart will be there all the days, that He lowers Himself, as it were, below. But on the Yom Kippur of Yovel, where there is great trust in the very act of sending everyone free, they are fit for Him to enter judgment with them, and then He raises them upward. He holds like the view He comes to the crushed one. Therefore he says that on Yom Kippur, the more a person straightens his mind the better, corresponding to the verse Let us lift our hearts to our hands, which indicates directing one's eyes upward, as written in Yevamos above. The other authority holds that it is a mitzvah to eat and rejoice on Rosh Hashanah. If so, since there is an action in practice that indicates trust, they are fit for Him to enter into judgment with them. Consequently he holds like the view He comes to the crushed one, and therefore he holds that the more a person straightens his mind the better. But on other fast days, where there is no freeing of servants and fields, and also no joy, and there is no allusion at all to trust, the judgment is certainly only through kindness, as the Akeidah says; therefore, the more a person bends his mind the better. This is well; understand it, for I have been brief. I am astonished at those who hold that it is a mitzvah to fast on Rosh Hashanah from the Mishnah in Chullin, chapter 5, folio 83, brought in tractate Avodah Zarah 5b, that on these occasions one must notify, I sold the mother today, I sold the daughter today, and it counts Erev Rosh Hashanah among them. If so, it is proven that on Rosh Hashanah there is a mitzvah to increase eating like other Yamim Tovim, and it is even greater than Shabbos, for Shabbos is not counted there. This proves that on Rosh Hashanah there is a mitzvah to increase eating more than on Shabbos. But according to what the Magen Avraham wrote here, that at night it is certainly forbidden to fast, one can say that both are true: at night there is a mitzvah to increase the meal, as on every Yom Tov, and by day there is a mitzvah to fast; and this is well. I have another proof from Tosafos in the third chapter of Shevuos that it is permitted to fast on Rosh Hashanah. It is from what they wrote there, folio 20b, s.v. Dechi Lo Nadar, where they challenged Rashi, who erased the text concluding, Since it is rabbinic, etc., for if it were D'Oraisa the vow would not take effect, since vows do not take effect to fulfill a mitzvah; see there. This is difficult, for Rashi's words are still correct. We say in tractate Rosh Hashanah that when there is no persecution, if they want they fast and if they want they do not fast. If so, even if he vowed at a time when there was persecution, nevertheless, since he vowed generally to fast on the day Gedalyah was killed, it includes forever, and the vow takes effect at a time when there is no persecution, when if they want they do not fast. If so, since it takes effect for a time when there is no persecution, it should also take effect for a time when there is persecution, and it would properly be a vowed matter. Why would the Talmud need to say, Since it is rabbinic? This requires analysis at first glance. It must be that their intent is as follows. Since in truth Gedalyah was killed on Rosh Hashanah, only his fast was postponed to a weekday, if one vowed like the day Gedalyah was killed, his intent is not the fast day but Rosh Hashanah itself, on which he was killed. What the Gemara initially thought, that it is not a vowed matter, was because it is a mitzvah to fast on Rosh Hashanah. Certainly there is no Torah mitzvah in it, only rabbinic; but inclusion does not apply here, since it is always a mitzvah to fast on Rosh Hashanah. Therefore they wrote well that if it were D'Oraisa the vow would not take effect. Examine this carefully. The later authorities bring proof that it is forbidden to fast on Rosh Hashanah from the verse in Nechemiah, Eat delicacies and drink sweet drinks and send portions to one for whom nothing is prepared. In my humble opinion this is not a proof; on the contrary, from there there will be proof the other way. It is difficult: why did he first mention their eating and afterward say, send portions to one for whom nothing is prepared? He should have said the reverse: send portions to one for whom nothing is prepared, and eat delicacies. Rather, it appears that from this there will be proof that it is a mitzvah to fast on Rosh Hashanah. The mussar books wrote that if one eats and in the middle of his eating interrupts from his eating, this is better than a fast. According to this, in Yoreh Deah, siman 119, it states that if one is suspected of selling forbidden items, if he sent a gift to someone's house, one may eat it, for there is a presumption that he sends him from what he himself eats; see there, for it is the way of one who sends food to his friend to send him what he himself eats. Therefore Ezra told them that in this manner they are permitted to eat: if first they eat and afterward take from their food and send portions, this is considered as though they fasted. This is what Ezra said to them: in this manner, Eat delicacies and drink sweet drinks, namely, and send portions, for from what you took for eating you should send portions to one for whom nothing is prepared, and this is like a fast. This implies that the reverse is forbidden. If so, it proves, on the contrary, that it is a mitzvah to fast on Rosh Hashanah. However, it appears to me there is a clear proof that it is forbidden to fast on Rosh Hashanah. For if it would enter your mind that it is permitted to fast on Rosh Hashanah, I have a great difficulty from the Talmud and from Choshen Mishpat, siman 199: on four occasions Chazal upheld their words like Torah law, that money effects acquisition, and one of them is Erev Rosh Hashanah. If it would enter your mind that it is a mitzvah to fast on Rosh Hashanah, why did Chazal enact there that money effects acquisition? One cannot say it is because of the nights, for regarding Shabbos they did not enact so, even though there is a mitzvah of delight. It must be that since the verse does not write joy regarding Shabbos, they did not enact it, but regarding Yom Tov, since it is written, You shall rejoice on your chag, and there is no joy except with meat, they enacted it. If it would enter your mind that Rosh Hashanah is not included in You shall rejoice on your chag, why did they enact more than for Shabbos? It must be that You shall rejoice applies also to Rosh Hashanah; if so, the day too is included like every chag. This is a clear proof in my opinion; examine it carefully. Another proof may be brought that it is forbidden to fast on Rosh Hashanah from the second chapter of Beitzah, where they dispute the reason for eruv tavshilin. One says it is so that he will choose a nice portion for Shabbos and a nice portion for Yom Tov, and they learned it from the verse, Remember the Shabbos day: remember it from the one that comes to make it forgotten. This is difficult, for the primary law of eruv tavshilin should be learned from the verse in Ezra, Send portions to one for whom nothing is prepared, meaning to one who did not make an eruv tavshilin. If so, if we say that it is a mitzvah to fast on Rosh Hashanah, there it was Rosh Hashanah; how does it belong to say that he should choose a nice portion for Shabbos and a nice portion for Yom Tov? On Rosh Hashanah there would be no obligation of joy at all. If so, why should it be worse than any Shabbos, for which they made no enactment? It must be that one is obligated to eat and drink on Rosh Hashanah, and therefore an eruv tavshilin is needed. But if eating on Rosh Hashanah were optional, then it has no superiority over a weekday, and why would I need an eruv tavshilin from Rosh Hashanah to Shabbos? One cannot say that this is only according to the one who gives the reason so that he will choose, but according to the one who gives the reason so that people will say: if one may not bake from Yom Tov to Shabbos, all the more so not from Yom Tov to a weekday, according to him one may say it is a mitzvah to fast on Rosh Hashanah. This is not so, for if so, why did the Gemara not state this difference between them: that according to the one who says it is so that he will choose, it is a mitzvah to eat on Rosh Hashanah, and according to the one who says it is so that people will say, it is a mitzvah to fast on Rosh Hashanah? It must be that they do not dispute this. Since according to the one who says it is so that he will choose, it is necessarily proven that it is a mitzvah to eat on Rosh Hashanah, it follows that even according to the one who says it is so that people will say, it is so. It is possible to explain the sequence of the verse in Ezra this way: Go, eat rich foods and drink sweet drinks, and send portions to one for whom nothing is prepared. Chazal said this means to one who did not make an eruv tavshilin. What connection does one have with the other? The intent is that they thought it was a mitzvah to fast on Rosh Hashanah, and consequently they did not make an eruv tavshilin, since the reason so that he will choose a nice portion for Shabbos and a nice portion for Yom Tov did not apply. Ezra said to them: Go, eat rich foods, and informed them that it is a mitzvah to eat on Rosh Hashanah. If so, the reason so that he will choose a nice portion for Shabbos and a nice portion for Yom Tov applies, and consequently an eruv tavshilin is required. Therefore, send portions to one for whom nothing is prepared; and this is well. Examine it carefully. I have written at length about this and brought proofs for both sides. Let the wise person hear and add learning.
