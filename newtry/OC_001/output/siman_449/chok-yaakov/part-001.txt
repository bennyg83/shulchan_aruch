**** ENGLISH ****
[245] <b>A Jewish-owned shop.</b> The Rif, the Rambam, the Rosh, and the other poskim omitted these laws entirely. The Raavad wrote in Tamim De'im, siman 245, in his glosses on the Baal HaMaor, that he omitted this law because there are variant readings, and we rule stringently. This is his language. In truth, however, the opposite is reasonable to say: since these poskim omitted it and did not teach us a prohibition, this indicates that they hold that one may permit it because of the doubt, like every doubt in a rabbinic law. Therefore they were not concerned to write it, since it is permitted. By contrast, if there were any trace of prohibition, they should have mentioned it and warned about the prohibition. In the Bach it sounds that even with regard to a doubtful case of chametz that remained over Pesach, one should forbid eating it and permit benefit from it, and Magen Avraham ruled likewise. He wrote that this is also the implication of Tosafos and Rashi in Eruvin 64; see there. In my humble opinion, however, it is as I wrote, and from the words of Tosafos and Rashi there is no proof at all. On the contrary, their words appear to show the opposite. For Magen Avraham seems to have understood Tosafos' intent as was stated in Retzuf Ahavah, siman 31, in order to reconcile their words, but in truth his words are very forced in explaining Tosafos' intent here. The Maharsha there also did not fulfill the obligation of explaining Tosafos' words. In my humble opinion, Tosafos' intent is clear and simple: to reconcile the Talmudic passage regarding one who found chametz after Pesach, where it permitted benefit from it but forbade eating it. This indicates that we follow the majority of passersby, who are non-Jews. For otherwise, if it were only a doubt whether it belonged to a Jew or to a non-Jew, he should have permitted even eating it. And one should not say that since its permissibility comes only from the possibility that perhaps it belongs to a non-Jew, it is therefore properly forbidden to eat, because it is bread of non-Jews, as Tosafos wrote there, and that in fact we do not follow the passersby. About this Tosafos wrote that nevertheless he should have permitted it even for eating, since there is room to doubt that it belongs to a Jew and was made after Pesach. Rather, it is certain that we follow the majority of passersby. It is therefore explicitly proven from Tosafos' intent that in a doubtful case of chametz that remained over Pesach, one may permit even eating it. There is no contradiction from what Tosafos wrote there in the following passage, "and we have learned, etc.," that if they held that chametz of a non-Jew after Pesach is forbidden for benefit, he should have forbidden it because of doubt, lest it was made on Pesach; this is their language. This is no contradiction at all to what they wrote at first, for Tosafos' intent is as follows: if you would say that chametz of a non-Jew after Pesach is forbidden, then the reason of a penalty would not apply to them at all, and perforce I would have had to say that they hold like Rabbi Yehudah, that even after Pesach one transgresses a negative commandment with chametz that remained over Pesach, and that it is biblical, as stated in chapter Kol Sha'ah, and unlike R. Acha bar Yaakov. If so, it would be a biblical doubt and would be treated stringently. But according to the truth, since we hold that chametz that remained over Pesach is rabbinic, we follow the lenient side in a doubt. This also appears to be the view of the Maharsha there in his Chiddushei Tosafos, that one should be lenient in a doubtful case even regarding eating, although he greatly forces the explanation there in reconciling Tosafos' words on this point. Retzuf Ahavah also veered from our received rule, and the rule in our hands is that a rabbinic doubt is treated leniently. According to what I have written, everything is settled. This is also somewhat implied by the Talmudic passage in Chullin 4, according to the initial assumption of the Gemara there regarding the chametz of transgressors, that one should be lenient in this doubt as in every rabbinic doubt; see Tosafos there. We have therefore obtained the law from this passage: when chametz is found on the road immediately after Pesach, if most of the passersby are Jews it is forbidden; but if it is half and half, and certainly if most are non-Jews, it is permitted even for eating for one who is not careful regarding their bread. The same applies to every doubtful case of chametz that remained over Pesach, as above. This is the main view. Examine this carefully.
