<b> אם קצץ וכו'</b> -  עיין במ"ב מה שכתבנו דבזמה"ז כלים לכובס וכו' כ"כ מ"א וא"ר אך סתם המגן אברהם דבריו מאד ולא פירש אם ר"ל שלא התנה כלל וכדעת הגמ"ר מפני שאין עושין בחנם א"כ למה התנה ע"ז בזמה"ז וגם למה סיים דאטו בכל פעם יקצוץ עמו ואם נפרש דבפעם ראשונה קצץ עמו וקמ"ל דדי בזה וכמו שביאר התו"ש אכתי לא יתפרש מה שהתנה ע"ז בזה"ז וע"כ נראה שר"ל שהבטיח ליתן לו שכר אך שלא פירש כמה יתן ומה שהקשה בנ"א דהא קיי"ל ברמ"ז דלא בעינן קציצה בהדיא נ"ל דעד כאן לא שרינן ברמ"ז שלא פירש כמה יתן לו אלא בהבטיחו שיתן לו שכרו כראוי או שיתפשר עמו [וכן מוכח בתשובת הרשב"א שהביאו ב"י בסימן רמ"ז ע"ש וזהו שכתבו בש"ע שם ס"ב שיתן לו שכרו היינו כראוי לו וכן משמע בח"א אך שסתר עצמו קצת בדבריו במ"ש או שהדבר ידוע שלעולם וכו'] ולכך סמכא דעתיה דא"י וסבר שיתן לו שכרו משלם ודינו כקצץ אבל אם אמר לו הישראל סתם שיתן לו שכר אין דינו כקצץ דהא אין הא"י יודע כמה יתן לו ושמא יתן לו דבר מועט ולכן כתב המגן אברהם דבזמן הזה שכלים לכובס קצבתן ידוע ולא יסבור הא"י שיתן לו דבר מועט לכן אפילו אם אמר לו סתם שיתן לו שכר ג"כ סגי והוי כמו שקצב לו בפעם אחד ששוב בודאי נוכל לסמוך ע"ז תמיד וזהו שסיים דאטו בכל פעם וכו' ומה שכתב עיין ב"י בשם הגמ"ר ר"ל שהוא הקיל ביותר מזה ואולי דכונת המגן אברהם לענין כביסה לפי שקצבתן ידוע לכל אפילו אם לא התנה עמו כלל סגי שסמך שיתן לו כפי הקצבה הידועה [משא"כ בעורות לעבדן שאין ע"ז קצבה ידועה לכן אף שאין עושין בחנם לא סמכא דעתיה דא"י וסבר שיתן לו דבר מועט ושלא כדברי הגמ"ר דמקיל גם בעורות לעבדן] וצ"ע למעשה. ודע דאף שהח"א בכלל ג' דין ג' העתיק לדינא דעת הגמ"ר דכל דבר הידוע שלעולם נותנין שכר על מלאכה זו דינו כקצץ קשה מאד לסמוך ע"ז למעשה דהרי כל הראשונים [והם הרמב"ם והתוס' בדף י"ט ע"א והרא"ש והר"ן והרשב"א בחדושיו והמ"מ והאו"ז והמאירי] כולם כתבו בהדיא בכל מלאכות ואף בעורות לעבדן וכלים לכובס דבודאי אין עושין בחנם ואפ"ה בעינן קצץ וע"כ הטעם כמו שכתבנו למעלה דמפני שאין הא"י יודע כמה יתן לו ושמא יתן לו דבר מועט ע"כ לא סמכא דעתיה לעשות בשביל שכרו ועושה אדעת ישראל ומחזי כשלוחו וע"כ הקיל המגן אברהם אלא בכלים לכובס שהיה בזמנו הקצבה ידוע:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>If he fixed a price, etc.</b> See the Mishnah Berurah regarding what we wrote that nowadays garments given to a launderer are different. This is stated by the Magen Avraham and Eliyah Rabbah, but the Magen Avraham expressed himself very briefly and did not explain whether he meant that no stipulation at all was made, following the Hagahot Mordechai, because such work is not done gratis. If so, why did he make this dependent on present-day practice, and why did he conclude, “Must one fix the price with him every time?” If we explain that on the first occasion he fixed the price and the novelty is that this suffices thereafter, as the Tosefet Shabbos explains, it still does not explain why he made the ruling dependent on present-day practice. It therefore appears that he means that the Jew promised to pay him but did not specify how much. As to the Nishmat Adam’s objection that in siman 247 we rule that an express fixed price is unnecessary, it appears that there we permit an unspecified amount only where the Jew promised to pay the proper wage or to reach an agreed settlement with him. This is also evident from the responsum of the Rashba cited by the Beit Yosef in siman 247. Therefore the non-Jew relies upon receiving full payment, and the case is treated as one in which a price was fixed. But if the Jew merely said that he would pay him, without more, this is not treated as a fixed-price arrangement, for the non-Jew does not know how much he will receive and perhaps will receive only a small amount. The Magen Avraham therefore wrote that nowadays, when the customary rate for laundering garments is known, even a general promise to pay suffices, for the non-Jew will not assume that he will receive only a token amount. It is then equivalent to having fixed the price once, upon which one may certainly rely thereafter. His reference to the Hagahot Mordechai may mean that with laundering, whose rate is universally known, even no express stipulation may suffice, because the non-Jew relies on receiving the established rate. This differs from tanning hides, for which there is no fixed known rate. Practical application nevertheless requires study. Although the Chayei Adam rules like the Hagahot Mordechai that any work universally known to be paid is treated as fixed-price work, it is very difficult to rely on this in practice. All the early authorities explicitly require a fixed price even for tanning hides and laundering garments, although such work is certainly not done without pay. The reason is as stated above: since the non-Jew does not know how much he will receive, he may expect only a small amount and therefore works on the Jew’s behalf, appearing as his agent. The Magen Avraham was lenient only with laundering in his time, because its rate was known.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>For then one may be lenient.</b> Eliyah Rabbah questions this ruling, for here the Rama appears to rely on the Mechaber’s adoption of the Ran’s view that, where a price was fixed, we do not prohibit benefit from work done on Shabbos, whereas in siman 276:1 the Rama records the same ruling without dissent. The Magen Avraham distinguishes between the two cases, as explained in the Mishnah Berurah. Yet it remains difficult why the Rama there omitted the rationale of the Shiltei Giborim and instead cited the rationale of benefiting from the work, regarding which he is lenient in pressing circumstances where a price was fixed. It is also difficult, according to the Magen Avraham’s distinction, why the Mishnah prohibits use of a ramp or water drawn by a non-Jew. The latter clause appears to address even a fixed-price arrangement, like the earlier case of a lamp, yet those cases resemble wearing a garment, because the non-Jew intends only to complete his own undertaking. Perhaps the Mishnah concerns a case in which it is evident that the work is being done for the Jew, which the Peri Megadim states is prohibited regarding wearing the garment according to all opinions. The Meiri similarly explains the position of the Geonim, who permit wearing garments given to a launderer or hides given to a tanner where a price was fixed, yet prohibit use of a ramp or lamp.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Unripe grapes and ears of grain.</b> The Magen Avraham asks why the Tur omitted garlic, which is mentioned in the Gemara. He answers that squeezing garlic does not incur a sin-offering even if it was not crushed before Shabbos, because Torah liability for squeezing applies only to olives and grapes; therefore the Sages did not prohibit liquid that emerges from garlic. The garlic mentioned in siman 321:19 concerns completing its grinding by hand. This is difficult according to Tosafot’s first explanation, which understands the dispute of Rabbi Yishmael and Rabbi Akiva as concerning whether one may allow the squeezing to finish and then eat it on Shabbos, for there garlic that had been crushed is explicitly mentioned. Machatzis HaShekel already raises this objection. The Gra expressly writes that the same rule applies to crushed garlic. The reason appears to be that although squeezing garlic is not prohibited by Torah law, liquid that emerges remains prohibited if the garlic was not crushed beforehand. Tosafot’s statement that wherever there is no liability to a sin-offering the liquid is permitted refers to a case where, by Torah law, the squeezing process was already completed before Shabbos; there is then no decree lest one squeeze. But where squeezing did not begin at all before Shabbos, the Sages did not distinguish between produce whose squeezing is biblically prohibited and produce whose squeezing is only rabbinically prohibited. Strawberries and pomegranates are likewise only rabbinically prohibited to squeeze, yet their liquid is prohibited when they were designated for juice. When the Sages prohibited liquid emerging from olives and grapes, they included anything somewhat intended for squeezing. Garlic in this context is comparable to strawberries and pomegranates, and the same applies to ears of grain.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Because of the production of sound.</b> The Magen Avraham suggests that placing wheat into a watermill on Shabbos may not incur a sin-offering, because the grinding then occurs automatically, and he cites Tosafot. All the later authorities reject his proof, and no support for it appears in the Semag, Semak, or Rambam. They agree with the Magen Avraham’s own proof from trapping: one who spreads a net and the animal enters and is caught at that moment is liable although the result follows automatically. The same is true of baking, where the bread is brought to the fire and the fire thereafter completes the process. So too, although the grinding is completed automatically, the person is liable. This discussion concerns a case where the water-gate is already open and he places wheat into the hopper, so that grinding begins immediately. If other grain is already beneath it, whether adding more creates Torah liability requires study. If, however, he opens the water-gate on Shabbos, it appears that even the Magen Avraham would agree that he is liable, for grinding begins immediately and his act directly causes the waterwheel and millstones to move. This is comparable to turning the millstones with his own force, for the first water released through his act certainly grinds at least the measure of a dried fig. The Tiferet Yisrael writes that with a windmill there is no sin-offering according to all opinions because the warning is uncertain, since the wind may stop. This is unconvincing, for even if the wind stops immediately, the millstones’ existing momentum will certainly grind at least the requisite measure.
