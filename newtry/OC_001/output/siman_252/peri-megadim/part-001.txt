**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And one who stirs</b> - See Taz, siman 318, seif katan 22 and 23. Either the wool or the dyes require extensive cooking, which appears to be the Mechaber's view here and will, with God's help, be explained in Magen Avraham seif katan 2, or the liability is for dyeing. The Rosh, first chapter of Shabbos 18b, cites Rashi's explanation that stirring is cooking. In the chapter Kirah, 40b, section 11, he gives two explanations: stirring constitutes cooking, for although there is no cooking after cooking, dyes always require further cooking; alternatively, stirring constitutes dyeing because it drives the dye into the wool. Thus the Taz's statement in siman 318, seif katan 22, that wool requires much cooking is not precise; rather, the dyes require much cooking and always remain subject to cooking after cooking. A practical difference arises when one stirs a vat of dye containing no wool. This will, with God's help, be explained in siman 318 and here in Magen Avraham seif katan 2. Since I have reached this matter, I will record several details. Rashi on Shabbos 18b, s.v. 'one who stirs,' writes that regarding cooked food stirring is cooking. The Rosh understands this as liability for cooking, but the Bach infers from the phrase 'and regarding cooked food' that only food involves cooking, whereas here liability is for dyeing, as Tosafot, s.v. 'lest he stir,' and Maharshal explain. Rashi's phrase can mean that stirring cooks only while the contents are in a first vessel and hot enough that the hand recoils; in a second vessel, or below that temperature, stirring does not cook, in accordance with the Rosh. According to the Bach, where cooking does not apply dyeing likewise would not apply, but I will explain below that even in a second vessel stirring may drive the dye into the wool. See Yoreh Deah 69, Shach seif katan 37 and 38, that vinegar and brine may cook even in a second vessel. Accordingly, those who insulate meat, onions, and sharp vinegar and pour them into a second vessel may not stir while it is hot enough that the hand recoils, unless fully cooked. If fully cooked, stirring is permitted as stated in siman 318, seif 18; below that temperature one may be lenient. According to the Rama in siman 318, the same rule applies in such a case even when fully cooked in a second vessel.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Now</b> Rambam, Hilchos Shabbos 3:11, writes that one may not insert a ladle into a pot on the fire because this stirs it; stirring is among the needs of cooking, so he is considered to cook on Shabbos. In chapter 9, law 4, he states that one who stirs cooks; in chapter 21, law 13, he permits completing the crushing of porridge after removing the pot from the fire. See Maggid Mishneh, Kesef Mishneh, and Lechem Mishneh to chapter 3. After careful consideration, Lechem Mishneh understands Maggid Mishneh to mean that inserting the ladle is not itself stirring, but was decreed against lest one stir and turn the food. With fully cooked food, where placing it on the fire is rabbinically forbidden, inserting the ladle is permitted, though actual stirring remains rabbinically forbidden. If not fully cooked, stirring incurs a sin-offering, and inserting the ladle is forbidden lest he stir. Rambam records this here rather than in chapter 9 because he derives it from wool in a dye-vat, where we fear stirring, as Maggid Mishneh writes. Although there stirring constitutes dyeing, the cases resemble one another: just as there we fear stirring, here too one ordinarily turns the ladle, so removing and inserting it are forbidden. All this concerns a pot on the fire. Once removed, even in a first vessel hot enough that the hand recoils, stirring does not advance cooking; a ladle is permitted. This explains why Tosafos, Shabbos 18b, s.v. dilma, spoke of dyeing rather than cooking: the baraisa can concern a vessel removed from the fire, where stirring does not cook even in a first vessel. It also explains chapter 21, where one may turn the porridge after removal. Although actual stirring of fully cooked food on the fire is at least rabbinically forbidden according to Rambam, once removed it has no effect. Kesef Mishneh, however, understands that everyone agrees stirring a first vessel removed from the fire, while hot enough that the hand recoils, advances cooking. Tosafos spoke of dyeing because dyes are ordinarily cooked before wool is inserted; since Beis Hillel permits insertion even before the wool absorbed the color, cooking cannot be the concern, because there is no cooking after cooking in the dyes and cooking does not apply to wool. This is Rosh's second explanation in Kirah 40b, section 11. Ran likewise derives from here that stirring cooks, as indicated by Ramach's objection cited by Kesef Mishneh. Rambam's ruling in chapter 21 remains difficult: if stirring fully cooked food on the fire is at least rabbinically forbidden, why permit it after removal? Chapter 9 indeed implies that one who cooks fully cooked food over fire is exempt but prohibited, and distinctions may exist between cooled and hot food; nevertheless, Kesef Mishneh's unqualified statement is difficult. With God's help, the Mechaber's ruling will be explained in siman 253, Magen Avraham 22 and 32, and siman 318:4 and 15.

<b>ועדיין</b> פש גבן לברורי כי שלש טעמים יש ביורה עקורה מגיס אחד בישול כפירש״י וכרא״ש פרק כירה מ״ם ב׳ סי״א בב״ח סימן (הוא שי״ח) דסמנים לעולם צריכין בישול ולפ״ז דוקא בכ״ר ויס״ב השני תי׳ הב׳ ברא״ש צובע כשמגיס נכנסו הסמנים בצמר ויש לכ״א לכאורה קולא וחומרא לתי׳ הא׳ קולא בכ״ש או אין יס״ב חומרא אף קלט העי״ן אסור כי הסמנים צריכין לעולם בישול ולתירוץ הב׳ קולא בקלט העי״ן מבע״י ל״ש צובע תו וחומרא אף בכ״ש י״ל מגיס צובע. ואמנם טעם השלישי למדתי מר״ן ז״ל לשבת שכן דרך הצובעים להגיס שלא יחרוך וכ״מ בירושלמי ומשמע מטעם צביעה הוא כי היא מלאכת הצביעה שלא יחרוך הצמר הילכך אף שקלט העי״ן אסור אבל בכ״ש י״ל ל״ש שמחריך ושרי אפשר אף בלא קלט העי״ן באופן שיש לכ״א מהפירושים קולא וחומרא ויבואר אי״ה במ״א אות ב׳:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>It still remains</b> to clarify that there are three possible grounds for prohibiting stirring a dye-vat removed from the fire. First, cooking, as Rashi and the Rosh in Kirah 40b, section 11, and the Bach in siman 318 explain, because dyes always require further cooking. On this view, the prohibition applies only in a first vessel hot enough that the hand recoils. Second, the Rosh's alternative explanation, dyeing: stirring drives the dye into the wool. Each explanation yields both a leniency and a stringency. Under the first, stirring a second vessel or contents below that temperature is lenient, but even after the wool has absorbed its color stirring remains forbidden because the dyes still require cooking. Under the second, once the wool absorbed the color before nightfall further dyeing does not apply, but stirring may constitute dyeing even in a second vessel. I learned a third reason from Ran on Shabbos: dyers ordinarily stir so that the wool not scorch, as also appears from the Yerushalmi. This seems part of the labor of dyeing, for preventing scorching is integral to the dyeing process. Accordingly, stirring remains forbidden even after the wool has absorbed the color, but in a second vessel, where scorching is not a concern, it may be permitted even before full absorption. Thus each explanation carries a distinct leniency and stringency. This will, with God's help, be explained in Magen Avraham seif katan 2.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>From where</b> do the Sages derive that stirring promotes cooking even in a first vessel removed from the fire? Ran derives it from the concern that one may stir the dye-vat even after it has been removed; the rule that a first vessel cooks after removal is itself learned from the Mishnah concerning a pan and pot removed while boiling on Shabbos. According to the explanation that stirring constitutes dyeing, however, there is no proof. One may answer that no textual derivation is needed: reason itself dictates that a first vessel continues cooking after removal, and stirring is an act of cooking whether or not the vessel remains on the fire. Ran is nevertheless difficult, because he first writes that we learn the rule from here and then attributes the prohibition to dyeing, which would remove the proof. The Yerushalmi likewise requires study, and the distinguished commentator on the Yerushalmi already addressed its derivation; see there. The Yerushalmi's own words remain difficult to me. My sins have prevented me from attaining a clear understanding of all this. With God's help, more will be explained in Magen Avraham seif katan 2.

<b>והעמדת</b> זיג״ר בשבת משמע כאן וד״מ דאסור בשבת ואפ״ה שרי להשמיע דיודעים דמאתמול העמידו וברחיים אסור שיאמרו היום נתנו החיטים ברחיים ועסי׳ של״ח ס״ג והרב כ״כ אף למאן דמחמיר ברחיים. ובטלטול מותר וכן אותן זכוכית שמניח בתוכו מול וקורין שעה מותר להפכו בשבת להוריק זה מזה דלאו מידי עביד ומוקצה לא הוה דיחדו לכך הוה כלי. לצורך גופו ומקומו עסי׳ ש״ח בזה ואם מותר לומר לעכו״ם להעמיד בשבת צ״ע דאפשר יש בו מלאכה ד״ת ושבות דשבות התירו במקום מצוה לידע זמן ק״ש ותפילה אבל ד״ת לא. ואם תיקן עכו״ם אותו בשבת בשביל ישראל אפשר דאסור ליהנות לשמוע ולידע השעה כמו נר שהודלק בשביל ישראל אע״ג דלית ביה ממש בשמיעת קול: ועשו״ת פנים מאירות בזה ואי״ה בשל״ח אבאר בזה וכאן אין להאריך ובש״ח סי״א אי״ה יבואר ופ״מ קכ״ג בזה ושל״ח:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Setting up</b> a clock on Shabbos appears here and in Darkei Moshe to be forbidden. Nevertheless, allowing it to sound is permitted because everyone knows it was set the previous day. A mill is different, since people may say the wheat was put in today. See siman 338:3; Rema writes this even according to the stringent view regarding mills. The clock may be moved. Likewise, a glass timepiece containing sand, called an hourglass, may be inverted on Shabbos so the sand empties from one side to the other, because no prohibited act is performed. It is not muktzeh, since it was designated for this use and is a utensil permitted for use of its body or place; see siman 308. Whether one may instruct a gentile to set it on Shabbos requires examination. It may involve Torah-prohibited labor; a double rabbinic restriction was permitted for a mitzvah, such as knowing the times for Krias Shema and prayer, but not Torah labor. If a gentile repaired it on Shabbos for a Jew, benefit from listening and learning the time may be forbidden like a lamp lit for a Jew, although sound has no tangible substance. See Responsa Panim Meiros on this matter and, with God's help, the discussion in siman 338. There is no room to elaborate here. It will also be explained in siman 308:11 and Responsa Panim Meiros 123.
