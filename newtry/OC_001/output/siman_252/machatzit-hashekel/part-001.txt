<b>Seif katan 2: Because of cooking, implying that if it is fully cooked it is permitted, as stated in siman 318.</b> The Tosefet Shabbat objects that this is difficult, since in siman 318 the Mechaber explicitly permits stirring a pot only when its contents are fully cooked, whereas wool in a dye-vat remains forbidden even after the dye has fully cooked. The Rosh gives two explanations: dyes require prolonged cooking, or stirring further drives the color into the wool. The Tosefet Shabbat therefore understands the Tur and Mechaber to have adopted the first explanation and to prohibit stirring even after complete cooking, and he also questions why the Magen Avraham in siman 318 attributes the prohibition to dyeing when the Mechaber here states cooking. In my humble opinion, however, the Magen Avraham's words can be sustained. The Beit Yosef explains that the Tur, when ruling that food remains subject to cooking until fully cooked even after reaching the stage of ma'achal ben Derusai, did not rely on the Rosh's unsupported distinction. The Rosh had said that before ma'achal ben Derusai one who advances cooking is liable, but once it has reached that stage liability arises only if it cooled and was reheated. He then offered two strained answers to the Gemara's question concerning stirring a dye-vat: either dyes always require further cooking, or stirring constitutes dyeing. Since the Tur rejected those strained answers and ruled that ordinary food also remains subject to cooking until fully cooked, he evidently saw no essential distinction between food and dyes. Accordingly, the Mechaber, who follows the Tur in siman 318, may likewise hold that once the dye is fully cooked there is no further concern of cooking, and therefore the Magen Avraham correctly inferred here that the decree lest one stir applies only before complete cooking. When the Magen Avraham adds that according to Tosafot and the Rosh it nevertheless remains prohibited because of dyeing, he refers to their distinct approach, under which stirring may deepen the color even after cooking is complete. He chose the language of dyeing because Tosafot state that reason explicitly, whereas the cooking explanation appears only in the Rosh. The Mechaber's later ruling in siman 318, that one may not stir wool in the vat even after it is fully cooked, can still be reconciled. The Beit Yosef argues that with respect to cooking there is no middle category: if the act constitutes cooking, there is Torah liability; if it does not, why should it be prohibited? That reasoning does not necessarily apply to dyeing. Once the wool is fully dyed, further stirring may no longer incur a sin-offering for dyeing, yet it can remain rabbinically prohibited because it improves and repairs the wool. This resembles the Rambam's ruling, cited by the Magen Avraham in siman 340, that writing ink over red pigment is liable, while passing ink over existing ink is exempt but prohibited, because it brightens and improves the letters. Likewise, the Magen Avraham explains in siman 328 that placing a cloth over a bleeding wound is prohibited even though the resulting color is destructive; an already red cloth is certainly prohibited because the blood intensifies its color and thereby repairs it. Thus all the rulings are consistent. Here, where the vessel is not sealed and the only concern is a decree lest one stir, the case must concern dye not yet fully cooked. Once it is fully cooked there is no Torah prohibition of cooking, and any additional improvement of the color is only rabbinic; prohibiting the unsealed vessel on account of a possible rabbinic act would be a decree upon a decree. In siman 318, however, actually stirring the wool remains rabbinically prohibited even after complete cooking because it improves the dyeing. This also explains why the Magen Avraham there gives the reason of dyeing rather than cooking.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Seif katan 4: One should be stringent, for in any event an observer assumes, etc.</b> The Magen Avraham gives this reason according to the Rama, but the Mordechai himself gives another reason for stringency, as stated in seif katan 5. The leniency for lending applies when there is a need, the city is walled, and the non-Jew lives within the city, so no one can say that the Jew instructed him to carry into a prohibited domain, since no prohibition of carrying applies there. Although one might still suspect that people will say the Jew sold it to him on Shabbat, as the Rambam explains, in a case of need we rely on the Beit Yosef, who did not entertain that suspicion and was concerned only that people might think the Jew instructed him to carry it out. According to the Beit Yosef, therefore, it is permitted in a walled city. A sale, however, should be treated stringently even in a case of need, because perhaps even the Beit Yosef agrees that people may say the Jew sold it on Shabbat. With a loan, observers suspect only what is in fact true, namely that the article is on loan. With a sale, even if people initially assume it was lent, when they later investigate and learn that it was sold they may conclude that their original assumption was mistaken and suspect that the sale itself occurred on Shabbat.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Seif katan 5: To be stringent, etc.</b> The non-Jew has not yet acquired it. The issue depends on the dispute among the authorities whether a non-Jew acquires through meshichah or through payment. The Mordechai may hold that a non-Jew acquires specifically through meshichah and not through money. Since the article has not yet been removed from the Jew's premises, even a Jew would not acquire it, because meshichah performed in the seller's domain does not effect acquisition, as explained in Choshen Mishpat. If, however, a place was designated for the non-Jew, that place is treated as his courtyard and his courtyard acquires for him. I nevertheless do not understand this, since acquisition by a courtyard is derived from agency, and we rule that there is no agency for a non-Jew. I have already discussed this in the laws of Pesach, siman 448; see there.

**** ENGLISH ****
<b>And also chametz, etc.</b> Although we rule concerning chametz that rental effects acquisition, and according to Rabbeinu Tam a Jew who accepts responsibility for a non-Jew's chametz does not violate bal yera'eh when the chametz remains in the non-Jew's house, the same being true of a genuine rental, we do not follow Rabbeinu Tam's further view that chametz belonging to a non-Jew may remain in a Jew's house merely because a place was designated for it even when the Jew accepted responsibility. Only an actual rental suffices; see the Magen Avraham at the beginning of siman 440. The ruling in siman 347 likewise rejects the Mordechai's proof that there is no concern of appearance that people will say the Jew instructed the non-Jew to carry it out. The Mordechai relied on the Mishnah's ruling that when the poor person extends his hand inside, the householder is exempt and permitted. The Magen Avraham rejects this independently: the Rosh asks why the householder should be entirely permitted, since there is a prohibition of placing a stumbling block before the blind. Even where that prohibition does not technically apply, it is no less serious than a minor eating forbidden food, whom beit din must restrain. The Rosh therefore explains that the householder did commit a prohibition; 'exempt and permitted' means only that he committed no prohibited act of carrying. The same response defeats the Mordechai's proof. We may indeed be concerned that observers will say the Jew instructed the non-Jew, and in the case of the poor person the householder may likewise violate a prohibition because of appearance, although he is permitted with respect to the intrinsic labor of transferring domains.

<b>Seif katan 7: For a favor, etc.</b> This means gratuitously. If the non-Jew initiated the matter and asked the Jew to permit him to do a favor and perform the labor for him, all agree that it is permitted. If the Jew initiated and asked the non-Jew to perform the labor, it is prohibited according to the Rama. According to the Beit Yosef, as the Rama understands him in siman 247, it is permitted even when the Jew initiated. See the Magen Avraham there, who writes that the present passage likewise supports the Rama's understanding. The Beit Yosef states without qualification that work performed as a favor is permitted and does not limit this to a case where the non-Jew initiated. This implies that he permits it in all circumstances, even when the Jew initiated. The Rama's statement here, 'some disagree if he does it for him gratuitously,' therefore refers specifically to a case where the Jew initiated.
