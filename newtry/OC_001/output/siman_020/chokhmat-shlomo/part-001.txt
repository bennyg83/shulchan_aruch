**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Seif 2.</b> One may not sell a fringed tallis to a non-Jew, etc. See Magen Avraham, who cites Rashi as explaining that the Gemara's concern regarding a prostitute is that she might say that a Jew gave it to her.

At first glance I found this difficult. Why did Rashi not explain that the concern is that the non-Jew might deceive the prostitute and claim to be Jewish? We rule that one suspected of a minor transgression is not presumed suspected of a more severe one. It is well known that relations with a non-Jew are regarded as more severe than relations with an unmarried Jewish woman. This is certainly so according to those who maintain that “You shall not intermarry with them” applies while they remain non-Jews, making the prohibition more severe according to the Torah. Even according to those who interpret that verse after conversion, in public perception relations with a non-Jew are certainly regarded as more severe than relations with a Jew. One suspected in people's eyes of a lesser offense is not presumed suspected in their eyes of the greater. It would therefore be appropriate to enact a safeguard to reduce the prohibition. If she is suspected of relations with a Jew, is every Jew suspected of listening to her? A non-Jew, however, is certainly suspected of promiscuity and may deceive her into thinking that he is Jewish, causing her to have relations with him.

I then examined Menachos 43a and saw that Rashi indeed explains this in his first interpretation, namely that she would violate “You shall not intermarry with them.” This proves that Rashi maintains that the verse applies while they remain non-Jews, consistent with his position elsewhere, as Beis Shmuel cites in Even HaEzer, siman 4; Rambam likewise holds this view. It is therefore surprising that Magen Avraham omitted Rashi's first interpretation.

This also enables us to understand Rambam, who omits the Gemara's reason involving a prostitute and records only Rabbi Yehudah's reason that perhaps the non-Jew will accompany a Jew. Beit Yosef notices this and answers with difficulty that the latter concern is more common and more severe. In my humble opinion the matter is readily explained.

The Mishnah in the third chapter of Demai teaches that one who gives produce to an innkeeper must tithe what he gives her, etc. Rabbi Yose says: “We are not responsible for deceivers.” Bava Kamma 69b explains the principle: “Feed it to the wicked person and let him die.” Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel likewise holds at the beginning of chapter 5 of Maaser Sheni that during the other years of the shemittah cycle we say, “Feed it to the wicked person and let him die.” Tosafos Yom Tov there notes that according to Rashi's explanation in the first chapter of Chullin, the first Tanna too accepts that principle, but holds that the innkeeper intends to help. The Yerushalmi, however, indicates that the first Tanna and Rabbi Yose disagree: according to the first Tanna we do not say, “Feed it to the wicked person and let him die,” while Rabbi Yose does say so.

Rambam follows Rashi's first interpretation that the concern involving a prostitute is that the non-Jew will present himself as Jewish and she will have relations with him. He found this difficult, because “we are not responsible for deceivers.” He therefore understood that the reason involving a prostitute accords with the first Tanna of Demai, who requires safeguards even for non-Jews. Rabbi Yehudah consequently gives another reason, that perhaps the non-Jew will accompany a Jew. His question is: the first reason may suffice according to the Sages, but according to Rabbi Yose, who says we are not responsible for deceivers, why should selling the tallis be forbidden? Rabbi Yehudah therefore introduces a different reason, namely that perhaps the non-Jew will accompany him. Since we rule like Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel that we are not responsible for deceivers, Rambam could not record the reason involving a prostitute, but only the second reason.

This also beautifully explains the Gemara's expression in Menachos, “Here they interpreted it because of a prostitute.” Why say, “Here they interpreted it”? According to Rashi, the first Tanna too accepts that we are not responsible for deceivers, and the innkeeper case is different only because she intends to help. The Yerushalmi, however, as Tosafos Yom Tov cites, maintains that the first Tanna does require safeguards for deceivers. Therefore, “here,” meaning in Eretz Yisrael, they interpreted the reason as involving a prostitute, because they held that the first Tanna requires a safeguard even for non-Jews. The Babylonian Gemara, however, follows Rashi that even the first Tanna accepts that we are not responsible for deceivers. It therefore could not accept the prostitute concern, and Rabbi Yehudah in Babylonia needed the alternative concern that perhaps the non-Jew will accompany him. This is very satisfactory.

Our explanation also resolves Peri Megadim's question here upon Rashi: in an isolated act of promiscuity there is certainly no Torah prohibition of “You shall not intermarry.” According to what I have written, Rashi's intent is that the Sages enacted a safeguard because perhaps, if she knows he is a non-Jew, she will abstain. Even if relations with a non-Jew are likewise only D'Rabbanan and she is already suspected of violating a D'Rabbanan prohibition, relations with a non-Jew are regarded by the public as more severe. In public perception, people understand “You shall not intermarry” to apply even to promiscuous relations, just as ordinary people misunderstand “You shall not covet” to apply even where payment is made. Thus there are cases in which the public imagines a prohibition to be according to the Torah even though it is not. Accordingly, in the eyes of the public she would violate “You shall not intermarry,” and she might not do so if she knew he was a non-Jew. If she does not know, she may have relations with him. Since it is possible to separate her from a prohibition, we establish a safeguard.

This also accords with what I wrote in my addenda to Yoreh Deah, siman 118, that if a person is wicked only with respect to a D'Rabbanan prohibition, we certainly establish a safeguard to prevent him from coming to violate a Torah prohibition. According to Rashi's explanation, relations with a non-Jew would be a Torah prohibition while relations with an unmarried Jewish woman would be only D'Rabbanan, and my words would appear contradicted. According to what I have written, however, relations with the non-Jew here are likewise only D'Rabbanan, but are regarded as more severe in public perception. The matter therefore depends upon the dispute between the Sages and Rabbi Yose, and Rambam's position is fully explained.

See also what I wrote on these matters in the second edition of my work on Yoreh Deah, siman 118, and in my work on Even HaEzer, section 44, in the responsum to the community of Mastyshch, Even HaEzer, siman 109. Examine this carefully.
