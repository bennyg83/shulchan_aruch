חלוקו עט״ז כפי׳ הב׳ בב״י על מה שכתב הטור לקמן ברל״ט ובש״ע ס״ב לענין פשיטת הכתונת: מ״ש אמאי לא אמר ר״י בלבישת חלוקו (שהיה מכניס ראשו בעודנו שוכב) הנה י״ל דה״א דרשאי לפשוט דרך ראשו ולהפך צד פנימי לחוץ קמ״ל דא״א שלא יתגלה מקצת גופו משא״כ לבישה פשיטא דאין לישב במטה בגלוי הגוף א״נ כמ״ש הדרישה דר״י תרתי קאמר דלא היה מהפכו דא״כ יצטרך להפוך אח״כ כשילבש גם קורת ביתו משום צניעות ותרתי אשמעינן בפשיטת לבושו: והוי יודע שהר״מ ז״ל פ״ה מהלכות דיעות ה״ו צניעות גדולה נהגו ת״ח לא יתגלו ראשם וגופם משמע שאר אדם מתגלים גופם וראשם ועיין לקמן דריב״ל לא סגי ד׳א בגילוי הראש למדות חסידות ומרבי יוסי דאמר לא ראו קורות ביתי ומאי רבותיה משמע ג״כ לחסידות ושאר כל אדם אין איסור ולמ״ש הדרישה י״ל ר״י תרתי קאמר דלא הפכו שיצטרך להפכו אח״כ הא שאר כל אדם אין מקפיד עט״ז אות ב׳ ועמ״ש לקמן בהא דלא ילך בקומה זקופה ג״כ כתב הר״מ ז״ל בה׳ דעות לת״ח יע״ש:

Taz, following Beit Yosef on Tur later siman 259 and Shulchan Aruch 2 about removing an undershirt: why did Rabbi Yochanan not speak when donning his robe (inserting his head while lying)? One can say when removing through the head he might turn the inner side outward — we learn one cannot avoid some exposure; not so donning — obviously one does not lie naked on the bed. Or per homiletics Rabbi Yochanan teaches two things: he did not turn inside-out lest he need to reverse again when dressing, and “my house beams” for modesty — two lessons in removing clothing. Note Rambam Hilchos De’os 5:6: great modesty — scholars do not uncover head and body, implying others may uncover head and body; see below Rabbi Yochanan on four cubos bareheaded for saintliness; Rabbi Yosi “beams” greatness is also saintliness; others have no prohibition. Per homiletics Rabbi Yochanan may teach two things: not turning so as not to need to reverse later, whereas others are not careful — Taz letter 2; on erect posture Maharal in Hilchos De’os for scholars, see there.

<b>ללובשו</b> עט״ז לדעתי כיון האדון ז״ל כוונה גדולה כמו שאגיד כי ידקדק כו׳ שלא יהפוך קשה טובא מה״ת יהפוך פנימי לחוץ ויתגנה שהוצרך להזהירו ואיזה דקדוק שייך בזה והול״ל ידקדק בחלוקו להפכו כדרכו כמ״ש ר׳ יוחנן המקפיד על חלוקו להפכו (הטור הוסיף מלת כדרכו) ולזה אמר בשלמא הטור הביא דר״י וכפירש״י לובשה כשהיא הפוכה וכמה בגדים טורח ומסיר ולובש כדרכו (ויצא לרש״י זה מרבי יוסי קי״ח שם דלא ראו קורות ביתי אמרי חלוקי ותרתי קאמר כמ״ש באות א׳ בשם הפרישה ולאו מדת חסידות לת״ח כ׳א לכ״ע א״כ ת״ח מעלה יתירה דלובשה כשהיא הפוכה מסירה) ומש״ה אמר מדקדק ללובשו כדרכו ולא אמר להופכו כדרכו דלא תטעה לומר הפי׳ לובשה מסירה כמימרא דרבי יוחנן ויהיה כפי׳ שלא יהפוך היינו בפשיטה לא יהפוך אחר שפשט לאיזו סיבה כי ישכח למחר וילבוש כך או שאר פירושים. משא״כ המחבר השמיט כאן מימרא דר׳ יוחנן הול״ל ידקדק בחלוקו להופכו כדרכו הן אמת בח״מ רס״ב סכ״א הביא המחבר תנאי ד׳ שהוא מדקדק בחלוקו להופכו ולא סיים כפירש״י לובשה כו׳ ומ״מ הרי רש״י סובר כן ועד״ר כאן ושם צ״ע דבריו במ״ש רק חלוק בטביעת עין:
**** ENGLISH ****
<b>“To wear [it] properly.”</b> Taz, in my view: our author had a deep subtlety, as follows. “Careful not to turn [inner outward]” is difficult — from the Torah’s wording one might turn inner to outer and look repulsive, hence the warning — but what exact care applies? It should have said “be careful with his robe to turn it to its proper side,” as Rabbi Yochanan was careful to turn his cloak (the Tur added “properly”). Therefore the Tur cited Rabbi Yochanan and, per Rashi, one “wears it reversed” and must remove several garments and wear properly (Rashi derives this from Rabbi Yosi Shabbat 118b: “They did not see my house beams — my cloak,” two points, as Perishah explains — not mere saintliness for a scholar alone but for everyone; a scholar has an extra stringency when wearing reversed that he removes it). Hence the Tur said “be careful to wear [it] properly,” not “to turn [it] properly,” lest one think the meaning is “remove when wearing reversed” like Rabbi Yochanan’s statement, or that “do not turn” means when undressing not to turn lest one forget and wear wrongly the next day, or other interpretations. By contrast, the Mechaber omitted here Rabbi Yochanan’s dict that should read “be careful with his robe to turn it to its proper side.” True, in Choshen Mishpat 182:21 the author cited the tenant who is careful to turn his cloak and did not finish per Rashi “wears it reversed”; still Rashi holds so — “see Orach Chayim” here and there — his words need study in what he wrote; only a distinction in outward appearance applies.

<b>He should put it on.</b> See Taz. In my humble state, I have several thoughts here, which I shall set out with Hashem’s help. There are five interpretations of this passage.

The first is the interpretation of Beit Yosef. Rav Yosef and Rav Ashi follow one approach, as Tosafos write in the passage beginning, “One who acts.” The baraisa and Rabbi Yochanan do not fundamentally disagree; rather, they differ over right and left, because each side possesses a particular distinction, one through the big toe and the other through tefillin. Rif, Rambam, and Rosh omitted both the baraisa and Rabbi Yochanan, because Rav Yosef, and also Rav Ashi who is later, maintain that one may honor whichever side he chooses, since each has importance. Tur, however, rules like Rav Nachman bar Yitzchak, because the baraisa supports him. It teaches that for putting on, washing, and anointing, the right precedes, while for removing, the left precedes. Why did the baraisa distinguish among these acts? It should have stated regarding all four that one may choose either right or left. Furthermore, had the baraisa ended before its final clause, it could have been reconciled with the explanations of Rav Yosef and Rav Ashi. Once the Gemara cites its conclusion, “He removes the left first,” it necessarily follows Rav Nachman bar Yitzchak. This also supports Beit Yosef’s explanation of Tur, that one removes the left shoe first out of honor for the left. Were it as Rashi explains, that this is out of honor for the right, the entire baraisa would be concerned with honoring the right, and it would remain difficult why Tur did not rule like Rav Yosef and Rav Ashi. Understand this.

Tur omitted Tosafos’ ruling concerning shoes that have no laces, and he also omitted the laws of removing, washing, and anointing. At first glance, with respect to shoes without laces he does not agree with Tosafos. In his view, only where both right and left can be honored must one honor both, namely by putting the shoe on the right foot first and tying the left shoe first. Where there is no tying, one may do whichever he wishes. Likewise, the statement that one removes the left shoe first is not necessarily exact. Its meaning is that putting on must begin with the right and tying with the left. Removing, however, cannot honor both sides, so he may remove the left first and thereby honor it, as Beit Yosef explains Tur. Yet this is difficult. Why did the baraisa not say that when removing, washing, and anointing, one may begin with the left? Beit Yosef therefore writes that this qualification applies specifically to washing, not to putting on. Understand this, and study Beit Yosef and Rashi carefully.

The second interpretation is that of Bach. In his view there are three opinions. Rav Yosef follows the explanation of Tosafos, that the baraisa and Rabbi Yochanan do not disagree. Nevertheless, one who consistently favors the right should always favor the right, and one who favors the left should always favor the left. Rabbi Yochanan objected to Rav Shemen’s conduct, etc. Rav Ashi, however, said that he observed Rav Kahana and saw that he was not particular at all, because in a discretionary matter no concept of honor applies. Rav Ashi therefore disagrees with both the baraisa and Rabbi Yochanan. The authorities who omitted these teachings ruled like Rav Ashi. This avoids Beit Yosef’s difficulty that they should have taught that one who once chose to favor a particular side should continue doing so. See Bach there. Tur, however, ruled like Rav Nachman bar Yitzchak.

The third is the interpretation of Eliyah Rabbah here, seif katan 2. According to him, Rav Nachman bar Yitzchak and Rav Ashi follow one approach, unlike Tosafos in the passage beginning, “And he concluded.” Rav Nachman bar Yitzchak explains that the baraisa requires one to put on the right shoe first and tie the left shoe first where it is possible to honor both sides. Where it is impossible to honor both, one may choose whichever side he wishes, because each side has a distinction. Rav Ashi then comes to explain Rav Nachman bar Yitzchak’s words: “I observed,” etc., meaning that Rav Kahana was not particular; sometimes he put on the right shoe and tied the left, and sometimes he put on the left shoe and tied the right. Study his words carefully. According to this, we gain that Tur ruled in accordance with the majority, Rav Nachman bar Yitzchak and Rav Ashi. He omitted shoes without laces because one may choose, and likewise one may choose which shoe to remove first. This accords with Rashi, that removing the left first honors the right, while the reverse is also permitted. The baraisa would mean that one puts on the right first, whereas in all the other cases one may choose the right because it too is important, and the same applies in reverse, similar to the rule that one who acts according to either master has acted properly. Nevertheless, when putting on and tying, he should put on the right and tie the left, or the reverse. According to this, however, it is difficult why Tur cited Rav Nachman bar Yitzchak rather than Rav Ashi, whose formulation would have been even more suitable.

The fourth interpretation, in my opinion, is as follows. Rabbi Yochanan said, “Like tefillin, so too shoes.” The comparative word “like” is otherwise unexplained and superfluous; he could simply have said, “Just as tefillin,” etc. Rather, the order of putting on tefillin is known: one puts on the arm tefillin on the left, and afterward the more important head tefillin, because of the significance of the word totafos. When removing them, one removes the head tefillin first for the same reason. Shoes follow precisely the same order. One puts the left shoe on first, because it is less important; otherwise, the right shoe would be treated as the weaker one. I discussed this at length in my novellae to Shabbos 61a. This is the approach of Baal HaMaor and other authorities, who maintain that Rabbi Yochanan follows Rav Huna concerning Shabbos, that the shoe is worn on the foot that has the wound. The Yerushalmi states this explicitly. As for what Rabbeinu Shimshon bar Avraham writes, citing Maseches Derech Eretz, that the latter clause of the mishnah says that one removes the left shoe first, the opening clause may be explained as follows: he puts on the right shoe without tying it, and then puts on and ties the left shoe. So long as he has not tied it, the right has not been treated as the weaker one; the difficulty arises only from the rule concerning removal. Understand this.

Accordingly, there is no need to say, as I wrote in my novellae there, that the Bavli and Yerushalmi disagree. Rav Yosef said that one who follows either master has acted properly because Rabbi Yochanan and the baraisa certainly disagree. Rif and Rambam therefore omitted both views, and Rav Ashi likewise maintained this approach. Rav Nachman bar Yitzchak stands alone in this matter, because the final clause of the baraisa concerning removal is difficult, as I have written. Nevertheless, we rule in siman 301 like Chiya bar Rav, and with Hashem’s help this will be explained further there.

We now come to the words of Taz, which constitute the fifth interpretation. He maintains that there is no dispute here at all. Rav Yosef and Rav Ashi hold that there is no required preference in cases such as shoes without laces or removal; one may begin with either the right or the left. Rav Nachman bar Yitzchak teaches that one who fears Heaven fulfills both considerations. Where the shoe has laces, it is proper to honor both right and left. The phrase “fulfills both” may be understood according to whichever explanation one adopts. Tur therefore omitted shoes without laces and the removal of shoes, etc. Rama, in his gloss, cited all these cases. Mechaber, however, cited the law of removal but omitted shoes without laces. This may be resolved as explained above, or Mechaber may hold that removing the left shoe first honors the left, and for that reason he omitted the case of shoes without laces. There is no need to elaborate further.

**** ENGLISH ****
Taz did not cross-reference siman 8 letter 3 where it says “like sitting at home” — today there is prohibition of “their practices,” and placing the hand on the head helps; likewise if he bows his head it is permitted when obvious he is unlike one walking four cubos bareheaded under the sky — hand on head does not help, see there. What he wrote “sitting in a wagon like walking” — hence hand does not help — Eliyah Rabba letter 4; I did not understand Taz letter 4: there if he cannot stand he sits and need not descend if travel delay is hard — he should intend only if possible to stand, for greater awe of the Divine Presence — how is that like walking? Hence if he can stop the wagon and reach his destination without painful delay he certainly should not stand in the wagon as if walking — see there.
