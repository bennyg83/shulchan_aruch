<b>But if he recited Borei Peri HaEtz, he fulfilled his obligation, for that appears to be the primary ruling.</b> This is indeed the clearly correct ruling, and I see no basis for ruling that one should not recite Borei Peri HaEtz. Mahari wrote in Terumas HaDeshen, siman 29, that since Rashi wrote that one recites Borei Peri HaEtz on terimah, meaning fruit that was pounded somewhat but not completely crushed, this implies that on fruit completely crushed one recites Shehakol. Beit Yosef already challenged this at the end of siman 204 from Rambam, who rules that even if one mashed the fruit and made it into a paste, one recites Borei Peri HaEtz. He also brought proof from the Tur in siman 204, in his dispute with his colleagues, that everyone agrees that where the substance of the fruit remains, the fruit itself is primary. A further proof is the Tur in siman 205, who rules that even when fruit is cut into very small pieces, it does not lose its blessing. Here too, the Tur explicitly writes that Rabbeinu Yonah agrees with Rambam on this point. The inference of Terumas HaDeshen from Rashi is therefore astonishing. On the contrary, the Gemara proves the reverse. Rav Asi said that dates of terumah may be made into terimah but may not be made into beer. If heavily crushed fruit did not retain the law of terimah, Rav Asi should have stated the greater novelty, that it is forbidden even while the substance of the fruit still remains, though it has been heavily crushed. Instead, he prohibited making beer, where the substance of the dates is entirely gone and only their taste remains, as Tosafos writes in Berachos 38a, s.v. hai devasha. This is a strong proof that whenever the substance of the fruit remains, it still retains the status of the fruit itself. Nor can one infer otherwise from Rashi. The Tur likewise writes that the dates were pounded somewhat, yet then rules in accordance with Rambam that if they were made into a paste one still recites Borei Peri HaEtz. Rashi's phrase, “pounded somewhat,” appears merely to explain the word terimah and why it is called by that name. He explains that it is pounded somewhat, but not entirely crushed. This resembles targis in Berachos 37a, which Rashi explains as grain cut into thirds; in Greek, three is called tri. Rashi therefore writes that the term terimah denotes anything pounded somewhat but not completely crushed. This is a general lexical explanation. The Gemara mentions terimah here because dates were commonly prepared in this manner, but the same law applies when they are crushed thoroughly. Furthermore, in Nedarim, siman 217, it is stated that one who vows not to eat dates may nevertheless eat date honey. This implies that pounded dates remain forbidden so long as their substance remains. The same applies here. Moreover, the discussion below states that according to the Rosh, even the water in which fruit was cooked requires Borei Peri HaEtz if it contains the fruit's taste. Although the Rosh writes “it is possible,” here, where the substance of the fruit remains, the ruling is unquestionably so. It therefore appears to me that one who initially recites Borei Peri HaEtz on plum or cherry preserves has no concern at all and has acted correctly, since Borei Peri HaEtz is a more specific blessing than Shehakol, as explained below. A further proof is seif 13, which states that spices ground and mixed with sugar retain the blessing appropriate to those spices. Thus, even when completely ground, they do not lose their blessing. We likewise practice every day that when apples are cooked until completely crushed, one recites Borei Peri HaEtz, as stated in seif 12 regarding cooked fruit. They are certainly crushed through cooking, and there is no concern of a blessing in vain. So it appears to me.

<b>Honey that flows from dates.</b> Beit Yosef cites the Rosh regarding the view of the Halachos Gedolos: “It is possible that the Gaon's reason is that Scripture says ‘honey’ and does not say ‘dates.’ The verse therefore refers to honey that flows by itself from dates, which is included among the seven species, and one recites Borei Peri HaEtz upon it. It differs from liquid extracted from pomegranates and apples, which emerges only through crushing and squeezing. Honey that flows naturally from dates is the honey mentioned in the Torah.” At first glance, this implies a distinction between date liquid that flows naturally and that which emerges through crushing or squeezing, and Beit Yosef understood it this way. He therefore wrote that the Tur deliberately states that even on honey flowing from dates one recites Shehakol, and all the more so when it emerges through crushing. This is astonishing, for crushing, and certainly squeezing, extracts more of the actual fruit than does natural seepage, which is merely moisture. Rather, the Rosh intended only to prove that the honey mentioned in the Torah is date honey, because dates naturally exude liquid, and Scripture describes the Land as flowing with milk and honey. Other fruits, such as figs and pomegranates, do not exude liquid naturally; their liquid emerges only through crushing or squeezing, which is not included in the term “flowing” used concerning honey. The Tur here mentioned the simpler case, namely natural seepage, while he discusses squeezing in siman 205. See what I wrote there in seif 3.

The Tur adds that it appears to him that one should recite Borei Peri HaAdamah upon them, like unripe grapes. Here, however, he rules in accordance with Maharam. It appears to me that there is a clear proof for this from Yevamos 80a, where Abaye states that an aylonis leaves minority directly into adulthood. According to Rabbi Meir, she is regarded during minority as though already grown, since she will never develop the usual signs. The same applies here. Since these grapes will remain as they are and will never ripen, their law is already that of their final state. They therefore do not possess the blessing of grapes at all, and only Shehakol applies, for they are destined to become vinegar.

<b>And the Rosh wrote that it is possible, etc.</b> The Rosh writes: “Although it contains only the broth and the flavor of the vegetables, one recites upon it the same blessing as upon the vegetables themselves. This is unlike fruit juice, which we call mere moisture, because that liquid does not contain the fruit's flavor. It is possible that if one cooked fruit and the fruit's flavor entered the water, one recites Borei Peri HaEtz upon it.” Beit Yosef concludes that the liquid in which vegetables were cooked has the same law as the vegetables, whether they were cooked in water or merely soaked in it, provided that they imparted flavor to the water. Since the Rosh uses the phrase “it is possible,” it would appear at first that he was uncertain, in which case one should recite Shehakol to avoid doubt. Yet the Tur states this ruling unequivocally in siman 205, that on water in which vegetables were cooked one recites the same blessing as upon the vegetables. It therefore appears that according to the Tur there is no doubt here. Although the Rosh writes “it is possible,” he did not mean to express uncertainty about the law itself. This may be explained from the approach of Tosafos, who write that the water of cooked beets has the same law as the beets and requires Borei Peri HaAdamah, even though it contains only water and vegetable flavor. Although we stated earlier that fruit juice is mere moisture, Tosafos writes that perhaps one may distinguish between them, but does not explain the distinction. The Rosh therefore writes that perhaps this is the distinction: ordinary fruit juice lacks the flavor of the fruit, whereas cooked water contains that flavor. The law itself is not in doubt, for the water of cooked vegetables certainly has the same law as the vegetables, meaning that water flavored by fruit has the same law as the fruit. In practice, one should recite Shehakol on liquids from foods not normally cooked or crushed, but Borei Peri HaEtz on liquids from fruits normally cooked. One should not object from what I wrote in this siman, that we follow the majority rather than the flavor, because here the other component is merely water and has no significance against the fruit's flavor. So it appears to me. See also what I wrote in siman 205, seif 2.
