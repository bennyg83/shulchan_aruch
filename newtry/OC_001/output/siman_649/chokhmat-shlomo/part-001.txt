**** ENGLISH ****
<b>Se'if 1.</b> All four species are invalid if robbed or stolen, etc. Note: in Responsa Mahari Mintz he was uncertain about one from whom a good esrog was stolen, whether the thief can discharge himself with an esrog inferior to it or not. See Responsa Chacham Tzvi and Mishneh LaMelech, what they wrote about this. Now at first glance it seemed possible to bring proof from what is written in Kesubos 99, where it asks about one who said to an agent, "Buy me a kor," and he went and bought him a half-kor, whether he is considered to have transgressed his instructions or to have added to his instructions. It challenges there from the Mishnah in Meilah: if he gave him a gold dinar to buy him a cloak, and he bought with three parts a cloak and with three parts a tallis; see there. This matter is astonishing, for one who examines the Mishnah there sees that it first taught a case where he told him to buy an esrog for a dinar, and he went and bought for half a dinar an esrog and for half a dinar a pomegranate, and both committed meilah. Why did it not challenge from that? Why did it leave the first clause and challenge from the latter clause? It is true that Tosafos in Meilah there wrote on the Mishnah that just as the Talmud explains the reason regarding the cloak, the same applies here. But the matter is astonishing: why did the Talmud in Kesubos point this challenge to the cloak and not to the first clause of the esrog? Therefore it appears that from the esrog there would be no proof, for one can say that it is speaking where he needs it only for the mitzvah. Therefore, for the mitzvah, he is not particular between a large esrog and a small one, so it is not considered a deviation from the owner's intent. But with an optional matter, it is properly considered a deviation. Therefore it proves from a cloak, which is optional. If so, it is proven that for a mitzvah one is not particular between large and small; and if so, the same applies where one stole an esrog: one may say that he can discharge himself with a small one. It is true that one can distinguish, for with robbery, since it has monetary value for sale, if he gives one smaller than it he has caused him monetary loss, as the Mishneh LaMelech and Chacham Tzvi wrote to reject the proofs of Mahari Mintz; see there. But in Meilah, since he returns the remaining money, and there is no monetary concern, only the body of the esrog, one can properly say that small and large are the same to him. In any event, there will be a practical difference where the robber wants to pay him money, and the robbed party wants him to give him only an esrog. Now on the festival we maintain that returning money does not help, but he must return the body of the esrog; and likewise with a gift on condition to return, where we maintain that on the festival returning money does not help, only the body of the esrog. Nevertheless, it appears that if he returns only a small esrog, he may return the excess money, and the owner cannot say that he specifically prefers to fulfill his mitzvah with a large esrog. For this is proven in the Talmud, that this is not a particularity, and it is sufficient for him to fulfill the mitzvah with a small esrog. From the side of the mitzvah, there is no particularity between small and large, and from the monetary side he can return the excess money. So it appears correct and clear in my humble opinion. See my work on Yoreh De'ah, Mahadura Tinyana, siman 144, in the laws of a Torah scroll, in a responsum to the province of Wallachia, what I wrote that is relevant to this law; see there and examine it. See Magen Avraham subparagraph 3, what he wrote. So too it appears in the Talmud, which says that it is not despair, etc. And see my work on Choshen Mishpat, Mahadura Tinyana, siman 71, in Choshen Mishpat siman 128, where we wrote to reject the proof: even according to those who hold it is valid for others, that means for one for whom the transgression was not performed; but for one for whom the transgression was performed, it is certainly invalid, as with one who nullifies a prohibition; see there. Now the view of the Yereim, brought in Magen Avraham, is known: even if robbing a gentile is permitted, nevertheless it is not called "for yourselves," and I already elaborated on this above. The Rav Rabbi Chaim Moshe, Av Beis Din of Praszka, asked me from tractate Bechoros 13b, where it asks according to the one who says robbery of a gentile is permitted: why do I need a verse to exclude overcharge? If robbery is permitted, all the more so overcharge. What does it ask? Perhaps it teaches us that with overcharge too it is considered "for yourselves." I answered that there is no difficulty at all, since it says there: "to your fellow" you return overcharge, and it does not say that you return the acquisition itself. It is proven that it is not speaking of an actual voided sale, but of a case where the sale takes effect, only it is a sale with a sixth overcharge and he returns the overcharge money. If so, this is fine. Granted, with a specific item such as an esrog or lulav, it is relevant to say that it is not "for yourselves." But with overcharge money, granted he is obligated to return it, nevertheless as long as he does not return it, it is not extant; how can it be relevant to say about something not extant that it is not "for yourselves"? Especially here, since the money of a Jew, as long as it has not reached the gentile's hand, has always been the Jew's, and automatically it is "for yourselves." How can it be relevant here to say that it is not "for yourselves"? Therefore his difficulty is no difficulty at all; examine it. Now Tosafos in Sukkah asked: why do we need a verse regarding sukkah, "for yourself," to exclude a stolen one? Let it be derived because it is a mitzvah that comes through a transgression. I already wrote above several ways to answer their difficulty. However, in my opinion I do not understand their difficulty. Granted, at the beginning of chapter Lulav HaGazul, where it is speaking of the second day of Yom Tov, since that is only a rabbinic mitzvah and it is not relevant to say that a positive commandment overrides a negative commandment, therefore since in any event it is a transgression, it is again relevant to say that it is a mitzvah that comes through a transgression. But with sukkah, which is Torah law, if not for the verse we would have said that a positive commandment overrides a negative commandment and it is not a transgression; only the verse revealed that a stolen one is invalid. What, then, was their difficulty? In this one may say that their difficulty is that with this it is not relevant to say a positive commandment overrides a negative commandment to steal another's money, for even with preservation of life, which overrides the whole Torah, nevertheless it is forbidden to save oneself with another's money. If so, all the more so a single positive commandment does not override taking another's money. It is an a fortiori argument: if preservation of life, which overrides the whole Torah, is pushed aside before another's money, all the more so a positive commandment, which is pushed aside before preservation of life, which itself is pushed aside before another's money. However, what they asked in Pesachim regarding matzah of tevel, why a verse is needed, let it be derived because it is a mitzvah that comes through a transgression, there one can properly say that if not for the verse, a positive commandment would override a negative commandment. With lulav too one can say this, with a practical difference regarding robbing a gentile. In my work Sefer HaChayim, we settled their difficulty according to those who hold robbing a gentile is permitted, only it is not called "for yourselves." But now I saw that even according to those who hold it is forbidden, it is still fine. For what is forbidden to save oneself with another's money is regarding a Jew, since with his body it is forbidden to save himself because of "who says your blood is redder," etc.; therefore the same applies to his money, for "damim" has two meanings. But with a gentile, since it is certainly permitted to save oneself with the life of the gentile, for "who says your blood is redder" does not apply here, if so it is also permitted to save oneself with his money. Therefore, granted that otherwise it is forbidden to rob, nevertheless one can say here that a positive commandment overrides a negative commandment. Therefore one needs to exclude robbery from a gentile, which is not a mitzvah that comes through a transgression, since a positive commandment would override the negative commandment and there would be no transgression. Examine this well. Now regarding what is explained in the Shulchan Aruch, that all four species are invalid if robbed or stolen, whether before despair or after despair, etc. Note: if he bought the esrog with money and did not pay him, whether this is robbery. According to the view of the Chelkas Mechokek, this is robbery; see Even HaEzer siman 120 and what I wrote in my novellae there. Now it appears to bring proof from the Midrash in parashas Emor, as follows: "And you shall take for yourselves on the first day"; Rabbi Chiya taught: "and you shall take" means by purchase and not by robbery; "for yourselves" means from your own, to exclude the stolen, end quote. This is astonishing: why are both needed? Therefore it appears that the intent is that what it says "by purchase and not by robbery" excludes that initially it should not come into his hand by way of robbery, but only by way of purchase. Afterward it teaches us that even if it was in his hand by way of purchase, if afterward he does not pay him, this too is a way of robbery. This is not excluded from the language "and you shall take," for at first it came permissibly into his hand by way of purchase. Therefore it excludes from the language "for yourselves," that in any event now that he does not pay, it is robbery and not yours, and he has not fulfilled. However, we do not derive law from Midrash; one can also say as I wrote long ago in my commentary in my novellae to Aggadah there. But it appears to bring proof for this from the Talmud in Sukkah 31b, in the incident of that old woman who was crying out: the Exilarch and all the Rabbis are sitting in a stolen sukkah. One reason is that there is a distinction between the beginning of acquisition and the completion of acquisition. The Rashba holds that items forbidden for benefit cannot be acquired only regarding something that until now was not in his domain at all, and now we want to bring it into his domain; in such a case, when it is forbidden for benefit, it does not become in his domain. But when it is already in his domain, such as with a robber, where previously too it was in his domain regarding liability for accidents, and despair acts only to complete the acquisition, then even when it is forbidden for benefit it also completes the acquisition. Know this, for in any case the view of the Rivash is that one can acquire items forbidden for benefit, and therefore it is preferable to minimize the dispute. One may say that even the Rashba said his rule only about something that was not yet in his domain at all; but regarding what was already in his domain, to complete the acquisition he agrees with the Rivash. A distinction similar to this is proven according to the one who says mere despair acquires. It is difficult: we maintain regarding a lost object that if it came into his hand before despair, he does not acquire it because it came into his hand through prohibition; why then does a robber acquire through despair, even though it came into his hand through prohibition? Perforce the reason is that with a lost object it was not in his domain at all from the beginning until now, and therefore he does not acquire it since it came into his hand through prohibition. But with a robber, since it was already somewhat in his domain regarding liability for accidents, the acquisition is completed even though it came into his hand through prohibition. Therefore the meat would not be its spoil, since it is forbidden for benefit, and it would also not be spoil of Heaven. What, then, is the challenge? I saw in the Peri Megadim, Mishbetzos subparagraph 8, in the name of the Eliyah Rabbah, that he asked from oil in which a mouse was found, which is repulsive for a mitzvah. There is no difficulty at all, for two reasons. First, there its body did not change, whereas here it changed, for a piece from which the mice ate was cut off. Furthermore, there the intent is that before Him, may He be blessed, it is repulsive, and before Heaven it is revealed that mice drank from it. But with lulav, where the intent of beauty is that it be beautiful in people's eyes, and in the eyes of a person it is like a finger pointing to the Creator to praise Him, anything whose repulsiveness is not from its body is no longer recognizable after removal, so it is not repulsive. We are not speaking of the disqualification of beauty that actually invalidates and forbids reciting a blessing over it; there we say that in this case it is valid. Rather, the concern that it is repulsive for the mitzvah is only an initial caution where another is possible, or where it is possible not to light it at all. But where he must fulfill the mitzvah, which is an obligation upon him, and there is no other, certainly one should not be particular about what is revealed before Heaven as repulsive. Examine it, and this is fine with Hashem's help. See Magen Avraham subparagraph 27, what he wrote in the name of Mahari, as follows: that which a deficient one is valid means that he takes some for eating and recites the blessing on the rest, for it is a remnant of purity, as we find regarding tzitzis, etc. Regarding how astonishing his words are in this, see my responsum to Yoreh De'ah in the laws of a Torah scroll, what I wrote about this; see there and examine it.
