<small>מג"א סק"ג </small>ואפ"ה לאחרים לא הוי מצוה הבאה בעבירה. תמוה לי כיון דהתם ייאוש קונה אתי לידו בהיתר מש"ה לא הוי מהבב"ע. אף דרש"י ס"ל דבעשה קנין קודם המצוה מקרי ג"כ מהב"ע. היינו להגזלן עצמו. אבל לאחר י"ל דהוי חילוק כיון בהיתר בא לו לא הוי מה"ב. וחשבתי לומר דכוונת המג"א. דס"ל מ"ש רש"י א"נ ייאוש קני הוי לי' מהב"ע אין פירושו די"ל דר"ה ס"ל דיאוש קני ומ"מ הצריך שהנכרי יקצצו משום מהב"ע. דא"כ אמאי נקיט וש"ר בידך. הא הקנין נגמר ביד הנכרי מדין ייאוש א"ו דר"ה ס"ל דייאוש ל"ק. אלא דרש"י הסביר דא"נ דהיה הדין ייאוש קונה. מ"מ הוי מהצורך דלגזזו נכרי משום מהב"ע. ולפי"ז יקשה דהא ר"ה ע"כ דל"ס מהב"ע דהא אף בגזזו נכרי. כיון דס' דייאוש לא קני. והאונכרי קני מדין ייאוש וש"ר. א"כ בא לידם באיסור. והו"ל מהב"ע אע"כ דלאחר אף שבא לידו לא הוי מהב"ע. א"כ הו"ה קודם ייאוש.
**** ENGLISH ****
<small>Magen Avraham subparagraph 3</small> And nevertheless, for others it is not a mitzvah that comes through a transgression. This is astonishing to me. Since there, if despair acquires, it came into his hand permissibly, and therefore it is not a mitzvah that comes through a transgression. Although Rashi holds that where one made an acquisition before the mitzvah, it is also called a mitzvah that comes through a transgression, that is for the robber himself. But regarding another person, one may say there is a distinction: since it came to him permissibly, it is not a mitzvah that comes through a transgression. I thought to say that the intent of the Magen Avraham is as follows: he holds that what Rashi wrote, "alternatively, if despair acquires, it would be a mitzvah that comes through a transgression," does not mean that one may say Rav Huna holds despair acquires, and nevertheless he required that the gentile cut it because of a mitzvah that comes through a transgression. For if so, why did he mention "and change of domain is in your hands"? The acquisition was completed in the gentile's hand by the law of despair. Rather, perforce, Rav Huna holds that despair does not acquire. But Rashi explained that even if the law were that despair acquires, nevertheless it would be necessary for the gentile to cut it because of a mitzvah that comes through a transgression. According to this, it is difficult: Rav Huna necessarily does not hold of mitzvah that comes through a transgression, for even where a gentile cut it, since he holds despair does not acquire and the gentile acquires through despair and change of domain, if so it came to their hands through prohibition, and it should be a mitzvah that comes through a transgression. Rather, perforce, for another person, even though it came into his hand through prohibition, it is not a mitzvah that comes through a transgression. If so, the same would apply before despair.

דנחלקו הפוסקים דייאוש ושינוי רשות אם במה דמשנה רשות קונה הגנב והוא קונה ומקנה לא הוי מהב"ע כיון דהראשון קנהו או דרק השני קנה והוי מהב"ע ודו"ק והנה מ"ש בכוונת המהרש"א דפרכת הש"ס דרך ממנ"פ עדיין יש להקשות דלמא ר"ה ס"ל יאוש ל"ק וגם ס"ל דין מהב"ע אלא דאצל הנכרי הוי ייאוש ושינוי רשות אצל האונכרי וכשיאגדנו אחר כך הוי קנין אחר קנין דייאוש וש"ר מש"ה לא הוי מהב"ע ויש ליישב:

<small>However,</small> it appears that one cannot explain this way. For according to Rashi, it is difficult: what does the Gemara ask, "let him acquire it with a physical change," since in any event it is a mitzvah that comes through a transgression? The Maharsha answered that since it was already acquired through despair, when he afterward makes an acquisition through physical change, it is not a mitzvah that comes through a transgression. If so, it is proven that he holds Rav Huna maintains despair acquires, and necessarily what he mentioned, "and change of domain is in your hands," is in truth not precise. And although Rashi wrote one approach, that Rav Huna holds despair does not acquire, this means that according to that side Rav Huna holds that we do not say mitzvah that comes through a transgression; and according to the other side, that Rav Huna holds despair acquires, Rav Huna holds of mitzvah that comes through a transgression. If so, the opposite is proven: Rashi's view is that even for others it is a mitzvah that comes through a transgression. For if not, the difficulty of the Maharsha remains in its place: what does it ask, "let him acquire it through physical change"? Perhaps Rav Huna holds despair does not acquire, and it is a mitzvah that comes through a transgression. Rather, perforce, even for another, where it came into his hand through prohibition, it is a mitzvah that comes through a transgression. If so, the Talmud's challenge is either way: if Rav Huna holds despair does not acquire, then they came into the hands of the gentile merchants through prohibition, proving that he does not hold of mitzvah that comes through a transgression; and if he holds despair acquires, when he afterward makes an acquisition through physical change, it is not a mitzvah that comes through a transgression, as in the answer of the Maharsha. At first glance, it is still difficult: what does it ask, when gentiles cut it, let there be despair in their hands? Perhaps Rav Huna holds despair does not acquire and also holds the law of mitzvah that comes through a transgression; therefore the gentile should cut it, and there will be change of domain in the hands of the gentile merchants, and it will come permissibly into the hands of the buyers and not be a mitzvah that comes through a transgression. But if the gentile merchants cut it and the change of domain is in the hands of the buyers, since it came into their hands through prohibition, it is a mitzvah that comes through a transgression. One must say that even according to the initial assumption, the gentile merchants themselves would also fulfill with it; rather, it asks that nevertheless those who sell should themselves cut it. It is proven that he does not hold of mitzvah that comes through a transgression from the fact that the gentile merchants themselves also fulfill with it. At first glance it appears that Tosafos were uncertain whether for another person it is called a mitzvah that comes through a transgression, from what they wrote in Gittin 55, s.v. "what," "however, if it had been acquired by him previously," etc.; "and it asks: let him acquire it through change of name," etc.; and it is forced to say that he holds like Rabbi Yitzchak, etc. At first glance this is astonishing: why did they take the proof from the questioner, and not from Rav Huna himself, for what does it help that gentiles cut it? In any event it would not be a mitzvah that comes through a transgression. Also, what they wrote, "it is forced to say that he holds like Rabbi Yitzchak," how can it be that it asks because he holds like Rabbi Yitzchak? From where can it challenge? Perhaps Rav Huna does not hold like Rabbi Yitzchak. It therefore appeared that they did not want to prove from Rav Huna, for one may say that for another there is no issue of mitzvah that comes through a transgression. Therefore they took the proof from the Talmud's challenge, "let him acquire it through change of name." About this they wrote that it is forced to say, meaning that one may say that for another it is also a mitzvah that comes through a transgression, and it is proven from Rav Huna that he holds like Rabbi Yitzchak, for it implies that he holds despair does not acquire, since he mentioned that change of domain is in your hands, as above. Therefore it properly asks: let him acquire it through change of name. However, it appears to me that this is not so. For if so, why did they ask simply that a verse is not needed for stolen lulav and esrog, since one can derive it because it is a mitzvah that comes through a transgression? One could say that the robber gave it to another, where there is no issue of mitzvah that comes through a transgression. Rather, it is certainly obvious to them that for another as well it is a mitzvah that comes through a transgression. Therefore it appears that their intent in Gittin was that they were uncertain regarding another person: even if despair does not acquire, nevertheless, since through despair it partially leaves the owner's domain, as Tosafos wrote in Bava Kamma 111, s.v. "he robbed," but when it came into his hand before despair, when it had not yet left the owner's domain, he is the actual robber and there is an issue of mitzvah that comes through a transgression. And although one could still say that the verse is needed where the robber, after despair, handed it to another by way of borrowing, where there is no mitzvah that comes through a transgression, yet it is invalid because "for yourselves" excludes stolen property, one can answer that the difficulty of Tosafos is at least according to the one who says despair acquires: why do I need a verse? One can also say that after despair as well, if it did not acquire, then for another person too it is a mitzvah that comes through a transgression, since it came into his hand through prohibition. Rather, they were uncertain regarding what the authorities disagree about in despair and change of domain: whether, through the change of domain, the thief acquires it and then he acquires and transfers it, in which case it is not a mitzvah that comes through a transgression because the first one acquired it; or whether only the second one acquires it, in which case it is a mitzvah that comes through a transgression. Examine this. Now, regarding what I wrote in the intent of the Maharsha, that the Talmud's challenge is by way of either way, one can still ask: perhaps Rav Huna holds despair does not acquire and also holds the law of mitzvah that comes through a transgression, but with the gentile, there is despair and change of domain in the hands of the gentile merchant, and when he later binds it, this is an acquisition after acquisition of despair and change of domain; therefore it is not a mitzvah that comes through a transgression. This can be answered.

This means disqualifications of beauty and deficiency, and this follows the approach of the Ramban at the beginning of the first chapter, that what we say, that beauty is invalid on the second day, means in the Temple, but not outside the Temple, where it is only rabbinic; see there.
