[1] <b>Such as his father, etc.</b> Since there is much to develop on this matter, I must write at length. The Mishnah at the beginning of the chapter 'Hayah Korei' says: in the middle he may ask because of fear and respond because of honor; between the sections he may ask because of honor and return peace to any person. Rashi explained 'because of fear' as a person whom he fears lest he kill him, and 'because of honor' as an honored person to whom it is fitting to give an initial greeting. The Rambam, in the second chapter of Hilchos Krias Shema, explained 'because of fear' as a king, a violent person, and the like, and in his commentary to the Mishnah there he wrote: such as a violent person, an informer to non-Jews, or non-Jews. 'Because of honor' he explained as one whom he is obligated to honor, such as his father, his teacher, or one greater than he in wisdom. Thus there is a dispute between Rashi and the Rambam: according to the Rambam, an honored person, such as one great in years or wealth, is not included in 'because of honor'; according to Rashi he is included in 'because of honor' and is like his father, teacher, or one greater in wisdom. The Beis Yosef did not note this. In practice, the main view is like Rashi, for so it implies in the Semag and Semak, even though they follow the approach of Rashi and the Rambam. Now, the Rosh and Rashba wondered about Rashi and the Rambam, who explained 'because of fear' as lest he kill him, for this is obvious, since nothing stands in the way of saving life. Therefore they explained 'because of fear' as his father or teacher, and so the Shulchan Aruch ruled. The Bach objected and wondered about them: if so, how will they answer the chapter Ein Omdin, where Rabbi Yosef said that this was taught only regarding Jewish kings, but for kings of the nations one interrupts; surely that is obvious. Rather, one must say that there is a rationale that uncertainty does not remove certainty, since the danger from a king or violent person is uncertain, perhaps he will not be particular, whereas here there is a definite prohibition. These are his words. In my humble opinion, even in doubtful danger it is obvious, for we rule everywhere that safek nefashos is treated leniently even regarding desecrating Shabbos. Rabbi Yosef needed to distinguish because the Mishnah explicitly taught that even if a king asks his peace he should not answer; and also to exclude the baraisa from which the Gemara challenges there, if he saw a wagon or violent person coming toward him, he should not interrupt. Therefore Rabbi Yosef needed to distinguish so that we can answer that there it speaks where it is possible to shorten, as it answers there. The Bach further asked: what was the view of that chasid who did not answer the officer when he greeted him? It seems to me that he was stringent upon himself. Similar to this, Tosafot in Eiruvin 21a, s.v. 'mutav,' wrote regarding Rabbi Akiva, who said: better that I die by my own death than eat without netilas yadayim; see there. I found in Sefer Chasidim, siman 490, regarding that chasid: A person who is stringent upon himself in prayer, even though he endangers himself, does well, as Daniel did. End quote. In Menoras HaMaor, ner 3, chapter 18, it is likewise explained that a chasid who was careful with a mitzvah all his life is stringent even in actual danger, and kindness surrounds him. The Bach further wrote that the Semag and Semak wrote like Rashi and the Rambam, so the Rosh and Rashba remain as individuals against the many. This is surprising, for the Rashba and Tur wrote that the geonim explained this way, and the Rashba likewise wrote in Responsa, siman 321, in the name of the Ritz Giat, and so wrote Abudraham, Agur, and great later authorities. Also, in the Rashba's novellae he proved this explanation with complete proofs. Therefore the ruling of the Shulchan Aruch and Levush appears primary. However, what the Shulchan Aruch wrote, that one greater than him in wisdom is included in fear, is indeed what the Beis Yosef wrote in the name of the Rashba; but I have a great wonder, for the Rashba's novellae to Berachos 13a ask against Rashi and the Rambam: We learned that if he was reading Shema and his father, teacher, or one greater than him in wisdom encountered him, between sections he asks because of honor, and in the middle he asks because of fear. This implies that these cases contain honor and contain fear, and among all these, namely his father, teacher, or one greater in wisdom, none involves fear that he might kill him. Rather, certainly 'because of honor' means one greater in wisdom, as we say later regarding Rav bar Shaba, while father and teacher are because of fear, for he is commanded in their fear; regarding a father it is written, 'A man shall fear his mother and his father,' and the fear of one's teacher is like the fear of Heaven. End quote. It is explicit that one greater in wisdom is included in honor. I later found that the Magen Avraham raised this objection against the Beis Yosef. Moreover, if one greater in wisdom is also included in fear, the Rashba's question remains, since it implies that these also have honor. Perhaps the Beis Yosef intended the Rashba's responsum, siman 321, where he answered differently: because his teacher entails two obligations upon him, honor and fear, he used that case, and this is what it teaches: if his teacher or one greater in wisdom encountered him, between sections he asks even because of honor, and even in the middle he asks because of fear. This is correct. Nevertheless, in my humble opinion one may answer this: although he answered differently, that was because he wanted to establish both the beginning and end of the baraisa regarding his teacher himself; but in truth he may hold that one greater in wisdom is included in honor, and we should learn the unspecified from the explicit so that the Rashba's words not contradict. Also, the Beis Yosef should have brought the Rashba's contradictory words and decided between them. Further, it is known that the plain reference to Rashba in the Beis Yosef is to the Rashba's novellae, even where they contradict. Therefore the practical halacha appears primary like his novellae, for the answer in the responsum is very forced. Moreover, the Rosh, Tur, Rabbeinu Yerucham, and Abudraham, who follow the Rashba's approach, all mentioned only father, mother, and teacher as included in fear, and none of them mentioned one greater in wisdom. Also, according to Rashi and the Rambam, father and teacher too are included in honor. Therefore it appears to me as practical halacha that one greater than he in wisdom is included in honor, and likewise one greater in years or wealth; but father, mother, and teacher are included in fear, as is an outstanding Torah scholar, as Terumas HaDeshen wrote in siman 135. On this matter I have great wonder at the Ma'adanei Melech, page 11, who wrote at length that one who explains teacher as included in fear does not have the reading 'one greater than he in wisdom,' for if he read 'in wisdom,' then the whole baraisa would speak only of fear. This is surprising, for in the Rashba's novellae and responsa it is explicit that he reads 'in wisdom,' and he also answers that the baraisa speaks of both categories. Know that there is no distinction between greeting in the holy tongue and in a weekday language. The Magen Avraham wrote that it implies from the Rashba, page 14, that it is permitted initially to greet the reader, even though he knows that the reader will need to answer him.

[2] <b>And he returns peace to every person, etc.</b> I found in Sefer HaChinuch of the Ra'ah, parshas Vaeschanan, as follows: for some time we have not seen anyone be particular at all with his fellow if he does not interrupt for him, even between the sections. End quote. It is written in the glosses of Mordechai in the name of Rabbi Yehudah Chasid: he responds because of honor specifically with 'shalom' and no more; wherever it says he asks or responds, this is with a new face, where if he does not ask he will come to hatred. In a synagogue, where we do not greet, Heaven forbid to respond or ask, even words of Torah, neither between the sections, nor in the blessings, nor in Pesukei D'Zimrah. End quote. It implies that if he is not engaged in the blessings or Pesukei D'Zimrah, they do greet in a synagogue. So too it implies in Tashbetz, siman 246: the Maharam would greet every person before prayer, even in the synagogue.

**** ENGLISH ****
[3] <b>In the middle of the verse, etc.</b> The Kesef Mishneh wrote in the name of Rabbeinu Manoach that when he interrupts in the middle of a verse, this means where the matter has concluded; but in the middle of a matter, such as 'and you shall place these words of Mine upon your heart,' he should not. If he interrupted, he returns to the beginning of the verse. End quote. See above, siman 64, seif 2.

[4] <b>To put them on and bless, etc.</b> I saw in a gloss on the Shulchan Aruch as follows: It seems to me that one should emend and read, 'and he should not bless over them,' for below in this siman, seif 8, he wrote that he should not bless over them. One cannot distinguish and say that here it speaks only between the sections, for the Beis Yosef wrote on the words of Shibbolei HaLeket that it seems to him that one should not bless over them even between the sections. Therefore it seems to me that he should not bless, neither on tzitzis nor on tefillin. End quote. This is like the view of the Levush, who ruled unlike the Rama. At first glance this is surprising, for below, siman 80, the Levush ruled like the Rama. Perhaps he holds a distinction between one who forgot and one who was compelled; so it seems to me. But I wonder that they missed the words of the Bedek HaBayis of the Beis Yosef, who wrote on the words of Shibbolei HaLeket: So too our teacher wrote in siman 80, and wrote that he should bless over them. These are the words of the Rosh in responsa, and one must strike the pen through 'and it seems to me that he should not bless over them,' etc., until 'to prayer.' End quote. It is explicit that the Beis Yosef himself deleted it and ruled to bless, as he wrote in the Shulchan Aruch. Accordingly, the view of the 'some say' that one should not bless is entirely nullified, for we found no one who holds this except the Beis Yosef, and I have written that the Beis Yosef himself deleted it. I have no doubt that had the Rama and Levush seen the Bedek HaBayis, they would not have brought the view of 'some say' at all. Even more, I wonder how one can explain Shibbolei HaLeket as saying not to bless over them between sections, for Shibbolei HaLeket, page 4, wrote that even between redemption and prayer one blesses over tzitzis and tefillin, as Tosafot wrote in the name of Rabbi Yitzchak bar Yehudah in the chapter Hayah Korei; see there. So too the Rokeach wrote, siman 321, and all the more so one blesses between the sections. In truth, Nachalas Tzvi ruled that one blesses even over tzitzis between sections, since according to Ri one blesses even between redemption and prayer, and so ruled Piskei Tosafot. I would have wanted to establish the halacha this way, since so it implies in the Bedek HaBayis I cited. Also, even in Tosafot, Berachos 14a, we did not find that they disagree regarding the blessing on tzitzis except between redemption and prayer; but between sections, or even in the middle of a section, one may say that they agree. However, I found in Responsa Maharam of Rothenburg, siman 73, that even between sections one should not interrupt for the mitzvah of tzitzis. It appears that his intent is the blessings of tzitzis, but as for wrapping, everyone agrees it is permitted. This is also explained in Sefer Zikaron of the gaon Rabbi Y. HaKohen, unlike the Bach in siman 80, who forbids even wrapping between the sections. Therefore practical halacha appears to me: one blesses over tefillin between sections, but not over tzitzis, as the Rama ruled. Afterward the Magen Avraham was printed, and he too brought the words of the Darkei Moshe in siman 54, who wrote in the name of Or Zarua and Rabbi Avraham of Prague that one should not bless over a tallis. If he remembered in the middle of a section, it seems to me that he should finish the section and afterward put on tefillin with a blessing, since it is possible to wait and one should be concerned for Maharam of Coucy, whom Tosafot cited there. So too it implies in Olas Tamid, seif katan 4. It seems to me that there is a textual error in his words in seif katan 17, and it should read: then he may put them on with a blessing; see there and examine it carefully. It implies that this is also the law where he was compelled, unlike Piskei Tosafot, who distinguishes between forgetting and compulsion. Regarding wrapping in tzitzis, he should not wait; rather, it seems to me that he should wrap immediately without a blessing. All this is during Krias Shema and its blessings that precede Krias Shema, but the law regarding the blessing after Krias Shema will be explained in seif 8.
